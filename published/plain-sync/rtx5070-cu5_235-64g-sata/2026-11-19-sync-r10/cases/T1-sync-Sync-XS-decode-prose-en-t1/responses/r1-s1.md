A city’s water supply system is a massive, complex, and often invisible infrastructure that delivers clean, safe, and reliable water from natural sources to the taps of millions of residents. This process involves several critical stages, each with its own engineering challenges and operational requirements. The journey of water begins at its collection point and culminates at the end user’s faucet, but it is far more than a simple flow from a river to a sink. It requires careful planning, technological intervention, and continuous quality monitoring to ensure public health and municipal efficiency. In this explanation, we will trace the path of water through a hypothetical but representative metropolitan system, using concrete examples from major cities like London, New York, or Singapore to illustrate how these principles operate in real-world scenarios.

The first stage is **collection**, also known as sourcing. Water sources can be broadly categorized into surface water—such as rivers, lakes, and reservoirs—and groundwater—such as aquifers. The choice of source depends heavily on geography, climate, and urban density. For instance, London relies heavily on the River Thames and its tributaries, supplemented by reservoirs in the surrounding countryside, while Singapore imports water from Malaysia and Malaysia, and also harvests rainwater, while utilizing local reservoirs. In the United States, the Colorado River supplies water for Los Angeles, while the Hudson River and the Delaware River are primary sources for New York City. Groundwater sources are common in cities located on arid plains or karst landscapes, such as Phoenix or parts of Texas. Collection involves drawing raw water from these sources using intake structures, which may be dams, weirs, wells, or pumping stations. A dam, like the Glen Canyon Dam on the Colorado River, not only blocks the flow to create a reservoir but also creates a controlled point for intake. Intake structures are designed to handle fluctuating water levels, sediment loads, and seasonal variations. For example, during the spring flood season, intake systems may adjust their draw rates to prevent excessive sediment ingress, or during droughts, they may prioritize alternative sources. The raw water collected is not potable; it contains sediments, organic matter, bacteria, dissolved minerals, and potentially pollutants from agricultural runoff, industrial discharge, or urban stormwater.
