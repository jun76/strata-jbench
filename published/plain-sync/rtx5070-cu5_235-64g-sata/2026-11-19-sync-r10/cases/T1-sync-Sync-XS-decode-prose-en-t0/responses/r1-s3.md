A city’s water supply is a complex, multi-stage engineering system that transforms raw natural resources into safe, potable water delivered to millions of households. The journey from source to tap involves collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is critical, and failures at any point can compromise public health or infrastructure reliability. Below is a detailed explanation of how this system works, using concrete examples from real-world cities such as New York City, London, and Singapore.

### 1. Collection: From Natural Sources to Intake Facilities

Water collection begins with identifying reliable sources. Cities typically rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). For example, New York City draws most of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are fed by rainfall and snowmelt. These watersheds are protected by strict land-use regulations to prevent contamination from agriculture, industry, or urban runoff. In contrast, London primarily uses the River Thames and its tributaries, while Singapore relies heavily on reservoirs like Marina Reservoir and imported water from Malaysia.

Intake facilities are where raw water is captured. In New York, the Catskill Aqueduct system includes intake structures at Ashokan Reservoir and Delaware Aqueduct intakes at Cannonsville and Neversink Reservoirs. These intakes use gravity-fed aqueducts to transport water over long distances without pumping, reducing energy costs. In London, the Thames Water intake at Hampton Court uses pumping stations to lift water into treatment facilities. Groundwater collection, as seen in cities like Phoenix or parts of Los Angeles, involves wells drilled into aquifers, where water is extracted via pumps and transported to treatment plants.

### 2. Treatment: Purification and Safety

Raw water contains sediments, organic matter, pathogens, and chemical pollutants. Treatment facilities remove these contaminants through a series of processes. The standard municipal treatment process includes coagulation, sedimentation, filtration, disinfection, and sometimes advanced oxidation or membrane filtration.

Coagulation involves adding chemicals like aluminum sulfate or ferric chloride to raw water. These chemicals bind with suspended particles, forming larger clumps called flocs. In New York’s Catskill treatment plants, coagulation is followed by sedimentation in large basins where flocs settle to the bottom. In London’s treatment plants along the Thames, similar processes occur, but additional steps like ozonation are used to remove taste and odor compounds.

Filtration removes remaining particles.
