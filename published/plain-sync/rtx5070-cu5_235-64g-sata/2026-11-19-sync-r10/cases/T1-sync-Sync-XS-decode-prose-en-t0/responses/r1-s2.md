A city’s water supply is a complex, multi-stage engineering system that transforms raw natural resources into safe, potable water delivered to millions of households. The journey from the source to the tap involves collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is critical, and failures in any one can compromise public health or infrastructure reliability. Below is a detailed explanation of how this system works, using concrete examples from cities like New York, Los Angeles, and London.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Water

Water collection begins at the source, which can be surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). Cities often rely on multiple sources to ensure redundancy and resilience. For example, New York City draws water from three major reservoirs: the Catskill system, the Delaware system, and the Croton system. These reservoirs are fed by rivers and streams in upstate New York, where rainfall and snowmelt replenish the water supply. The water is collected via dams and intake structures, which control flow and prevent contamination from upstream pollution.

In contrast, Los Angeles relies heavily on imported water from the Colorado River via the Los Angeles Aqueduct, as well as local groundwater from the San Fernando Valley. This mix of surface and groundwater sources ensures supply continuity during droughts. Groundwater collection involves drilling wells into aquifers, where water is pumped to the surface. Aquifers act as natural underground storage, but over-pumping can lead to subsidence or saltwater intrusion in coastal areas like Miami.

Collection systems must also account for seasonal variability. In arid regions, snowpack in mountain ranges (like the Sierra Nevada feeding California’s reservoirs) is a critical predictor of annual supply. Climate change has altered snowmelt timing, forcing cities to adapt collection strategies with more flexible reservoir management.

### 2. Treatment: Making Water Safe

Raw water contains sediments, pathogens, chemicals, and organic matter. Treatment removes these contaminants through a series of physical and chemical processes. The primary steps include coagulation, sedimentation, filtration, disinfection, and sometimes advanced treatment for specific pollutants.

Coagulation involves adding chemicals like aluminum sulfate or ferric chloride to raw water. These chemicals bind with suspended particles, forming larger clumps called flocs.
