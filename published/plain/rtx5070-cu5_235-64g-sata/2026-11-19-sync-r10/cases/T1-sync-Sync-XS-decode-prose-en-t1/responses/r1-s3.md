A city’s water supply system is one of the most complex and critical infrastructures in modern society, functioning as the backbone of public health, sanitation, and urban sustainability. To understand how water moves from a natural source to a residential tap, we must trace its journey through several key stages: collection (source and intake), treatment (purification and processing), storage (reservoirs and tanking), distribution (piping and pumping), pressure management (maintaining flow and safety), and quality monitoring (ongoing safety checks and regulatory compliance). Each of these stages involves sophisticated engineering, chemistry, and logistics, working in tandem to ensure that the water delivered to citizens is safe, consistent, and reliable. Let’s examine these processes in detail, using concrete examples to illuminate how modern municipal waterworks function.

### **1. Collection and Source Intake**

The journey begins at the source, where raw water is gathered from a natural body such as a river, lake, reservoir, or groundwater aquifer. The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and local hydrology. For instance, **Los Angeles, USA**, relies heavily on the Los Angeles River and imported water from the Colorado River via the Los Angeles Aqueduct, a 240-mile-long system engineered by William Mulholland in the early 20th century. This aqueduct carries raw, untreated water from the Owens Valley in the Eastern Sierra all the way to Los Angeles, requiring careful intake infrastructure. Meanwhile, **London, UK**, sources a majority of its water from the River Thames and the River Lea, as well as from boreholes (artificial wells) tapping into underground aquifers.

Collection systems include intake structures such as weirs, dams, or pumping stations that capture water from the source. These systems often use screening mechanisms to remove debris, leaves, fish, and other particulates before the water enters the treatment plant. Large cities like **New York City**, USA, draw water from reservoirs such as the Croton and Delaware systems, which are fed by streams and rivers across upstate New York. The intake process involves a series of tunnels and filtration facilities that prevent contamination at the earliest possible stage, crucial for protecting downstream processing.

The raw water from these sources is not yet safe for direct consumption. It often contains sediment, bacteria, algae, and dissolved minerals, requiring extensive purification.
