A city’s water supply is a complex, multi-stage engineering system that transforms raw water from natural sources into safe, potable water delivered to households and businesses. The journey from source to tap involves collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is critical to ensuring that water is safe, reliable, and available on demand. Below is a detailed explanation of how this system works, using concrete examples to illustrate each phase.

### 1. Collection: Gathering Raw Water from Natural Sources

The first step in the water supply chain is **collection**, where raw water is gathered from natural sources such as rivers, lakes, reservoirs, groundwater aquifers, or desalinated seawater. Cities typically rely on multiple sources to ensure redundancy and resilience. For example, New York City draws water from three major reservoirs: the Catskill Aqueduct, the Delaware Aqueduct, and the Croton Aqueduct. These systems transport water over long distances—up to 100 miles—via gravity-fed pipelines and tunnels. The Catskill system alone supplies about 40% of the city’s water.

In contrast, cities like Los Angeles rely heavily on groundwater and imported water from the Colorado River and Sierra Nevada snowmelt. The Metropolitan Water District of Southern California manages a vast network of pipelines, reservoirs, and pumping stations to deliver water across the region. Groundwater collection involves drilling wells into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing rock. In Phoenix, Arizona, groundwater is pumped from deep aquifers using electric pumps, while surface water from the Salt River and Colorado River supplements supply.

Collection systems must account for seasonal variations, droughts, and contamination risks. Reservoirs like Lake Mead (on the Colorado River) serve as critical storage points for cities in the southwestern United States. When water levels drop due to drought, cities must adapt by increasing reliance on groundwater or desalination. In coastal cities like San Diego, desalination plants convert seawater into potable water, supplementing traditional sources.

### 2. Treatment: Purifying Raw Water for Safe Consumption

Once collected, raw water undergoes **treatment** to remove contaminants, pathogens, and impurities. Treatment plants are typically located near collection points or within the city. The process generally includes several stages: coagulation, sedimentation, filtration, disinfection, and sometimes advanced treatment like membrane filtration or activated carbon adsorption.
