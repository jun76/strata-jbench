The journey of water from a natural source to the kitchen tap is one of the most complex, critical, and invisible engineering feats in modern civilization. It is a continuous, multi-stage process that involves hydrology, chemistry, civil engineering, and rigorous quality control. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of a single drop of water through six distinct phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This explanation will use concrete examples, such as the New York City water system and the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (LADWP), to illustrate these principles.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source. Cities typically rely on one of two primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice depends on geography, climate, and population density.

**Surface Water Collection:**
For cities like New York, the primary source is surface water. New York City draws its water from the Delaware River Basin and the Catskill/Delaware reservoir system. These are massive, man-made reservoirs located hundreds of miles north of the city. The water is collected via intake structures—large concrete tunnels or pipes that draw water from the reservoirs. For example, the Catskill Aqueduct is a 92-mile-long tunnel that transports water from the Ashokan Reservoir to the Hillview Reservoir in Yonkers. This gravity-fed system is remarkable because it requires no pumps for the initial transport, relying entirely on the elevation difference between the mountain reservoirs and the city.

**Groundwater Collection:**
In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, or parts of Los Angeles, rely heavily on groundwater. Groundwater is accessed through wells drilled deep into aquifers. A well is essentially a vertical pipe with a pump at the bottom. When the pump is activated, it draws water up from the porous rock layers underground. In Los Angeles, the LADWP operates numerous wells in the San Fernando Valley. However, groundwater extraction must be carefully managed to prevent aquifer depletion or saltwater intrusion in coastal areas.

**Raw Water Characteristics:**
At this stage, the water is "raw." It contains suspended solids (dirt, algae), dissolved minerals (calcium, magnesium), organic matter, and potentially pathogens (bacteria, viruses, parasites). It is not yet safe for consumption.
