The journey of a single drop of water, from its origin deep within the earth or flowing through a river, to the moment it emerges from a kitchen faucet, is one of the most complex and critical engineering feats of modern civilization. While the tap is a simple interface, the system behind it is a sophisticated network involving massive infrastructure, rigorous chemical processes, and constant vigilance. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of water through five primary stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and final delivery, while also addressing the invisible forces of pressure management and quality monitoring that keep the system alive and safe.

### Stage 1: Collection (The Source)

The process begins with the source of raw water, known as the "intake." Cities generally rely on two main types of sources: surface water and groundwater.

**Surface Water Intakes** are typically located in rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. For example, a city like New York relies on the Delaware River Basin and the Catskill Aqueduct. These intakes are not simply pipes submerged in water; they are massive, concrete structures designed to draw water while excluding debris, fish, and sediment. Often, these intakes are protected by trash racks—large screens that prevent large organic matter from entering the pumps. In coastal cities, such as San Diego or parts of the Mediterranean, desalination plants serve as the source. Here, seawater is drawn in and pumped into reverse osmosis facilities. This step is energy-intensive because it requires high-pressure pumps to force seawater through semi-permeable membranes, leaving salts and impurities behind.

**Groundwater Sources** are accessed via wells. In cities like Tucson, Arizona, or parts of Los Angeles, deep boreholes are drilled into aquifers. These wells are lined with concrete to prevent collapse and are equipped with powerful pumps that lift water from hundreds of feet below the surface. Unlike surface water, groundwater is often cleaner upon arrival because it has been filtered naturally through layers of soil and rock, but it may contain high levels of minerals like iron or manganese, which require specific treatment later.

Once the raw water is extracted, it is sent through large-diameter transmission mains (pipes ranging from 30 to 100 inches in diameter) to the water treatment plant.
