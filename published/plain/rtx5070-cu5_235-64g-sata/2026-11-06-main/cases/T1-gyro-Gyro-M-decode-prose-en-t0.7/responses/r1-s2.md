The journey of a city’s water supply is a marvel of modern engineering, transforming raw, often contaminated environmental water into the safe, pressurized liquid that flows from a household tap. This system, known as municipal water infrastructure, operates through a seamless chain of processes: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. To understand this complex network, we must trace a single drop of water from its origin in nature to its arrival at a consumer’s sink, examining the scientific, mechanical, and regulatory mechanisms that ensure reliability and safety.

### 1. Collection: Sourcing the Raw Water

The process begins with the acquisition of raw water, known as "source water." Cities typically rely on two primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The choice of source depends on geography, population size, and environmental conditions.

For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill Aqueduct, a gravity-fed tunnel system that transports water from mountain reservoirs in the Catskill Mountains directly into the city without pumping or treatment for certain portions of its supply, relying on high-quality source protection. In contrast, Los Angeles draws water from distant sources like the Colorado River and the Sierra Nevada mountains, requiring massive pumping infrastructure to overcome elevation changes.

Surface water collection involves intake structures located in reservoirs or rivers. These intakes are designed to exclude large debris and aquatic life. For instance, intake screens prevent fish and algae from entering the processing plant. Groundwater collection involves drilling deep wells into aquifers, where water is pumped to the surface. In cities like Phoenix, Arizona, groundwater wells are critical because surface water sources are scarce and heavily regulated. The raw water collected is often turbid, containing suspended solids, organic matter, bacteria, and dissolved minerals, necessitating the next crucial phase: treatment.
