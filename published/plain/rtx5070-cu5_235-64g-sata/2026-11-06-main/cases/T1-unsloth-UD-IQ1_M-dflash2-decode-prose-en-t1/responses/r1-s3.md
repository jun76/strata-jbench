The journey of water from a distant mountain spring or a sprawling reservoir to the moment it flows from your kitchen faucet is one of modern engineering’s most critical yet often overlooked achievements. It is a complex, continuous process that involves massive infrastructure, precise chemistry, and rigorous legal standards to ensure that every drop is safe to drink. This narrative traces the lifecycle of municipal water supply through its primary stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring, utilizing real-world examples such as New York City’s Croton system and the complex systems serving major metropolises like Los Angeles or London.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The journey begins at the source, which is typically categorized as either surface water (lakes, rivers, or reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source dictates the complexity of the subsequent treatment. For example, New York City primarily relies on surface water from three reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, while Phoenix, Arizona, draws heavily from the Colorado River. In contrast, many European cities, such as those in the Netherlands, rely extensively on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers.

Once the source is identified, **intake structures** are built to draw raw water into the system. In surface water systems, these intakes are designed to minimize the intake of debris and aquatic life. They often feature coarse bar screens to remove large objects like logs and branches. In colder climates, these structures must be designed to withstand ice formation. Groundwater systems, meanwhile, utilize boreholes drilled into the earth, fitted with pumps and screens to filter out sediment as the water is drawn up.

A critical aspect of collection is **watershed protection**. Because treatment can be incredibly expensive, cities invest heavily in protecting the source. The New York City Department of Environmental Protection, for instance, spends millions of dollars annually purchasing land in the Catskill Mountains to prevent development, logging, or pollution near the reservoirs. This "source water protection" strategy is so effective that the water from the Catskills requires minimal treatment compared to cities like Atlanta, which draws from the Chattahoochee River and requires extensive filtering due to agricultural runoff and urban pollution.
