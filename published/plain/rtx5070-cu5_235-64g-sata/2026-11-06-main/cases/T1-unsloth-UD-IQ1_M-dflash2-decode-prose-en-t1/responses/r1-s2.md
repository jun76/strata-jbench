The journey of a single glass of water from a distant reservoir to the kitchen sink is a marvel of modern civil engineering, public health, and complex logistics. While we flip on a tap and expect clear, safe, and pressure-stable water instantly, this seamless experience is the culmination of a multi-stage process that can span hundreds of miles and involve intricate chemical, mechanical, and biological interventions. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace its path through six critical phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

### Phase 1: Collection and Intake

The journey begins at the source, which is typically either a surface water body (rivers, lakes, or reservoirs) or groundwater aquifers. For many major metropolitan areas, surface water is the primary source due to its abundance. For instance, New York City draws much of its supply from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, while Los Angeles imports water via the Colorado River and State Water Project. Groundwater sources, such as the Ogallala Aquifer in the Great Plains or the Floridan Aquifer in the Southeast, provide critical backup or primary supply for smaller cities and rural areas.

At the intake site, large structures known as intake towers or screening facilities are constructed. Their primary function is to draw raw water into the treatment plant while preventing the entry of large debris, fish, and invasive species like zebra mussels. Screens with mesh sizes ranging from a few millimeters to an inch are used. In some advanced systems, like those in Denver’s intake at Lake Powell, massive pumps located hundreds of feet below the surface draw water through large-diameter pipes. This stage is crucial because the quality of the raw water determines the complexity of subsequent treatment. For example, raw water from a glacial lake like Lake Como in Italy might have low turbidity but high mineral content, whereas water from a heavily agricultural river might be high in nitrates and sediment, requiring more intensive processing.
