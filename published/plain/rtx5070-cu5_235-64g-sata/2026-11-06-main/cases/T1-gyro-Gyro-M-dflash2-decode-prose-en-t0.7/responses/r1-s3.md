A city’s water supply is one of the most complex, critical, and invisible systems in modern urban infrastructure. While a tap delivers clear water instantly, the journey from the raw source to the household faucet involves a sophisticated network of engineering, chemistry, and physics. This process is designed to ensure that water is not only accessible but also safe, consistent, and reliable for millions of people. To understand how this works, we must trace the water’s path through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring.

### Stage 1: Collection and Source Water Management

The journey begins with the identification and collection of raw water sources. Cities typically rely on three primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs), groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells), or sometimes desalinated seawater in coastal regions. The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and population density.

For example, New York City relies primarily on three upstate reservoirs—Catskill, Delaware, and Croton systems—collected via massive aqueducts and tunnels. These are surface water sources that require extensive treatment due to potential contaminants from runoff, wildlife, and human activity. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely heavily on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers, supplemented by surface water from the Salt River.

Collection involves more than just pumping water. Source protection is a critical first step. Municipalities often manage land use around reservoirs to prevent pollution. For instance, in many European cities, strict regulations limit farming and industrial activity near water intakes to minimize nitrate and pesticide contamination. Intake structures, such as screened pumps or gravity-fed conduits, draw raw water into the treatment facility. During this stage, the water is "raw," meaning it contains suspended solids, algae, bacteria, viruses, and dissolved minerals. It is not yet suitable for human consumption.
