The journey of a single glass of water from its natural origin to the faucet in your kitchen is a marvel of modern engineering, public health policy, and logistical precision. It is a system that operates invisibly, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, ensuring that safe, clean, and pressurized water is available on demand. To understand this complex network, we must trace the water’s path through its six primary stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage involves specific technologies, challenges, and concrete examples of how cities manage the delicate balance between supply and demand.

### Stage 1: Collection and Source Water

The process begins at the source, where water is extracted from the environment. Municipalities typically rely on one of two primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The choice of source depends heavily on geography, climate, and population density.

For example, the city of New York City relies heavily on surface water, drawing from a vast system of reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located up to 125 miles north of the city. In contrast, the city of Phoenix, Arizona, relies on a mix of surface water from the Colorado River and Salt River Project, as well as groundwater from local wells.

**Surface Water Collection:**
In surface water systems, water is diverted into a treatment plant via large intake pipes. These intakes are often equipped with screens or barriers to prevent large debris, fish, and other organisms from entering the system. For instance, the Chicago Water Department draws its supply from Lake Michigan. To protect the intake, the city maintains a strict "no swimming" zone and uses sonar technology to monitor the water quality at the intake point. However, surface water is vulnerable to contamination from runoff, algae blooms, and industrial spills. A concrete example of the risk is the 2014 West Virginia water crisis in Charleston, where a chemical spill into the Elk River contaminated the city’s surface water supply, leaving tens of thousands without safe water for days. This highlights the critical importance of source protection and monitoring.
