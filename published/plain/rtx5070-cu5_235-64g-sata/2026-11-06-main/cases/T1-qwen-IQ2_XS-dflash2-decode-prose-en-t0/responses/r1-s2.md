The journey of water from a natural source to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical engineering feats in modern society. It is not merely a matter of opening a valve; it is a sophisticated system involving hydrology, chemistry, mechanical engineering, and public health policy. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring. Each stage relies on concrete examples and specific technologies to ensure that the water delivered is safe, reliable, and consistent.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, population size, and environmental regulations. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are protected forests that feed reservoirs like the Ashokan Reservoir. In contrast, many cities in the American Southwest, such as Phoenix, Arizona, rely on the Colorado River, requiring massive intake structures to draw water from a flowing river.

The collection phase involves intake structures. In surface water systems, these are often submerged pipes or pumps located near the shore of a reservoir or river. These intakes must be designed to prevent debris, such as leaves, fish, or sediment, from entering the system. A concrete example is the use of bar screens at intake points, which act as giant filters to catch large solids before the water enters the main pipeline. If the source is groundwater, collection involves wells drilled into the earth. These wells use submersible pumps to lift water from aquifers, which are underground layers of water-bearing permeable rock or unconsolidated materials.

A critical aspect of collection is the protection of the watershed. Cities often invest millions of dollars to preserve land around reservoirs to prevent pollution from agriculture, industry, or urban runoff. For instance, the City of Seattle protects the watershed of its reservoirs by limiting development and managing stormwater runoff, ensuring that the raw water entering the treatment plant is as clean as possible before treatment begins.

### 2. Treatment: Making Water Safe

Once collected, raw water is rarely safe to drink directly. It may contain pathogens, sediment, chemicals, or organic matter.
