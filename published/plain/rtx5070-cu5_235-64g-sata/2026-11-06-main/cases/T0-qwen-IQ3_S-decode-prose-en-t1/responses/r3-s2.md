The journey of a single glass of water from a distant mountain stream to the kitchen faucet is one of the most complex, critical, and often overlooked feats of modern infrastructure. While the tap may turn easily with a twist of the wrist, the system behind that convenience represents a massive, interconnected network of engineering, chemistry, biology, and logistics. This system, known as the municipal water supply, ensures that millions of people have access to safe, clean, and consistent drinking water every day. To understand how this works, we must trace the path of water through its primary stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

### Collection: From Nature to Intake

The process begins at the source. Most major cities rely on surface water sources such as rivers, lakes, or reservoirs, though some utilize groundwater aquifers. The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and population density. For example, Los Angeles relies heavily on imported water from the Colorado River and California State Water Project aqueducts, while cities in the American Midwest often draw from the Mississippi River or local reservoirs like Lake Michigan in Chicago.

Collection is not simply about placing a pipe in a river. It involves sophisticated intake structures designed to prevent debris, aquatic life, and large sediments from entering the system. These intakes are often located at the deepest point of a reservoir to avoid algae blooms or ice. A critical component here is the screening system. These screens, often rotating drums or bar screens, act as the first line of defense, removing leaves, fish, and other organic matter before the water even enters the treatment plant. In coastal areas, seawater desalination plants represent a different type of collection, where ocean water is pulled in and treated to remove salt, but this is energy-intensive and typically used only when traditional sources are scarce.

Once collected, raw water is transported to the treatment facility via large-diameter aqueducts or pipelines. This transport can span hundreds of miles. In Boston, for instance, water travels from the Quabbin Reservoir, a massive man-made lake, through miles of tunnels to the Chestnut Hill Reservoir in Newton, Massachusetts. The sheer volume of water moving through these pipes requires gravity to play a role; many systems are designed so that water flows downhill from high-elevation reservoirs, reducing the need for pumping and saving energy.
