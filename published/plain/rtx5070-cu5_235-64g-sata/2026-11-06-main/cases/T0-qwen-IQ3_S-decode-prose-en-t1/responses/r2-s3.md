The journey of a drop of water from its natural source to the tap in your kitchen sink is one of the most complex, engineered, and vital public health triumphs in modern history. It is a process that transforms raw, often hazardous, environmental water into a sterile, drinkable commodity through a seamless series of physical, chemical, and mechanical stages. To understand this system, we must trace the water through six critical phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This explanation will break down each stage with concrete examples, illustrating how modern municipalities ensure that the water flowing from the tap is safe, reliable, and consistent.

### 1. Collection: Sourcing and Intake

The process begins with the identification and abstraction of raw water sources. Municipalities typically rely on one of two types of sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers tapped by wells). The choice of source dictates the initial infrastructure.

Surface water collection involves intake structures located in large bodies of water. For example, consider the Chicago Water Works, which draws millions of gallons daily from Lake Michigan. Large submerged pipes, known as intake crib structures, extend into the lake. These structures are equipped with screens to prevent debris, such as fish, branches, and trash, from entering the system. The raw water here is called "raw water," and its quality varies significantly depending on weather, seasons, and human activity upstream. If heavy rains occur, runoff from agricultural fields containing pesticides, fertilizers, and sediment can dramatically increase the turbidity and chemical load of the water.

Groundwater collection, conversely, involves drilling deep into geological formations to access aquifers. In cities like Albuquerque, New Mexico, the water supply relies heavily on groundwater from the Santa Fe Group aquifers. Here, water is pumped from deep wells to the surface. Groundwater is generally clearer than surface water because the earth acts as a natural filter, but it may contain higher levels of dissolved minerals, such as iron, manganese, or naturally occurring radioactive elements, requiring specific treatment adjustments.

### 2. Treatment: Removing Contaminants

Once collected, raw water must be treated to remove suspended solids, pathogens, and chemical contaminants. This is the most chemically intensive part of the process. A typical surface water treatment plant follows a standard sequence: coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection.
