The journey of a single drop of water from a distant reservoir to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical engineering feats in modern civilization. It is a silent, continuous miracle that most people take for granted, yet it involves a sophisticated interplay of civil engineering, chemistry, biology, and fluid dynamics. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of water through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring, while paying close attention to the crucial role of pressure management.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source. For many major cities, this is a surface water source, such as a river, lake, or reservoir. For example, New York City draws much of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, while Los Angeles relies heavily on the Colorado River and local groundwater. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely significantly on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers.

The collection phase starts with an intake structure. In a river system, this might be a submerged pipe or a screened intake tower that draws water from the deepest, coldest, and least turbid part of the river to avoid surface debris and algae blooms. In a reservoir, the intake is often a floating platform that can adjust its depth to draw water from the optimal layer, avoiding sediment at the bottom and ice or pollutants at the surface.

Concrete Example: Consider the intake for the Chicago Water Department. Water is drawn from Lake Michigan through massive underwater tunnels. These intakes are designed with coarse screens to prevent large debris, such as logs or fish, from entering the system. The water then travels through large-diameter transmission mains, often miles long, relying on gravity or pumping stations to move it toward the treatment plant. This initial transport is critical because any contamination or sediment introduced here can complicate downstream treatment.
