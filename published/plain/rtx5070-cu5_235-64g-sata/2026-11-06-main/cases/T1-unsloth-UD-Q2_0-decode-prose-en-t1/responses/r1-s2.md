The journey of water from a distant source to the kitchen tap is one of the most complex and critical engineering feats in modern civilization. It is not a simple pipe system; rather, it is a highly regulated, multi-stage industrial process that balances physical law, chemical science, public health mandates, and hydraulic engineering. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through six distinct phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each phase introduces specific challenges and solutions that ensure the water remains safe, palatable, and reliably available.

The process begins with **collection**, also known as abstraction. The source of municipal water varies significantly by geography. In many American cities, such as New York City or Portland, Oregon, the primary sources are surface water reservoirs. These are large artificial lakes created by damming rivers, situated in protected watersheds to minimize contamination. For instance, the Catskill and Delaware reservoirs supply New York City with billions of gallons per day, relying on natural gravity flow to minimize pumping costs. In other regions, such as Phoenix or parts of the Midwest, groundwater is extracted from deep aquifers via wells. Groundwater generally requires less treatment than surface water because the earth acts as a natural filter, but it can still contain dissolved minerals like iron or manganese. Regardless of the source, intake structures are critical. These are screened openings in lakes or rivers designed to keep out debris, large organisms, and sometimes even invasive species. The screens must be maintained carefully; if they become clogged, flow rates drop, and the ecosystem may be disrupted by impingement (where fish are pressed against the screen) or entrainment (where smaller fish are sucked into the system).

Once collected, the water undergoes rigorous **treatment**. This phase is the heart of the system, designed to remove contaminants that are visible, invisible, or chemically hazardous. For surface water, the process usually begins with coagulation and flocculation. Chemicals such as aluminum sulfate or ferric chloride are added to the raw water. These coagulants destabilize the electrical charge of suspended particles, causing microscopic dirt, algae, and organic matter to clump together into larger particles called floc. Next, the water moves to large sedimentation basins, where gravity pulls this floc to the bottom, allowing clearer water to flow over the top.
