The journey of a single drop of water from a distant reservoir to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, engineered marvels of modern civilization. It involves a sophisticated network of civil engineering, chemical science, electrical power systems, and rigorous regulatory oversight. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is critical to ensuring that safe, potable water reaches millions of people reliably every day.

### 1. Collection: The Source

The process begins at the source, which is typically one of two types: surface water or groundwater. Surface water sources include rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. For example, the New York City water supply draws from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, vast forested areas where rain and snowmelt collect in reservoirs like the Ashokan Reservoir. Groundwater sources, common in arid regions like Arizona or Texas, involve drilling deep wells into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing permeable rock or unconsolidated materials.

The collection method depends on the source. In a surface water system, intake structures, often located near the bottom of a reservoir to avoid floating debris and ice, draw raw water into large pipes. These intakes are equipped with coarse screens or bar racks to prevent large objects like logs or fish from entering the system. In groundwater systems, submersible pumps located at the bottom of the well shaft lift the water to the surface. Regardless of the source, the water collected at this stage is known as "raw water." It is not yet safe for consumption. It may contain sediment, algae, bacteria, viruses, dissolved minerals, and pollutants from agricultural runoff or industrial activity. The primary goal of the collection phase is simply to transport large volumes of raw water to the treatment plant efficiently.

### 2. Treatment: Making Water Safe
