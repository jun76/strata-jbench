A modern city’s water supply is often perceived as a simple, mundane utility: you turn on a faucet, water flows, and it disappears down the drain. However, this apparent simplicity masks one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and meticulously engineered public health infrastructures imaginable. The journey from a distant watershed to the kitchen sink is a marvel of civil engineering, environmental science, and logistical precision. This process involves five primary stages: collection and source management, treatment and purification, storage and transmission, distribution and pressure management, and final quality monitoring. Each stage serves a critical function in ensuring that safe, potable water reaches millions of users reliably.

### Collection and Source Management

The journey begins at the source. For most major cities, water is not sourced from a single local stream but from a network of reservoirs, lakes, rivers, and aquifers located in the surrounding watershed. The selection of a source is dictated by hydrology, geography, and population density. Concrete examples illustrate the diversity of sources. In New York City, the water supply relies heavily on gravity-fed reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located hundreds of miles north of the city. These waters are naturally soft, low in contaminants, and protected by strict land-use regulations that prevent development in the watershed areas, reducing the need for extensive chemical treatment. Conversely, cities like Los Angeles draw water from the Colorado River and local groundwater basins, which require complex infrastructure due to the arid environment and high salinity levels.

Collection involves more than just drawing water from a lake. It requires the construction of intake structures—submerged pipes or wells that allow water to be pumped into the system. For surface water sources, such as reservoirs, intake towers are built to ensure that water is drawn from optimal depths, avoiding the surface (which can have algae blooms) or the bottom (which may have sediment or anoxic conditions). In the case of groundwater, the collection process involves drilling deep wells. In cities like Phoenix, Arizona, the city operates thousands of wells that tap into the underlying aquifers. These wells must be carefully managed to prevent over-extraction, which can lead to land subsidence—a phenomenon where the ground physically sinks due to the removal of water from the porous rock structure.
