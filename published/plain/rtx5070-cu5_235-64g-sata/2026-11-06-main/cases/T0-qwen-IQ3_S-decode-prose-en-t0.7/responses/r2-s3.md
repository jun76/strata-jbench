The journey of a drop of water from a distant reservoir or aquifer to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and meticulously engineered logistical feats in modern civilization. While we often take the act of turning a handle for granted, this simple gesture is the culmination of a sophisticated infrastructure system that involves environmental science, chemical engineering, hydraulic physics, and rigorous regulatory oversight. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

### 1. Collection and Source Water Intake

The process begins with the identification and intake of raw water sources. Most cities rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). For example, consider New York City, which draws much of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds via gravity-fed aqueducts, or Los Angeles, which imports water from the Colorado River and California State Water Project. In contrast, cities like Phoenix rely heavily on groundwater pumped from deep wells in the Salt River Basin.

The collection phase involves intake structures. In surface water systems, these are often submerged screens or towers that draw water from the reservoir or river. These screens are crucial because they prevent large debris, such as fish, logs, and algae, from entering the system. A concrete example is the intake at the John H. Glenn Water Treatment Plant in Tucson, Arizona, which draws from the Colorado River via the Central Arizona Project canal. Here, bar racks and fine screens remove suspended solids before the water even enters the treatment facility. For groundwater, the collection point is the wellhead. A pump located deep within the earth draws water up through a casing. The quality of the raw water at this stage dictates the complexity of the subsequent treatment. Surface water is generally more turbid and biologically active, requiring extensive treatment, while groundwater is often clearer but may contain high levels of dissolved minerals like iron, manganese, or arsenic.

### 2. Water Treatment

Once collected, the water must be treated to meet health standards, primarily those set by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in the United States under the Safe Drinking Water Act. Treatment is not a single step but a multi-barrier approach designed to remove physical, chemical, and biological contaminants.
