The journey of a glass of water from a distant reservoir to the kitchen tap is one of the most complex, critical, and often invisible feats of modern civil engineering. It is a process that transforms raw, potentially hazardous natural water into a safe, potable commodity, delivered reliably to millions of people every day. This intricate system, known as municipal water supply, operates through a seamless chain of collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and rigorous quality monitoring. To understand how this works, we must trace the path of water through these distinct stages, examining the engineering principles and concrete examples that make urban life possible.

The process begins at the **source**, where water is collected. Municipalities typically rely on two primary types of sources: surface water and groundwater. Surface water sources include rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. For example, the city of New York relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware aqueduct systems, which transport water from upstate reservoirs over hundreds of miles via gravity. Groundwater sources, on the other hand, are accessed through wells drilled into underground aquifers. Cities like Phoenix, Arizona, depend significantly on groundwater pumped from deep wells, as well as surface water from the Colorado River. The choice of source dictates the subsequent treatment requirements; surface water is generally more susceptible to contamination from runoff, algae, and debris, requiring more intensive treatment than groundwater, which is naturally filtered by layers of soil and rock.

Once collected, the raw water enters the **treatment plant**, a facility designed to remove contaminants and pathogens. This stage is multi-step and chemically complex. The first phase is usually **coagulation and flocculation**. Chemicals called coagulants, such as aluminum sulfate or ferric chloride, are added to the water. These chemicals neutralize the negative charges of suspended particles, allowing them to clump together into larger, heavier particles called flocs. In a concrete example, the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power uses this process to remove turbidity from water sourced from the Colorado River and local reservoirs. Following flocculation, the water moves to **sedimentation** basins. Here, the heavy flocs settle to the bottom due to gravity, forming sludge that is removed. The clearer water on top flows to the next stage.
