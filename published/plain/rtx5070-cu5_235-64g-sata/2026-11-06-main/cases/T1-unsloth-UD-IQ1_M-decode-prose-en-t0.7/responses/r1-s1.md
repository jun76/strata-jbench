The journey of a glass of clean, cold water from the municipal tap to the consumer’s lips is a complex, engineered marvel that spans hundreds of miles and involves multiple stages of physical and chemical processing. It is a system designed not merely to move water, but to preserve its integrity, ensure its safety, and deliver it with consistent pressure against the forces of gravity and friction. To understand this process, we must trace the water’s path from its raw source through a series of increasingly controlled environments, each serving a distinct purpose in transforming a natural resource into a safe, potable commodity.

### Stage 1: Collection and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can vary significantly depending on the region. Sources are generally categorized into two types: surface water (lakes, rivers, and reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers tapped via wells). For instance, the New York City water supply relies heavily on surface water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, while many cities in the American Southwest, such as Phoenix, Arizona, depend on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers.

For surface water, collection begins at an intake structure. In a city like Chicago, drawing water from Lake Michigan, giant intake cribs—large, cage-like structures anchored to the lakebed—are used to draw water while preventing large debris, fish, and other organisms from entering the system. The water is pulled by powerful pumps through large-diameter transmission mains, often miles long, leading to a treatment plant. In contrast, groundwater collection involves drilling wells hundreds of feet deep. Pumps installed at the bottom of the well draw water up to the surface. Groundwater is often easier to treat initially because it has been naturally filtered by layers of soil and rock, but it may contain high levels of minerals like iron, manganese, or hardness that require specific removal processes.

### Stage 2: Coagulation and Flocculation

Once the raw water arrives at the treatment plant, the first step in purifying surface water is coagulation and flocculation. Raw water often contains suspended particles, organic matter, and microorganisms that make it appear cloudy or "turbid." To remove these, chemicals called coagulants, typically aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride, are rapidly mixed into the water. This process neutralizes the negative electrical charges that keep particles suspended, causing them to stick together.
