The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical engineering feats in modern civilization. While we often take the turn of a handle for granted, the infrastructure behind municipal water supply is a vast, interconnected network that relies on physics, chemistry, biology, and rigorous regulatory oversight. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This process ensures that water is not only available but also safe, consistent, and reliable for millions of people.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Supply

The first step in the water supply chain is collection, which begins at the source. Cities typically rely on two primary types of sources: surface water and groundwater. Surface water sources include rivers, lakes, and reservoirs, while groundwater is accessed through wells drilled into aquifers. The choice of source depends heavily on geography, climate, and population density.

For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast, protected forested areas that feed reservoirs like the Ashokan Reservoir. These watersheds are crucial because they act as natural filters; the soil and vegetation absorb pollutants and sediment before the water enters the reservoirs. In contrast, cities in arid regions, such as Phoenix, Arizona, rely on the Colorado River, requiring massive infrastructure to transport water over long distances. Groundwater sources, common in cities like San Antonio, Texas, involve pumping water from deep underground aquifers. This water is often naturally filtered through layers of rock and soil, but it may contain high levels of minerals like calcium and magnesium, which require specific treatment later.

Collection infrastructure includes intake structures, such as screens and pumps, which draw water from the source. These intakes are designed to prevent debris, fish, and other large objects from entering the system. In surface water systems, the water is often transported via large aqueducts or pipelines to treatment plants. The Los Angeles Aqueduct, for instance, transports water from the Owens Valley over 200 miles to the city, demonstrating the scale of collection infrastructure required for large metropolitan areas.
