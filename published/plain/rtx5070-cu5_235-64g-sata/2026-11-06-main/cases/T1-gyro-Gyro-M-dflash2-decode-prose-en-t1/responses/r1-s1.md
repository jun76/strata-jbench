The journey of municipal water, from its raw beginning in nature to the familiar stream flowing from a household tap, is one of the most complex, critical, and sophisticated infrastructure systems in modern civilization. This process, often referred to as the "water cycle" in an urban context, transforms potentially hazardous or chemically complex natural sources into safe, reliable, and palatable drinking water. To understand this system fully, we must trace the water through six distinct but interconnected stages: collection and intake, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. Each stage is designed not only to deliver volume but to ensure safety, consistency, and efficiency.

### Stage 1: Collection and Intake

The first stage begins with the identification and securing of a water source. Cities rely on either surface water (lakes, rivers, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The choice depends on geography, population density, and hydrology. For example, many large metropolitan areas, such as New York City, rely heavily on surface water from distant reservoir systems like the Catskill/Delaware watersheds. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, depend on a mix of surface water from the Salt River and groundwater pumped from aquifers.

Intake structures are engineered to draw water without damaging the ecosystem or introducing contaminants. Surface intakes typically use large concrete or steel structures positioned at varying depths to avoid ice, surface debris, and shallow-layer pollutants. These structures often feature trash racks—grates that prevent large debris like branches or aquatic plants from entering the system. Groundwater intakes are usually wells drilled deep into the earth, lined with casing to prevent collapse and contamination from surface runoff. The goal here is not just to get water, but to get *stable* water. Seasonal changes in surface sources—such as increased turbidity (cloudiness) during spring floods or algae blooms in summer—require intakes to be dynamic, often moving to deeper layers during warmer months when oxygen levels drop near the surface.
