The journey of water from its natural origin to the faucet in your kitchen is a complex engineering feat that relies on precise coordination between infrastructure, chemistry, and fluid dynamics. While most people simply turn a handle and receive clean water, the system behind that tap is a vast network of collection, treatment, storage, and distribution designed to ensure safety, reliability, and pressure stability. This explanation details the entire lifecycle of municipal water supply, using concrete examples from major cities like New York, London, and San Francisco to illustrate how each stage functions.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake
The process begins at the source. Municipal water systems typically draw from surface water (rivers, lakes, or reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source dictates the infrastructure required for collection.

**Surface Water Collection:**
For cities relying on surface water, intake structures are critical. For example, New York City’s water supply comes primarily from the Catskills and Catskill Mountain reservoirs. Water flows via gravity through massive aqueducts—such as the Catskill Aqueduct, a 32-mile long conduit buried underground—to the city’s treatment plants. Intake structures at reservoirs prevent debris from entering the system. These structures often use screens and gates to block large objects like logs or fish. In contrast, smaller rivers might require pumping stations if the city is elevated above the water source.

**Groundwater Collection:**
Cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely heavily on groundwater pumped from deep wells. Here, collection involves drilling boreholes into aquifers. Pumps extract water directly, often at depths of hundreds of feet. Groundwater is generally cleaner than surface water because it is filtered through soil and rock layers, but it may require specific treatment to remove minerals like iron or manganese.

### 2. Treatment: Purification and Safety
Once collected, water must be treated to meet regulatory standards for potable consumption. The treatment process varies based on the source but generally follows a sequence of steps: coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection.

**Coagulation and Flocculation:**
Raw water often contains suspended particles, algae, and organic matter. Chemicals like aluminum sulfate (alum) are added to coagulate these particles, making them clump together into larger masses called "flocs." For instance, in Los Angeles’ treatment facilities, precise dosing of coagulants is managed by automated sensors that adjust chemical levels based on turbidity readings.
