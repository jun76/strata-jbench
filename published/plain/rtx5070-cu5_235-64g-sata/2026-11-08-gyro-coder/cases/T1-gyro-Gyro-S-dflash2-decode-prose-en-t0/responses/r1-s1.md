A city water supply is a complex, engineered system designed to deliver safe, potable water from natural sources to millions of consumers through a network of pipes, pumps, and treatment facilities. The journey from source to tap involves several critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is essential for ensuring that water is not only available but also safe, reliable, and consistent in quality. Below is a detailed explanation of how this system works, using concrete examples to illustrate each phase.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Water

The process begins with the collection of raw water from natural sources. Cities typically rely on one or more of the following sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs), groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells), or desalinated seawater. The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and infrastructure availability.

**Surface Water Example:**
In many large cities, such as New York City, water is sourced from reservoirs in the Catskill Mountains. These reservoirs collect rainwater and runoff from surrounding forests and hills. The water flows into large basins where it is stored before being transported to treatment plants. This method relies on gravity-fed systems, reducing the need for pumping and lowering energy costs.

**Groundwater Example:**
In arid regions like Phoenix, Arizona, groundwater is a primary source. Wells are drilled into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing permeable rock. Pumps extract water from these wells, which is then transported to treatment facilities. Groundwater is often cleaner than surface water because it is naturally filtered through soil and rock layers, but it may contain minerals like calcium or magnesium that require treatment.

**Desalination Example:**
In coastal cities like Tel Aviv, Israel, desalination plants convert seawater into potable water. Reverse osmosis membranes filter out salt and impurities, producing fresh water. This method is energy-intensive but crucial in regions with limited freshwater resources.

### 2. Treatment: Purifying the Water

Raw water collected from sources is not safe for consumption. It may contain pathogens, sediments, chemicals, or organic matter. Treatment processes remove contaminants to meet regulatory standards, such as those set by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) or the World Health Organization (WHO).
