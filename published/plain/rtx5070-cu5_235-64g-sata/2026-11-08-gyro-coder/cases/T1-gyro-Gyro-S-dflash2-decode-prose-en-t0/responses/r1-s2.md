A city water supply is a complex, engineered system designed to deliver safe, potable water from natural sources to millions of consumers through a network of pipes, pumps, and treatment facilities. The journey from a river, lake, or underground aquifer to the kitchen tap involves several critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is essential for ensuring that the water is not only available but also safe for human consumption. Below is a detailed explanation of how this system works, using concrete examples to illustrate the processes.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Water

The process begins with the collection of raw water from natural sources. Cities typically rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and infrastructure.

**Surface Water Collection:**
In many large cities, water is drawn from rivers or reservoirs. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are reservoirs located in upstate New York. These reservoirs collect rainwater and runoff from surrounding mountains. The water flows into intake structures, which are large pipes or tunnels that draw water from the reservoir. In the Catskill system, water travels through aqueducts—large underground tunnels—over hundreds of miles to reach the city. This method minimizes the need for pumping because gravity helps move the water downhill toward the treatment plants.

**Groundwater Collection:**
In areas where surface water is scarce or polluted, cities rely on groundwater. For instance, Phoenix, Arizona, draws much of its water from underground aquifers. Wells are drilled deep into the earth, and pumps extract water from porous rock layers. Groundwater is often cleaner than surface water because it has been naturally filtered through soil and rock, but it may contain minerals like calcium or magnesium, which require treatment.

**Intake Structures:**
Regardless of the source, intake structures are designed to prevent debris, fish, and sediment from entering the system. Screens and filters are installed at the intake points. For example, in Chicago, the Lake Michigan intake includes large screens that prevent aquatic life from being sucked into the pipes. These screens are cleaned regularly to maintain flow efficiency.

### 2. Treatment: Making Water Safe

Raw water is not safe to drink. It may contain pathogens, chemicals, sediment, and organic matter. Treatment plants are designed to remove these contaminants through a series of physical and chemical processes.
