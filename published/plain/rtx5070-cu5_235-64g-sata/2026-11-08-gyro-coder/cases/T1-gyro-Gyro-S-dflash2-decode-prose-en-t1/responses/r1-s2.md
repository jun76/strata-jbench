A city water supply system is one of the most complex, invisible, and essential pieces of infrastructure that supports modern urban life. It functions as a continuous pipeline that transforms raw, potentially unsafe sources of water into clean, potable water delivered to millions of homes, businesses, and institutions. The journey from a natural source to a kitchen tap involves a sophisticated sequence of engineering, chemical processing, hydraulic physics, and rigorous quality control. To understand how this system works, we must trace the path of water through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is critical to ensuring that the water arriving at the end of the pipe is safe, reliable, and consistent.

**1. Collection and Source Selection**
The process begins with the collection of raw water, which is often referred to as "source water." Cities rely on two primary types of sources: surface water (lakes, rivers, and reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The selection of a source depends on geological factors, seasonal availability, and regulatory constraints. For example, a large metropolitan area like New York City relies on a vast watershed system, collecting water from reservoirs in the Catskills and Catskill mountains. These reservoirs are fed by rainfall and snowmelt, which naturally percolate through soil and rock, filtering the water before it reaches the collection point. In contrast, cities like Albuquerque rely heavily on groundwater drawn from deep aquifers via pumping wells.

Surface water collection involves constructing intake structures, often located at the bottom of a reservoir or dam, where pipes draw water into the treatment plant. This raw water is rarely clean; it contains suspended solids, organic matter, bacteria, algae, and sometimes chemical contaminants from runoff. Groundwater, while often less turbid, may contain dissolved minerals like iron, manganese, or radion, and can be contaminated by agricultural runoff or industrial leakage. Thus, the collection stage is not merely about gathering water; it is the first step in identifying the specific contaminants that must be addressed in subsequent treatment.

**2. Treatment: Transforming Raw Water into Potable Water**
Once raw water is collected, it is transported to a water treatment plant. The goal of treatment is to remove harmful pathogens, reduce turbidity (cloudiness), and eliminate chemicals to meet safety standards, such as those set by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in the United States. The treatment process typically follows a sequence of physical, chemical, and biological steps.
