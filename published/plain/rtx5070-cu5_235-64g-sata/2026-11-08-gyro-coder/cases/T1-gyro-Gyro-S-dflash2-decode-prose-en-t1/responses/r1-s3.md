A city’s water supply system is a marvel of modern civil engineering, designed to transform raw, variable natural resources into a reliable, safe, and convenient commodity available at every household tap. The journey from source to sink involves a complex chain of physical infrastructure, chemical treatment, hydraulic management, and continuous quality assurance. This process ensures not only that water flows when a faucet is opened but that it arrives clean, pressurized, and compliant with stringent health standards. To understand this mechanism, we must trace the flow through six critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and monitoring.

### 1. Collection: From Nature to Infrastructure

The journey begins with **source selection**. Cities rely on diverse water sources depending on geography and geology. Surface water sources include rivers, lakes, and reservoirs, while groundwater sources include aquifers tapped via wells. In many urban areas, a blended approach is used to ensure resilience against droughts or seasonal fluctuations.

Consider the example of **New York City**, which draws water primarily from the Catskill and Delaware systems. These are reservoir systems located hundreds of miles away, fed by rainfall and snowmelt in upstate forests. The water is collected through intake structures—underground tunnels or aqueducts—that draw from reservoirs. Similarly, a city like **Phoenix, Arizona**, relies heavily on groundwater pumped from wells and treated surface water imported via pipelines from the Salt River Project, drawing from distant lakes like Lake Pleasant.

The collection process must be selective. Intakes are often positioned below the surface in reservoirs to avoid surface ice or floating debris during winter. In rivers, intake screens prevent fish and debris from entering the system. Environmental monitoring is crucial at this stage; if a river is polluted upstream, the entire supply could be compromised. Thus, watershed protection is an integral part of collection, involving land-use restrictions around reservoirs to minimize runoff of pollutants like pesticides or agricultural waste.
