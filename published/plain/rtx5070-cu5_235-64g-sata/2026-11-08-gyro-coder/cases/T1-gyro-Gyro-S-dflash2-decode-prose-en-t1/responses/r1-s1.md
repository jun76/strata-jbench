A modern urban water supply is a marvel of civil engineering, public health infrastructure, and logistical precision. It is not merely the act of turning on a faucet; it is the culmination of a complex journey that begins miles away from the city center, often in remote forests, mountains, or beneath the earth’s surface. This journey involves collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and rigorous quality monitoring. To understand how a single drop of water arrives at your tap, safe and ready to drink, we must trace its path through six critical stages, examining the technical details and concrete examples that make this system function.

### Stage 1: Collection – The Source and Intake

The journey begins at the water source. Cities typically rely on two primary types of sources: surface water (lakes, rivers, reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells).

**Surface Water Collection:**
In many large metropolitan areas, water is sourced from reservoirs created by dams. For example, New York City relies heavily on reservoirs in the Catskill Mountains, while Los Angeles draws much of its supply from the Colorado River system. The collection process involves intake structures located at the dam or lake shore. These intake structures often include screens to prevent debris and aquatic organisms from entering the system. In the case of a river intake, water might be diverted into a forebay—a holding basin—where heavy sediments settle out before the water is pumped or allowed to flow by gravity toward the treatment plant.

**Groundwater Collection:**
Smaller cities or suburban areas often rely on wells. An aquifer is an underground layer of water-bearing permeable rock. Wells are drilled into these layers, and pumps draw water up to the surface. For instance, the aquifer beneath Houston, Texas, provides a significant portion of the city's supply. Groundwater is often cleaner initially than surface water but may contain dissolved minerals like calcium or iron, which require treatment later.
