A city water supply system is one of the most complex and essential pieces of infrastructure in modern urban life. It functions as a continuous, living network that transforms raw natural resources into a safe, potable commodity delivered directly to homes, businesses, and institutions. The journey from the source to the tap involves six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. To understand how this works, we must trace the path of a single drop of water through a hypothetical urban system, drawing on real-world analogies from cities like Denver, Colorado, or New York, New York.

### Stage 1: Collection and Source Protection
The process begins long before water enters any pipe. It starts in the environment where the raw water is harvested. Cities typically rely on two primary sources: groundwater (aquifers tapped by wells) and surface water (lakes, rivers, and reservoirs).

In many cities, such as Denver, water is collected from high-altitude reservoirs like Lake Dillon. Here, the water is already relatively clean because it originates from snowmelt and rainfall in protected mountain watersheds. However, in urban environments reliant on rivers, such as those drawing from the Hudson River or Lake Erie, collection is more challenging. Surface water sources are exposed to pollution from agricultural runoff, industrial discharge, and urban stormwater. Therefore, collection is not just about "taking" water; it is about "selecting" and "protecting."

Water utilities often construct intake structures—large submerged pipes or pumps—that draw water from the source into a transmission pipeline. In surface water systems, the water usually flows through a **screen chamber** first. These screens, often made of stainless steel or woven mesh, filter out large debris like leaves, fish, sticks, and trash. This pre-treatment is crucial to protect the sensitive equipment downstream. If these solids enter the treatment plant, they can clog filters, damage pumps, and introduce contaminants that are difficult to remove later. Concrete example: In Chicago, which draws water from Lake Michigan, intake tunnels extend deep into the lake, using submerged screens to prevent algae blooms and debris from entering the city’s system.
