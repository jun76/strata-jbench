A city’s water supply is a complex, engineered network that transforms raw environmental resources into safe, potable water delivered to millions of homes, businesses, and public institutions. The journey from the source to the tap involves several critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This process ensures that water is not only clean but also consistently available at the correct pressure and quality standards. Below is a detailed explanation of each stage, with concrete examples to illustrate the mechanics of urban water systems.

### 1. Collection: Gathering Raw Water

The process begins at the source, which is typically one of three primary types: surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs), groundwater (aquifers), or a combination of both. Many cities use multiple sources to ensure resilience against drought or contamination.

For example, **Los Angeles** relies heavily on imported surface water from distant reservoirs such as Hoover Dam and Lake Mead, transported via aqueducts. **Chicago** draws primarily from Lake Michigan, while **Denver** uses a mix of local surface reservoirs and deep aquifers tapped for groundwater.

Surface water collection involves intake structures—large pipes or canals that draw water from a lake or river. These intakes often include screens to prevent debris and aquatic organisms from entering the system. In groundwater systems, wells are drilled deep into underground aquifers. Pumping stations activate pumps to bring groundwater to the surface. A concrete example is the **Central Florida aquifer**, where wells supply drinking water to much of the region. The choice of source affects treatment needs; groundwater is generally clearer but may require mineral adjustment, while surface water often contains organic matter, turbidity, and pathogens requiring more intensive treatment.

### 2. Treatment: Purifying the Water

Raw water from the source is not safe to drink. Treatment facilities, known as water treatment plants, remove contaminants, pathogens, particles, and dissolved minerals. The standard process follows a sequence of steps:

1. **Coagulation and Flocculation**: Chemicals like aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride are added to raw water. These cause fine particles and dissolved solids to clump together into larger masses called "flocs."
