A city water supply is a complex engineering marvel that transforms raw, often unpredictable natural resources into a safe, reliable, and consistent commodity delivered to millions of households. The journey from source to tap involves a multi-stage process of collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and rigorous quality monitoring. To understand this system, we must trace the path of water through the infrastructure of a typical modern city, using concrete examples drawn from real-world municipal systems.

### 1. Collection and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be surface water (lakes, rivers, or reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). Most large cities rely on surface water due to its higher volume and easier accessibility, though many smaller towns depend on groundwater wells. For example, New York City draws its water primarily from the Catskill Mountains watershed, where rain and snowmelt feed into reservoirs like the Ashokan Reservoir. In contrast, Phoenix, Arizona, relies heavily on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers beneath the desert, supplemented by imported surface water from the Colorado River via canals.

The collection point is typically an intake facility. In surface water systems, this is often a dam or a reservoir outlet. The water flows through large pipes or canals to the treatment plant. For instance, the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power uses aqueducts to transport water from distant sources like the Colorado River to local storage tanks. In groundwater systems, pumping wells are drilled into the earth, and pumps draw water up through pipes. These intakes must be designed to handle seasonal variations; during droughts, surface reservoirs may drop to low levels, requiring cities to manage rationing or seek alternative sources.

### 2. Treatment: Purification and Safety

Once collected, the water is rarely safe for consumption. It contains sediment, organic matter, bacteria, viruses, and dissolved minerals. Treatment facilities, known as water treatment plants, are designed to remove contaminants. The process varies depending on the source water quality but generally follows a standard sequence: coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection.

**Coagulation and Flocculation:** Chemicals, typically aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride, are added to the raw water. These chemicals bind with suspended particles and colloids, forming larger clumps called flocs. This step is critical for removing turbidity (cloudiness).
