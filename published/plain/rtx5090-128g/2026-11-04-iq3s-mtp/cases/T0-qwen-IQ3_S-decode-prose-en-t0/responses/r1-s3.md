The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, critical, and often invisible engineering feats of modern civilization. It is a continuous, high-stakes logistical operation that involves massive infrastructure, precise chemical engineering, rigorous biological monitoring, and sophisticated hydraulic management. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This process ensures that water is not only available but also safe, palatable, and delivered with sufficient force to reach every household, regardless of elevation or distance.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be a surface water body like a river, lake, or reservoir, or groundwater extracted from aquifers via wells. The choice of source depends heavily on geography and geology. For example, New York City relies on a vast network of reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are protected forests that naturally filter water before it even enters the treatment plant. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely heavily on the Colorado River, while many Midwestern cities draw from deep aquifers.

The collection phase involves intake structures. For surface water, this might be a submerged pipe extending into a reservoir or river, often equipped with trash racks to prevent large debris like logs or plastic from entering the system. For groundwater, the collection involves pumping from deep wells. A critical aspect of collection is the protection of the watershed. If the source is a river, upstream pollution from agriculture or industry can compromise the water quality before it even reaches the city. Therefore, many cities invest in land acquisition and conservation easements to protect the land surrounding their reservoirs, ensuring that runoff remains clean.

### 2. Treatment: The Science of Purification

Once collected, raw water is rarely safe to drink. It contains suspended solids, organic matter, pathogens (bacteria, viruses, protozoa), and dissolved minerals. The treatment plant is where the transformation from "raw water" to "potable water" occurs. This process is typically divided into several sequential steps, each targeting specific contaminants.
