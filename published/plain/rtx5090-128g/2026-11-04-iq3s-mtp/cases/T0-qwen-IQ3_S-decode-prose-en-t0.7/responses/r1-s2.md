The journey of drinking water from its natural source to the glass in your kitchen is one of the most complex, critical, and often invisible engineering feats in modern civilization. It is a multi-step process that involves hydrology, chemistry, civil engineering, and rigorous public health monitoring. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of a single drop of water through six major stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This system is not merely about moving water; it is about transforming raw, potentially hazardous natural resources into safe, potable liquid that reaches millions of consumers reliably.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which typically falls into two categories: surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, population density, and environmental regulations.

For many large cities, surface water is the primary source. Consider New York City, which relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware aqueduct systems. These systems tap into pristine, mountain-fed reservoirs like the Ashokan and Croton. The water here is relatively clean but still requires treatment. The intake process involves large screens and gates that prevent debris, fish, and sediment from entering the treatment facility. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely on a mix of sources, including the Colorado River, but also heavily on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers. Groundwater collection involves drilling wells that tap into underground layers of permeable rock or soil. The water is then pumped to the surface. A critical aspect of collection is watershed protection. For example, the city of Seattle invests heavily in protecting the forests and lands surrounding its reservoirs to prevent pollution from runoff, logging, or development. This "source water protection" is far cheaper and more effective than trying to remove contaminants later in the treatment process.

Once collected, the raw water enters the treatment plant. The complexity of the treatment process depends on the quality of the source water. A river that has passed through industrial areas will require much more intensive treatment than a protected mountain lake.

### 2. Treatment: Making Water Safe

Water treatment is a sequential chemical and physical process designed to remove particulates, pathogens, and dissolved contaminants. While the specifics vary by facility, the general workflow follows a standard pattern: coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection.
