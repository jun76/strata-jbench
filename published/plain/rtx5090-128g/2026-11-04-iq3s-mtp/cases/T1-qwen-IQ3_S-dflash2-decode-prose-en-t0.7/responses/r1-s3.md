The journey of a glass of water from the moment it enters the municipal system to the moment it flows from a kitchen tap is one of the most complex, critical, and often invisible engineering feats in modern civilization. While the act of turning a faucet is instantaneous, the process behind it involves a sophisticated network of infrastructure spanning hundreds of miles, utilizing principles of physics, chemistry, and biology to ensure that every drop is safe, clean, and available on demand. This intricate system, known as the city water supply, can be broken down into several distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

### Collection: The Source of Life

The first step in the water supply chain is collection, where raw water is gathered from natural sources. Cities typically rely on two primary types of sources: surface water and groundwater. Surface water sources include rivers, lakes, and reservoirs, while groundwater is extracted from underground aquifers through wells.

Take, for example, the city of New York. Its water supply is primarily derived from three major upstate reservoir systems—the Catskill, Delaware, and Croton systems. These reservoirs collect rainwater and snowmelt from vast, protected watersheds. The watershed management is crucial here; because New York’s water is so pristine, it has historically avoided filtration by investing billions in protecting the land around the reservoirs to prevent runoff pollution. In contrast, a city like Los Angeles relies heavily on imported surface water from the Colorado River and California State Water Project, as well as local groundwater pumped from deep wells in the San Fernando Valley. The choice of source dictates the subsequent treatment needs. Surface water is more susceptible to contamination from algae, sediment, and industrial runoff, whereas groundwater is generally cleaner but may contain high levels of minerals like calcium and magnesium, making it "hard."

Once collected, the raw water travels via large intake pipes or canals to a treatment plant. These intakes are often equipped with screens to prevent large debris, such as fish, leaves, and branches, from entering the system. This initial physical barrier is the first line of defense in the water’s journey.
