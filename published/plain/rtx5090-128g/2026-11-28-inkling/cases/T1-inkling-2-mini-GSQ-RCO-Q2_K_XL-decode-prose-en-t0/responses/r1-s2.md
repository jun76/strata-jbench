I can explain the general engineering and public-health logic of a city water supply system, but I can’t provide a step-by-step operational guide for bypassing or compromising a specific municipal system. The details below are educational and focus on how these systems are designed and monitored.

A city water supply begins with a source. In many cities, the source is a river, reservoir, lake, or groundwater aquifer. The source must be large enough to meet demand during normal weather and dry periods, and it must be protected from pollution. For example, a city near a river may build a dam or reservoir upstream to store water and smooth out seasonal flow. A city in a dry region may rely on wells drawing from an aquifer, but then it must manage recharge, pumping limits, and contamination risk.

Collection is the first physical step. If the source is surface water, intake structures draw water from the river or reservoir. These structures may include screens or grates to keep out large debris, and sometimes a settling basin to reduce sediment. If the source is groundwater, wells or pumps extract water from underground. The intake must be located away from wastewater outlets, industrial discharge points, and areas with high sediment or contamination risk.

Once water is collected, it is transported to a treatment plant. This transport can be by gravity, pumps, or a combination of both. In a hilly city, gravity flow may move water from a reservoir downhill to a treatment facility. In a flat city, pumps may be needed to move water from a source to the plant. The treatment plant is where the water is made safe for drinking.

Treatment usually follows a sequence: screening, coagulation, sedimentation or filtration, disinfection, and sometimes additional polishing. In a surface-water plant, coagulants such as aluminum sulfate or ferric salts are added to help tiny particles clump together. These particles then settle in a basin or are removed by filters. Sand, gravel, or membrane filters can remove remaining solids. Disinfection is then used to kill or inactivate microorganisms. Common methods include chlorine, chloramine, ultraviolet light, or ozonation. Chlorine is widely used because it also provides residual protection in the distribution system. Ultraviolet treatment is effective for microorganisms but does not leave a long-lasting residual, so it is often paired with another disinfectant.
