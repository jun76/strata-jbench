I can explain the general engineering and public-health logic of municipal water supply, but I can’t provide a step-by-step operational guide for bypassing or compromising a specific city’s system. The details below are educational and focus on how systems are designed and monitored.

A city water supply begins with a source. In many places, that source is a river, reservoir, lake, or groundwater aquifer. The source is selected for reliability, quality, and capacity. For example, a city near a river may draw water from a reservoir upstream, while a city in a dry region may rely on wells tapping a deep aquifer. The source must be protected because pollution, sediment, or contamination can make treatment harder or more expensive.

Collection is the first physical step. At a surface-water intake, water is drawn through a screen or intake structure. Screens and coarse filters remove large debris such as leaves, sticks, and floating material. Intake design also tries to avoid drawing in sediment-heavy water from the bottom of a river or reservoir. For groundwater, collection happens through wells. Wells are drilled or bored into an aquifer, and pumps draw water upward. The city may use multiple sources to reduce risk: if one reservoir is low or one well is contaminated, another source can supply the system.

Once collected, water enters treatment. Treatment is not one single process; it is a sequence of steps matched to the source. For surface water, common steps include screening, coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation or clarification, filtration, and disinfection. Coagulation uses chemicals such as aluminum sulfate or ferric salts to help tiny particles clump together. Flocculation gently mixes the water so those particles form larger “flocs.” Sedimentation or clarification lets the flocs settle or be removed in a settling basin. Filtration then passes water through sand, gravel, or other media to remove remaining particles. Disinfection—often with chlorine, chlorine dioxide, or ultraviolet light—kills or inactivates pathogens. Some systems also add fluoride or other treatment chemicals for public-health reasons, depending on local policy.
