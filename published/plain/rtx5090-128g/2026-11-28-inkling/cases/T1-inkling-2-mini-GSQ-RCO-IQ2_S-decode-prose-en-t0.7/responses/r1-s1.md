The city water supply system works as a chain of controlled stages. A source is selected, water is collected, treated, stored, distributed, monitored, and delivered to the tap.

Collection begins at the source. A city can use surface water, groundwater, or a combination of both. Surface water may come from a river, reservoir, lake, or canal. Groundwater comes from wells in aquifers. Concrete example: a city near a river may use a dam or reservoir to capture river water, while a desert city may rely on deep wells drilled into a groundwater aquifer.

The collection point must be protected. If a city uses a river, the intake may be placed upstream of heavy pollution or downstream of a clean source, depending on the river’s condition. If a city uses groundwater, the well must be located away from waste, industrial sites, or poorly managed landfills. Example: a well near a polluted landfill may be unsuitable, while a well in a stable aquifer far from contamination can provide reliable supply.

Treatment is needed because raw water is not always safe for drinking. Treatment may include screening, filtration, settling, chemical treatment, disinfection, and sometimes coagulation or sedimentation. Example: river water may be passed through a screen to remove leaves and debris, then through sand filters to remove particles, then treated with chlorine or ultraviolet light to kill bacteria and viruses. Groundwater may require less treatment, but it may still need disinfection, chlorination, or filtration if it contains contaminants.

Storage is used to smooth supply. Water is not used at the same rate every hour, so reservoirs or tanks hold a reserve. Example: a reservoir may supply water overnight when demand is lower, while a tank stores water for morning and evening use. Storage also helps during outages, emergencies, or maintenance.

Distribution moves water from storage to homes and businesses. Pipes, pumps, valves, and pressure zones carry water through the city. Example: a city may use a main pipe from a reservoir to a distribution network, then smaller pipes to neighborhoods, buildings, and schools.

Pressure management is essential. Too much pressure can damage pipes and fixtures. Too little pressure can cause weak flow. Example: a city may use pressure regulators to reduce high pressure in a tall building, while pumps raise pressure in a low area.
