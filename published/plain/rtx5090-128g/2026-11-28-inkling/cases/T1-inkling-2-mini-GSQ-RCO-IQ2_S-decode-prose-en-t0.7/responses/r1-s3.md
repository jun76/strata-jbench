The city water supply system is a carefully engineered chain that moves water from its source to the tap. It is not a single pipe but a sequence of stages, each designed to protect people and infrastructure.

Collection begins at a source such as a river, lake, reservoir, groundwater aquifer, or municipal treatment intake. In cities with surface water, engineers build intake structures that draw water from a river or reservoir. For example, a river-fed city may use a gated intake to collect water while avoiding sediment, debris, and flood surges. In a groundwater-fed city, wells or boreholes extract water from underground aquifers. The source must be selected for reliability and quality. A reservoir may provide stable storage, while an aquifer may be less vulnerable to surface contamination but can be affected by overuse, pollution, or land subsidence.

Once collected, water is moved to a treatment facility. Treatment is the core stage of the system. The process depends on the source. Surface water often requires coagulation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection. In a concrete example, a city may add a coagulant such as aluminum sulfate or ferric chloride to make fine particles stick together. Those particles form larger flocs that settle in a sedimentation basin. The water is then passed through sand or gravel filters to remove remaining particles. Finally, it is disinfected, commonly with chlorine, chloramine, or ultraviolet light. Some systems also add fluoride or other treatment chemicals for specific public-health purposes.

Groundwater treatment is often simpler because the water may already be relatively clean, but it still needs testing and, where needed, removal of contaminants. For example, if groundwater contains iron, manganese, hardness, or arsenic, the plant may use oxidation, filtration, softening, reverse osmosis, or other targeted processes. If the source is polluted, treatment may include activated carbon, membrane filtration, or chemical precipitation.

Storage is the next major stage. After treatment, water is stored in reservoirs, tanks, or elevated tanks. This storage serves several purposes. It provides a buffer against demand fluctuations, protects the city during emergencies, and ensures that the treatment plant can run at a steady rate. In a city with a large reservoir, water may be stored in a dam or artificial lake before entering the distribution system. In a smaller city, an elevated storage tank may hold a few days of supply.
