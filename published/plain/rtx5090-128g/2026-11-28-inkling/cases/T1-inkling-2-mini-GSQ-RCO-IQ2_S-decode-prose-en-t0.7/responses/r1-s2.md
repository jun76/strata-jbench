A city water supply is best understood as a chain of engineered systems rather than a single pipe. Water moves from a natural source to a reservoir or treatment plant, then through distribution networks, meters, and homes or businesses. Each stage has a different job, and each stage has a different failure mode.

Collection begins at the source. In a river-fed system, a intake structure draws water from the river or a nearby reservoir. In a groundwater system, wells or springs are used. In a lake-fed system, water is drawn from a larger reservoir with a controlled intake. The source is not simply “where water comes from”; it is also the point where the city must manage sediment, temperature, flow, and contamination risk.

A concrete example is a city that draws from a river with seasonal flooding. During dry periods, the intake may need to be adjusted to avoid drawing too much sediment or too little flow. During storm periods, the city may need to control intake to avoid pulling in large amounts of turbid water. In a well-fed system, the challenge is different: the city must monitor groundwater levels, flow rate, and whether the aquifer is being depleted.

Collection also includes screening and pretreatment. Before water reaches the main treatment plant, it may pass through screens, gravel filters, or a sediment basin. This reduces the amount of large material entering the treatment process. In some systems, the water is stored in a raw-water reservoir before treatment. This is useful because it allows the treatment plant to process water at a steady rate even when the source flow varies.

Treatment is the central part of the supply. The exact process depends on the source. River water often requires more treatment than clean groundwater. Groundwater may need less treatment but still requires disinfection and monitoring. Surface water may need coagulation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection.

In a river-fed example, the first stage is coagulation. A chemical is added to the water to help small particles and colloids form larger, heavier particles. These particles then settle in a sedimentation basin. The water is then filtered through sand, gravel, or other media to remove remaining particles. Finally, it is disinfected, often with chlorine, UV light, or another method.
