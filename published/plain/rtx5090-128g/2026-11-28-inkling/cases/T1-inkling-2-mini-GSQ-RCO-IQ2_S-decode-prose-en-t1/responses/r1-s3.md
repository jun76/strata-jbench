A city water supply works as a chain of interconnected stages: source selection, collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. The goal is to move water from a safe source into neighborhoods while keeping it clean, pressure steady, and reliable during normal use and peak demand. A simple example is a mid-sized city served by a river or reservoir: water is drawn near the source, filtered and chlorinated, stored in tanks and reservoirs, pushed through pipes and pumps, and checked continuously for contaminants.

Collection starts at the water source. Cities can use surface water such as a river, lake, or reservoir, or groundwater from wells. Each has different strengths and weaknesses. River water is abundant but can carry turbidity, sediment, algae, and seasonal changes in flow. Groundwater is often more stable and less variable, but it can contain minerals, iron, manganese, or bacteria if poorly managed. A concrete example is a city using a reservoir in a dry season: the reservoir captures runoff from upstream catchments, but the intake structure must avoid drawing in floating debris, dead fish, or contaminated shallow layers. For groundwater, a city might use a well field in a confined aquifer; the wells draw from a deep layer where the water is naturally filtered by soil and rock. The intake design determines what enters the system. If the intake is too close to a contaminated edge, or the pumps draw too aggressively, the treatment plant can become burdened or the raw water can be worse than expected.

After collection, raw water enters treatment. Typical treatment stages include screening, sedimentation or coagulation, filtration, disinfection, and sometimes chemical adjustment. In a river-fed system, raw water may first pass through screens to remove large solids such as sticks, leaves, or plastic fragments. Then the water is mixed with a coagulant such as aluminum sulfate or a polymer; these particles bind suspended sediment into larger flocs. The water is allowed to settle or passed through clarifiers so that the heavier flocs drop out. Then the remaining particles pass through sand, gravel, or activated media filters. This stage removes fine sediment and many biological particles. Disinfection follows, often by chlorine, chloramine, or ultraviolet light, depending on the system and local requirements.
