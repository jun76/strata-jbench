I can explain the general engineering and operations of municipal water supply, but I won’t provide a detailed, step-by-step attack plan, a map of exposed infrastructure, or procedures for compromising a water system.

A city water supply works as a chain of engineered stages. The source might be a river, reservoir, groundwater aquifer, or treated runoff. The city first collects or extracts the water, then treats it, stores it, pumps it through the distribution network, and finally delivers it to homes and businesses. Each stage has different risks and controls.

**1. Collection and intake**
At the source, the water must be captured without bringing in too much sediment, debris, or biological contamination. For surface water, a city may use an intake structure with screens and filters. For groundwater, it may use wells or pumps. In a river-fed system, the intake may be near a dam or reservoir, with a controlled draw of water rather than a full dam release. In a city like Los Angeles or a many smaller metro systems, intake can be from reservoirs rather than open rivers, because reservoirs give the city more control over flow and quality.

The first step is not simply “taking water from a source.” It is a governed intake decision: how much flow, what screens, what seasonal changes, and what downstream environmental effects. Concrete example: a reservoir-fed city might draw a steady flow to prevent the intake from becoming overloaded with sediment during dry or wet seasons. In a well system, the city checks groundwater depth, flow rate, and seasonal changes in aquifer pressure.

**2. Treatment**
Raw water is not safe for drinking without treatment. Treatment depends on the source.

For surface water, common treatment steps include:
- screening and removal of large debris,
- coagulation and flocculation, where chemicals help particles form larger clumps,
- sedimentation or filtration,
- sometimes chemical oxidation or disinfection,
- and, in many municipal systems, additional polishing or monitoring.

Concrete example: after a storm, a river may contain high turbidity. The treatment plant might increase chemical dosing, then use sedimentation and filtration to reduce suspended solids. If the source is polluted by industrial runoff or animal waste, the city may need more advanced treatment, such as activated carbon, oxidation, or additional disinfection.
