I can explain the general operation of a municipal water supply system. It is a large infrastructure network that moves water from natural sources to homes and businesses through collection, treatment, storage, and distribution. Here is an overview of each stage.

Collection. Water is collected from surface sources such as rivers, lakes, reservoirs, or shallow aquifers, and from groundwater through wells. A treatment plant usually begins with intake structures that screen out large debris and then send the water to settling basins. In a river intake, for example, screens and pumps draw water from the flow; in a well, a pump lifts groundwater from underground.

Treatment. Treatment removes particles, microorganisms, and dissolved contaminants. A common sequence is coagulation, where chemicals clump fine particles into larger flocs; sedimentation, where the flocs settle out; filtration, where the water passes through sand or other media; and disinfection, often using chlorine, chloramines, or ultraviolet light. Some systems also add fluoride, adjust pH, or remove iron and manganese. A concrete example is a surface-water plant in a temperate climate: raw river water is mixed with coagulant, allowed to settle, filtered through sand, then chlorinated before entering storage.

Storage. Treated water is stored in reservoirs or tanks to meet daily demand and handle peak use. Storage can be elevated, where gravity helps move water downhill, or underground, where pressure systems and pumps may be used. A concrete example is a downtown district fed from a mountain reservoir above the city: gravity gives stable pressure without relying entirely on large pumps.

Distribution. After storage, water moves through a network of pipes: large mains, smaller feeders, and service lines to individual buildings. The pipes are usually laid along streets and connected to buildings through private connections. The system is designed so that water can reach different parts of a city with relatively uniform pressure. In a dense city, water may travel through underground mains to neighborhoods, then branch into local service lines that run to apartments, offices, schools, and homes.

Too little pressure reduces flow and can affect firefighting or fixture performance; too much pressure can stress pipes and cause leaks or damage.
