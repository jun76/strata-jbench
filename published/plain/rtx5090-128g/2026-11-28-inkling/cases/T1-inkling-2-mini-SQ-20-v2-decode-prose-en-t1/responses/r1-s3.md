A city water supply begins at a source—usually a river, reservoir, groundwater well, or a combination of these. In a river-fed system, intake structures sit on the bank or in a calm section of the water body. These structures may include screens or grates to remove large debris like leaves, branches, and floating objects. In a reservoir-fed system, water is stored behind a dam or weir, which smooths out seasonal flow changes. In groundwater systems, wells are drilled into aquifers; these wells may be shallow, with limited depth, or deep, depending on the geology and the demand placed on the resource. The source is chosen for reliability, water quality, and capacity. A city may use more than one source so that if a river becomes temporarily turbid or a well produces less water, the supply can be maintained.

From the source, water is conveyed to a treatment plant. The conveyance system may use open channels, buried pipes, pumping stations, or gravity flow. Gravity is often preferred because it uses less energy, but steep terrain or low source elevation may require pumps. In a concrete example, a city near a river may draw water from an intake structure and move it through a buried pipe to a treatment plant a few miles away. Another city with high elevations may pump water from a distant reservoir to a storage reservoir nearer the population.

Treatment is the central part of the process. It removes or reduces particles, microorganisms, dissolved contaminants, and materials that could affect taste, odor, clarity, or health. Treatment usually starts with pre-treatment steps. For example, large particles and sand may be removed by screening, grit chambers, or sedimentation basins. In many surface-water plants, coagulation and flocculation are used: small amounts of chemicals are added so fine particles clump together into larger “flocs.” These flocs can then be removed by sedimentation or filtration.
