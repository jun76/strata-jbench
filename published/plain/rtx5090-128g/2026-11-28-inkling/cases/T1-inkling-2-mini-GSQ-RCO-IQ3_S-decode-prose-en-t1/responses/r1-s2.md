The water that reaches a tap in a city often starts far from that city, in a river, reservoir, lake, spring, or even a nearby aquifer. In an urban setting, the journey is a chain of engineered systems designed to move water safely, reliably, and at a consistent quality. The process can be broken into collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

Collection begins at the source. In many cities, surface water is drawn from rivers or reservoirs. For example, a city beside a large river may install intake structures along the bank to draw water from a depth where sediment and pollutants are lower. In a reservoir city, the source is an artificial or natural body of water built or maintained to provide a steady supply. In another case, a city may rely on groundwater, pumping from wells drilled into an aquifer. Municipal utilities often monitor multiple sources because weather, seasons, and contamination risks can change. A practical example is a dry-season city where the river drops and reservoir levels fall, forcing the utility to draw more from wells or buy water from a neighboring system.

Once collected, the water usually passes through pretreatment. This may include screening to remove large debris, settling or screening to reduce solids, and aeration to improve taste or remove certain compounds. The purpose is to make the water easier to treat. In a river-based system, for example, the intake may have fine bars or screens so fish, leaves, and debris do not enter the treatment plant. In a reservoir-fed system, pretreatment may include coagulation to bring suspended particles together into larger flocs. In groundwater systems, treatment can be simpler because the water is already filtered by soil and rock, though it may still need disinfection and correction of mineral imbalances.

Treatment is the heart of the process. The exact sequence depends on the source and the pollutants present. A common surface-water sequence includes coagulation and flocculation, where chemicals are added to cause tiny particles to cluster into larger masses. Then the water moves to a sedimentation basin, where solids sink to the bottom. This is followed by filtration, usually through sand or activated carbon beds, which remove fine particles and remaining contaminants. Next comes disinfection, often by chlorine, chloramine, ultraviolet light, or ozone. Chlorine is widely used because it leaves residual protection in the pipes, helping prevent recontamination
