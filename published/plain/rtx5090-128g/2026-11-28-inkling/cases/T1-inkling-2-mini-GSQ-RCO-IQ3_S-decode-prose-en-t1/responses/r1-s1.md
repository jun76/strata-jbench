The overview below is about how water systems work and how they are monitored, not how to disrupt, sabotage, or gain unauthorized access to them.

At a high level, a city water supply moves water from a source to buildings through a sequence of physical and administrative controls. The chain is usually: source intake → raw-water conveyance → treatment → storage → pumping/pressure management → distribution mains → customer service connection → in-home plumbing → tap. Each stage has distinct engineering goals: protect water quality, maintain reliable pressure, prevent contamination, and allow operators to detect failures quickly.

Collection begins at a source such as a reservoir, river, lake, aquifer, spring, or treated wastewater. For surface water, a dam or reservoir creates a controlled pool. For groundwater, a well or spring taps an aquifer. In a city like Los Angeles, water may come from the Los Angeles Aqueduct system, from local reservoirs, or from groundwater basins. In a city like London, historic supplies once relied on reservoirs and pumped wells, while modern systems combine surface-water catchments, deep wells, and regional transfer schemes. In many cities, source protection is as important as treatment: land-use rules, buffer zones, and monitoring near intake points reduce runoff and contamination risk.

Raw water is usually drawn through a protected intake structure. The structure is screened or filtered so large debris does not enter the system. Pumps move water to a treatment plant, or it flows by gravity if elevation permits. In a gravity-fed system, the source or a hillside reservoir sits higher than the city, so water moves downhill. In pumped systems, large pumps maintain flow through pressure pipes and reservoirs.

Treatment depends on the source and local standards. A common process for surface water includes screening or coarse filtration, sedimentation or clarification, coagulation or flocculation, filtration, and disinfection. In a clarifier, suspended particles settle out or are removed by a combination of chemicals and flow conditions.
