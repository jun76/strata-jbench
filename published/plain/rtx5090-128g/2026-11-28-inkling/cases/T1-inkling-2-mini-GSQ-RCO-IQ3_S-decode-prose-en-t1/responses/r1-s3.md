I can explain the process in general terms, but I can’t provide a step-by-step operating manual, exact setpoints, or a configuration that could be adapted to sabotage or misuse the system. Here is a detailed explanation of how a municipal water supply works from the source to the tap.

A city water supply starts at the source. That source may be a river, lake, reservoir, spring, or groundwater well. In cities that use surface water, engineers first identify the intake structure. An intake is a protected opening that draws water from a river or lake. It may be built with screens or bars to keep out large debris, and it may be located in a place where the water is reasonably stable. For example, a city near a large river might draw water from a bend in the river where sediment does not accumulate too quickly. In a desert city, the source may instead be deep groundwater drawn from wells.

Once the water leaves the source, it often enters an intake or pumping station. The water is pumped to the treatment plant or to an intermediate reservoir. This step raises the water to a level or pressure that allows it to move through treatment facilities. In a river-fed system, the pump may lift water from a river channel into a collection basin. In a well system, the pump may draw water from underground and send it to a storage tank or treatment facility.

The treatment process removes or reduces contaminants. A typical plant does not simply filter water; it uses several stages. The first step is often screening and sediment removal. Large trash, branches, leaves, and plastic are kept out, while finer suspended solids are separated. In a surface-water plant, this may involve grit chambers or settling basins. In a groundwater plant, treatment may focus more on removing iron, manganese, bacteria, or chemicals that are less visible to the eye.

After sediment removal, the water may pass through coagulation and flocculation. Coagulants are added to help tiny particles clump together. Flocculation then allows those clumps to grow into larger, easier-to-remove solids. For example, a plant treating turbid river water may add a coagulant and then mix the water gently so small particles gather into larger flocs.
