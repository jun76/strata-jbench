A city water supply system is a coordinated chain of infrastructure that moves water from its source to homes and businesses. It begins with collection, where water is taken from a river, lake, reservoir, spring, or groundwater well. Cities choose sources based on reliability and quality. For example, a city near a large reservoir may draw surface water, while a city in a dry region may rely on wells. The source must be protected because upstream pollution can affect the entire system.

Next comes intake and screening. Water entering the treatment plant is passed through screens or coarse filters to remove leaves, sticks, debris, and large particles. In a river intake, this is critical because floating material can clog pumps and damage equipment. In a well, the intake is simpler but still requires protection from sediment and contamination.

Treatment then removes harmful substances and improves safety. Surface water often undergoes several steps: coagulation, where chemicals cause tiny particles to clump together; sedimentation, where solids settle out; filtration, where water passes through sand or other media; and disinfection, where chlorine, ultraviolet light, or ozone kills bacteria and viruses. Groundwater may need less treatment, but it can still require filtration or treatment for iron, manganese, nitrates, or other contaminants. A concrete example is a city treating river water by adding aluminum sulfate to form flocs, letting the water settle, filtering it through sand, and then adding chlorine before it enters the distribution network.

Storage follows treatment. Water is held in reservoirs, tanks, or elevated towers to buffer demand and maintain supply during peak use. A reservoir can hold millions of gallons and provide a backup if treatment capacity is reduced. A distribution reservoir in a city helps even out fluctuations between morning showers and evening household use. Water is often kept under pressure so it can flow to homes without pumping every drop continuously.

Distribution is the final movement through pipes. Water travels from storage into main lines, then through branch pipes to neighborhoods. The system is divided into zones to control pressure. Cities often use pressure-reducing valves, pump stations, and separate zones for high-rise buildings and low-lying streets. A concrete example is a hillside neighborhood where a valve lowers pressure before water reaches a street, preventing burst pipes and excessive flow from faucets.

Pressure management is essential. Too much pressure can damage pipes, leak joints, and cause noise
