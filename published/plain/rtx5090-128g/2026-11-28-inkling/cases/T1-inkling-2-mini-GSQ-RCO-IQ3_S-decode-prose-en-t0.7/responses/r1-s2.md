City water supply begins at the source. In many cities the source is a river, reservoir, lake, or groundwater well. The intake structure is designed to collect enough water without drawing in too much sediment or debris. For surface water, engineers may place intake pipes below the surface so that the most settled water is drawn in. For groundwater, wells or springs provide a more direct source, but the water may still contain dissolved minerals, iron, manganese, or contaminants that require treatment.

A concrete example is a city drawing water from a river. The intake might be a horizontal pipe several feet below the water surface. Screens and fine filters prevent fish, leaves, and large debris from entering. The water is then pumped into the treatment plant. If the source is a reservoir, the intake may be located near the deeper part of the reservoir, where the water is less turbid and less affected by seasonal changes in temperature and sediment.

Once the water reaches the plant, it is commonly tested and screened before treatment. Raw water may contain sand, algae, organic matter, bacteria, and dissolved chemicals. The first stage often involves mixing the water with coagulants. Coagulants are chemicals that cause tiny particles to clump together. For example, aluminum sulfate, sometimes called alum, can be added to make small clay particles and fine organic material form larger clusters.

After coagulation, the water enters a flocculation stage. Here, gentle mixing causes the small clusters to grow into larger, heavier flocs. This step is important because it turns suspended particles into something that can be removed more easily. Without flocculation, the particles might remain too small to settle or be filtered.

The next stage is sedimentation or clarification. In a clarifier basin, the water slows down and the flocs settle to the bottom. A concrete example is a large circular or rectangular tank where water flows slowly. Heavy particles drop out, while clearer water is collected near the top. The settled material at the bottom is removed as sludge and may be treated separately or sent to a disposal facility.

Filtration follows. The clarified water passes through layers of sand, gravel, or other filter media. The filter traps smaller particles that did not settle.
