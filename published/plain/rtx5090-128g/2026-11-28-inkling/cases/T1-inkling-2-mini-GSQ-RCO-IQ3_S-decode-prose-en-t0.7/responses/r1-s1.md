A city water supply system moves water through a chain of stages: source, collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure control, and monitoring. The goal is to turn water that may be naturally scarce, variable, or contaminated into a dependable supply that is safe to drink and reliable for homes, businesses, and public buildings. The exact process depends on where the city gets water—surface reservoirs, rivers, lakes, groundwater wells, or a mix of these—but the basic logic is the same.

Collection begins at the source. In many cities, drinking water is drawn from a river or reservoir. For example, a city near a large river may build a intake structure that draws water from a controlled depth or location along the bank. The intake may include screens or bars to keep out large debris such as leaves, branches, and floating objects. In a lake-fed system, the intake may be deeper to avoid surface algae or windblown sediment. In groundwater-fed systems, collection happens through wells drilled into aquifers. A city may also combine sources: surface water for high-demand periods and groundwater for steady backup.

Before treatment, raw water is often screened and sometimes mixed with chemicals to begin the process. The first major step is usually coagulation and flocculation. Here, coagulants—commonly aluminum sulfate or ferric salts—are added to neutralize particles and cause tiny dirt, algae, and organic matter to clump together into larger flocs. Flocculation follows, where the water is gently stirred so the flocs grow. This makes the particles easier to remove later.

Sedimentation then allows the heavier flocs to settle. In a sedimentation basin, water moves slowly so that the clumped particles sink to the bottom. The cleaner water on top is collected and sent onward. The settled material is removed as sludge, which may be dried, treated, or disposed of according to environmental rules.

Next comes filtration. Even after sedimentation, water still contains small particles that are too fine to settle on their own. Sand filters, sometimes backed by activated carbon or other media, are used to trap these remaining particles. The filter bed acts like a sponge: as water passes through, particles are trapped in the pores of the material. Periodically, filters are backwashed—water is pushed backward through the bed to clean it and remove accumulated debris
