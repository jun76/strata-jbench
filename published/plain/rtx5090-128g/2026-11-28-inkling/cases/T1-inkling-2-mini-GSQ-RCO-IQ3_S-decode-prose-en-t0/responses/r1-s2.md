A city water supply system is a coordinated chain of infrastructure that moves water from a natural source to the taps in homes, offices, schools, and businesses. The process can be divided into collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage has a specific purpose, and each stage depends on the reliability of the previous one.

Collection begins at the source. In many cities, the source is a river, lake, reservoir, or groundwater well. A river or lake intake draws water from a controlled location, often with screens or coarse filters to keep out large debris such as leaves, branches, and fish. A reservoir intake may draw from a deeper part of the water body to reduce exposure to surface contamination. Groundwater wells draw water from aquifers, which are underground layers of permeable rock or sediment that store and transmit water. For example, a city near a river may use a river intake with a screened intake structure, while a city in a dry region may rely on deep wells drilled into a confined aquifer.

Once water is collected, it enters the treatment plant. The first step is usually screening and coarse filtration. This removes large particles and protects downstream equipment. Next, the water may be mixed with chemicals such as coagulants, often aluminum sulfate or ferric chloride. These chemicals cause tiny particles and colloids to clump together into larger flocs. The water then moves into a sedimentation basin, where the flocs settle out. This is a simple but effective way to remove suspended solids.

After sedimentation, the water passes through filtration. Common filtration media include sand, gravel, and sometimes activated carbon. Sand filters remove fine particles that remain after sedimentation. Activated carbon can remove organic compounds, odors, and some taste-causing substances. In some systems, membrane filtration is used instead of or in addition to sand filtration. For example, a city with a highly turbid river source may use rapid sand filtration followed by disinfection, while a city with a more contaminated source may use membrane filtration to remove bacteria, viruses, and dissolved contaminants.

Disinfection is a critical step. Water is treated with chlorine, chloramine, ultraviolet light, or ozone to kill or inactivate pathogens.
