A city water supply system is a coordinated chain of infrastructure that moves water from a natural source to homes, businesses, and public buildings. The process begins with collection, where water is drawn from a river, lake, reservoir, groundwater well, or a combination of these sources. In many cities, surface water is collected through intakes that draw water from a river or reservoir. These intakes are designed to avoid taking in sediment, debris, or contaminated water. For example, a city near a large river may use a screened intake structure that allows water to enter while keeping fish, leaves, and large debris out. Groundwater systems use wells drilled into aquifers, which are underground layers of permeable rock or soil that store and transmit water. A well may be shallow or deep depending on the geology and the amount of water needed.

Once water is collected, it enters treatment. The exact treatment process depends on the quality of the source water. Surface water often contains turbidity, organic matter, bacteria, and other contaminants, so it may be treated with coagulation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection. In coagulation, chemicals such as alum or ferric chloride are added to cause small particles to clump together. These particles then settle in a sedimentation basin, leaving clearer water above. The water is then passed through filters, often sand or gravel filters, which remove remaining particles. Finally, disinfectants such as chlorine, chloramine, or ultraviolet light are used to kill harmful microorganisms. Groundwater may require less treatment, but it can still need filtration, softening, iron removal, or disinfection. For example, a city with iron-rich groundwater may use aeration or oxidation to remove iron before the water is distributed.

After treatment, water is stored in reservoirs, tanks, or elevated storage facilities. Storage is essential because demand varies throughout the day. A city may use a large underground reservoir, a surface reservoir, or a series of elevated tanks. These storage systems provide a buffer between treatment and distribution. They also help maintain pressure and ensure that water is available during peak demand periods, such as morning and evening. For example, a city with a large residential population may see higher water use in the morning when people wake up and in the evening when they return home. Storage allows the system to meet this demand without interrupting service.
