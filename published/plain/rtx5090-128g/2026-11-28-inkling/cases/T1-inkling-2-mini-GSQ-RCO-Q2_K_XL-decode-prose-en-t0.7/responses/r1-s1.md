The city water supply system is a chain of engineered steps that turns raw water into something safe to drink. It begins at the source, whether that is a river, lake, reservoir, groundwater aquifer, or a mixture of several of these. The source provides the raw material, but it is not automatically safe or reliable, so the system must move that water through collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and monitoring before it reaches the tap.

Collection starts with the intake. A river or reservoir intake may be a screened structure or a submerged intake pipe that draws water from a controlled location. Groundwater systems use wells or boreholes, often with pumps and filters. In many cities, intake is designed to avoid sediment-heavy areas, dead zones, or places where pollution is concentrated. For example, a city near a river may draw from a deeper or faster-moving part of the channel, while a city relying on aquifers may place wells in a zone where recharge is steady and contamination is less likely. The intake must also be protected so that waste, stormwater, or industrial runoff does not enter the raw water stream.

Once water is collected, it often passes through preliminary treatment. This may include screening, settling, or coagulation. Sediment and larger particles are removed so that downstream equipment is not overloaded. In surface water systems, this step can remove debris and reduce the load on filtration. In groundwater systems, the source may already be relatively clean, but still needs treatment for nitrates, bacteria, metals, or other contaminants. Concrete example: a city using reservoir water may first add coagulants that make fine particles cluster together, then allow the clusters to settle into a sludge layer. A city using well water may instead add disinfection and filtration because its raw source is more stable but still vulnerable to microbial contamination.

Treatment is the core of the process. Different cities use different combinations of methods, but the goal is the same: remove harmful contaminants and make the water safe. Common treatment steps include coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, disinfection, and sometimes chemical adjustment. Coagulation helps tiny particles combine into larger flocs. Sedimentation lets those flocs sink. Filtration passes water through sand, gravel, or other media to remove remaining solids.
