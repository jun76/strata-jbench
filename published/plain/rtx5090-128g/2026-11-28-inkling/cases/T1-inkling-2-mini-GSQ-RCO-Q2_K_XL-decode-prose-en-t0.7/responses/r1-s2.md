City water supply begins at the source. For many cities, that source is a river or reservoir. In a river-fed system, intake structures withdraw water from the channel—often near a bend or a controlled intake point—and pass it through screens or coarse filters so that debris, leaves, and large sediment are excluded before the water enters the treatment plant. A reservoir system works differently: water is stored behind a dam or in an artificial lake, then released through controlled outlets. The amount released is adjusted based on demand, seasonal flow, and the need to maintain a minimum reservoir level. In either case, the first objective is not purification but reliable capture of water that can be treated safely.

Once water enters the treatment plant, it is usually exposed to a process designed to remove particles, organic matter, microbes, and undesirable chemicals. A common sequence is coagulation and flocculation, where chemicals such as aluminum sulfate or iron salts are added. These chemicals neutralize charges on fine particles and help them clump into larger flocs. Sedimentation then allows the heavier flocs to settle, either in basins or tanks. After settling, the water passes through filters—often sand beds, sometimes with additional media such as activated carbon—to remove remaining suspended solids and some dissolved contaminants.

Disinfection is the critical step that makes the water safe to drink. Chlorine is widely used because it is effective and relatively easy to monitor. Other disinfectants include chloramines, ultraviolet light, and sometimes ozone or other advanced methods depending on the plant. Chlorine also leaves a residual that helps protect water as it travels through the distribution network. The concentration must be controlled: too little leaves pathogens uncontrolled, while too much can create taste or odor problems and by-products. Some utilities also monitor for disinfection by-products and adjust treatment accordingly.

Examples: In a city drawing from a river, intake may occur at a weir or pump station. In a reservoir-fed city, release may be regulated by gates or pipes connected to a treatment building. In a municipal system, the plant may be split into several stages so that seasonal changes in water quality can be handled without shutting the entire operation down.
