A city water supply begins with source selection and collection. Municipal systems draw from surface reservoirs, rivers, groundwater wells, or sometimes a combination of sources. In a mid-sized city, a reservoir may be the main source, with wells used for supplemental supply during dry seasons. Collection involves intake structures that capture water while minimizing sediment, debris, and biological contamination. A well-designed intake uses screens and grates to keep fish, leaves, and debris out, while allowing enough flow for the plant. If the city depends on groundwater, pumping wells are drilled and monitored for depth, aquifer quality, and seasonal changes.

Once water enters the system, it moves to treatment. Treatment is not a single step but a sequence of processes. For surface water, intake water is first screened and then pumped to a treatment plant. Filtration removes suspended solids and fine particles. Sedimentation and coagulation help remove turbidity and some organic material. Chlorination or another disinfectant is added to kill bacteria, viruses, and other pathogens. In some systems, activated carbon or other adsorptive media may be used to improve taste and odor or remove specific chemicals. In groundwater systems, treatment may be simpler, but the water still needs testing for minerals, nitrates, and microbiological contamination.

Treatment is followed by storage. A city does not rely solely on the treatment plant; it stores water in reservoirs, elevated tanks, or underground tanks. Storage helps stabilize supply and provide pressure during peak demand. A reservoir may hold several million gallons, while a smaller elevated tank may supply local pressure to a neighborhood. Storage also allows the system to respond to fires, outages, and maintenance. If a treatment plant shuts down for a day, stored water keeps the city functioning until the plant returns.

Distribution is the network of pipes, valves, pumps, and pressure zones. Water moves from storage through mains, branch lines, and service connections. Mains are large-diameter pipes that carry water across the city; laterals and service lines reach individual homes and businesses. Pressure management is critical.
