Collection begins at the source. In a surface-water system, a city draws from a river or reservoir through intakes on the bank; in an inland aquifer city, pumps draw from wells. Concrete example: a mid-sized city draws from a reservoir fed by a river, using a gated intake that can be closed during floods or drought. In a groundwater city, wells are drilled into a confined aquifer and pumped continuously, with the pump rate set to avoid over-extraction.

Treatment follows the source. For surface water, the usual stages are screening and grit removal, coagulation and flocculation (adding alum or similar chemicals to clump particles), sedimentation, filtration through sand or other media, and disinfection. Concrete example: a municipal plant adds alum, mixes it with water, lets the floc settle, then passes the water through a sand bed, and finishes with chlorine or another disinfectant. For groundwater, treatment may be simpler—often just disinfection and, if needed, iron or manganese removal—because the source is already filtered through soil.

Storage follows treatment. Finished water is held in elevated tanks or ground storage reservoirs. Concrete example: a city builds a concrete reservoir near the treatment plant and a smaller elevated tank on a hill; the elevated tank provides gravity-fed pressure to upper-floor homes. Storage buffers demand swings, like a summer heatwave, and gives firefighting capacity.

Distribution moves water through pipes from the plant to homes. Pipes are sized so that pressure falls gradually from the source to the edge of the network. Concrete example: a city has a main trunk line at high pressure, then smaller branches, then service lines to each home. Pressure is managed by pressure zones—separate pressure ranges created by pumping stations or by tank height—so that older, low-pressure areas do not overload newer areas.
