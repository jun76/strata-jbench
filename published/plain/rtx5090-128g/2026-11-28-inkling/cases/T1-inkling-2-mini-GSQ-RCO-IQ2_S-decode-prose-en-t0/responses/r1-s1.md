A city water supply is best understood as a chain of engineered systems: source → collection → treatment → storage → distribution → pressure control → monitoring → tap. Each stage has a different purpose, and each stage can fail in a different way.

## 1. Source: where the water comes from

A city may get water from a river, reservoir, lake, groundwater aquifer, or a combination of these.

Concrete example: a mid-sized city might use a nearby reservoir fed by a river. The reservoir is not the “source” in the sense of a single well; it is a managed storage and intake system. Another city might rely on deep wells in a saturated aquifer. In that case, the source is underground, and the city must monitor groundwater levels, contamination, and pumping rates.

The source determines the raw water’s characteristics. River water may contain sediment, algae, organic matter, and seasonal changes. Groundwater may be clearer but can contain dissolved minerals, iron, manganese, or naturally occurring contaminants. Reservoir water may be more stable but can still have algal blooms or seasonal temperature changes.

The first engineering question is: what is the raw water like, and what must be removed or adjusted before it is safe for drinking?

## 2. Collection and intake

At the source, water is collected through an intake structure. For a river or reservoir, this may be a dam, intake gallery, or screened opening. For groundwater, it may be a well or pumping station.

The intake must prevent large debris from entering the system. Screens or grates can remove leaves, branches, and other visible material. In some systems, the intake also includes a settling or pre-treatment area to reduce sediment before the main treatment plant.

Concrete example: a city intake from a reservoir might use a screened intake pipe. The water enters a pumping station, where it is drawn into the treatment plant. If the reservoir has high sediment load, the intake may include a pre-settling basin or a coarse filter to reduce the amount of solids entering the main process.
