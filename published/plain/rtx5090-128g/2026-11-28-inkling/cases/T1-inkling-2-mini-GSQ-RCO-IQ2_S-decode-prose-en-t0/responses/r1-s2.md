A city water supply system is a coordinated chain of infrastructure that moves water from a natural source to the taps in homes, businesses, and public buildings. The process begins with collection, where water is drawn from a river, lake, reservoir, groundwater well, or treated surface water. In a city like Denver, for example, water may come from mountain streams and reservoirs; in a city like Houston, it may rely on a mix of surface water and groundwater. The source is chosen based on availability, quality, climate, and the city’s population.

Once collected, the water enters a treatment plant. Treatment is needed because raw water can contain sediment, bacteria, viruses, organic matter, dissolved minerals, and other contaminants. The first stage is usually screening or intake filtration. Large debris is removed, and the water is directed into a treatment process. In many municipal systems, the water is then mixed with chemicals such as chlorine, ozone, or other disinfectants to kill harmful microorganisms. Sediment and particles are removed through settling or filtration. In some plants, activated carbon or membranes are used to remove taste, odor, or specific contaminants.

After treatment, the water is stored in reservoirs, tanks, or elevated storage facilities. Storage is essential because demand varies throughout the day. A city may use more water in the morning and evening, so the system must hold enough supply to meet peak demand. For example, a suburban city might store water in a large elevated tank near a neighborhood, while a dense urban area may use underground reservoirs or multiple distribution tanks. Storage also helps maintain pressure and provides backup if a treatment plant is temporarily unavailable.

Distribution is the network of pipes, valves, pumps, and meters that carries water from storage to buildings. The system is usually divided into zones, with pressure maintained in each area. Pipes are sized according to expected flow and distance. In a city like Chicago, a large network of trunk lines and smaller service lines delivers water to residential and commercial areas. In a smaller city, the network may be simpler but still follows the same principle: water moves from high-pressure storage toward lower-pressure neighborhoods.
