A city water supply system is best understood as a chain of engineered stages: source, collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure control, and monitoring. Each stage has a different purpose, and each can fail in a way that affects safety, reliability, or cost.

1. Source and collection

Water can come from surface sources such as rivers, lakes, reservoirs, or wetlands, or from groundwater such as aquifers. Surface water is often easier to collect but may require more treatment because it can contain sediment, algae, organic matter, and pathogens. Groundwater is often naturally cleaner but may contain minerals, iron, manganese, or contaminants that require treatment.

In a city like Denver, Colorado, water is supplied partly from mountain streams and reservoirs. In a city like Los Angeles, water comes from a mix of imported surface water, groundwater, and treated wastewater. In a city like Atlanta, Georgia, supply comes from rivers and reservoirs, with treatment plants that remove sediment, organic material, and pathogens.

Collection begins with intake structures. For a river, an intake may be a screened opening that draws water into a pipeline. For a lake or reservoir, the intake may be a submerged pipe or a structure near the shore. For groundwater, the “collection” is a well or pumped aquifer.

The intake must be designed to avoid drawing in too much sediment, debris, or floating material. In rivers, intake screens may be used to keep fish, leaves, and debris out. In reservoirs, intakes may be placed at depths where water is less likely to contain surface algae or sediment. In groundwater systems, wells are screened to allow water to enter while limiting sand and silt.
