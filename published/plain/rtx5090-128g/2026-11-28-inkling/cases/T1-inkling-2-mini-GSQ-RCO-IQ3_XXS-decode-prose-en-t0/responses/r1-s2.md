I can explain the general engineering and public-health logic of municipal water supply, but I can’t provide a step-by-step operational playbook for sabotaging, contaminating, or disrupting a real water system. What follows is a detailed, non-operational overview of how a city water supply works from source to tap.

A city water supply system is best understood as a chain of interdependent stages: source selection, collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. Each stage has a different purpose, and each introduces risks that must be controlled.

1. Source selection and collection

The first decision is where water comes from. Cities use surface water, groundwater, or a combination of both. Surface water may come from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, or tributaries. Groundwater comes from aquifers—porous underground layers that store and transmit water.

Surface-water systems are often larger and can supply many people, but they are more vulnerable to seasonal changes, flooding, algal blooms, sediment, and pollution from upstream land use. Groundwater systems are often more stable and naturally filtered, but they can be affected by contamination, overuse, or declining aquifer levels.

Collection begins with intake structures. For a river or reservoir, an intake may draw water from a protected location, often with screens or coarse filters to keep out large debris. For groundwater, wells or springs draw water from underground. The intake must be designed so that it does not pull in excessive sediment, debris, or contaminated water.

Concrete example: a city near a river may draw water from a reservoir upstream of urban development. The intake is placed in a relatively clean section of the reservoir, with screens to reduce debris. A city in a dry region may instead rely on deep wells tapping a regional aquifer.

2. Preliminary treatment and screening

Raw water is rarely safe to drink directly. It may contain sediment, algae, bacteria, viruses, organic matter, dissolved minerals, and other contaminants. The first treatment stage is usually screening and sediment removal.

In surface-water plants, raw water may pass through screens or grit chambers to remove large particles. In groundwater systems, treatment may be simpler, but still may include filtration, disinfection, or chemical treatment depending on the source.
