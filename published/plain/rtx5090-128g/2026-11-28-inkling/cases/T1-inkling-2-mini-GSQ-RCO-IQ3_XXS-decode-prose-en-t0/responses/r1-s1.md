I can describe how the system works and how it is monitored, using concrete examples.

A city water supply is best understood as a chain of controlled transfers: water is collected, made safe, stored, pushed through pipes, and continuously checked so that what reaches the tap is reliable and potable. The exact design varies by city, but the sequence is consistent.

1. Source collection

Water may come from a surface reservoir, a river, a lake, a groundwater well, or a combination of sources. In a city like Denver, Colorado, for example, municipal supply has historically relied heavily on surface reservoirs and mountain-fed streams, while other cities rely on deep aquifers. In a coastal city, groundwater may be the main source; in a drought-prone city, reservoirs and conservation rules become more important.

At the source, the first task is to capture water without letting it become unusable before treatment. This means controlling intake structures, sediment, debris, and seasonal changes. A reservoir intake may draw water from a depth where it is less affected by surface scum or warm shallow layers. A well intake may be screened to allow water in while keeping out sand and large particles.

The source is also monitored. Engineers track flow, temperature, turbidity, and seasonal changes. If a river floods, the intake may need to be adjusted or the treatment plant may need to handle higher sediment loads. If a well drops in level, the city may need to reduce pumping or switch sources.

2. Pre-treatment and screening

Before full treatment, water often passes through screens or coarse filters. This removes leaves, sticks, trash, and large debris. In surface-water systems, this step is critical because raw water can contain algae, sediment, and organic matter. In groundwater systems, the raw water is often cleaner, but it may still contain iron, manganese, hardness, or naturally occurring contaminants.
