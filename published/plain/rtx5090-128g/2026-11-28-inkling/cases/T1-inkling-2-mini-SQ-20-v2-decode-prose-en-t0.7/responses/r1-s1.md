I can explain the general flow of a municipal water supply, but I can’t provide a step-by-step blueprint for bypassing treatment, tampering with monitoring, or gaining unauthorized access to a distribution system. With that boundary in mind, here is a detailed explanation of how a city water supply works from source to tap.

A city water supply begins with a source. In many places that source is a surface water body—a river, reservoir, lake, or wetland—while in other areas it is groundwater from a well. Surface water can be abundant and relatively easy to capture, but it also carries more variable loads: sediment, algae, leaves, microbes, and seasonal changes in chemistry. Groundwater is often naturally filtered by soil and rock, but it can contain iron, manganese, hardness, dissolved minerals, or contaminants depending on the geology. A city may use one source or several. For example, a large metropolitan region might draw from a reservoir and also supplement with wells during drought, while a smaller town may rely entirely on a local aquifer.

The first stage is collection. A surface-water intake may be a structure on a riverbank or reservoir shoreline with a screen or grate that prevents large debris from entering. The intake draws water into a channel or pipeline. Groundwater is collected through wells, which are drilled or dug into a permeable aquifer. A well has a casing to hold the borehole open, a screen or perforated section to allow water to enter, and a pump or pumping system to lift water to the surface. The source is chosen for reliability, quality, and capacity, and the city may maintain backup sources to handle drought, contamination events, or emergency demand.

Once water is collected, it is transported to a treatment plant. This is the first major engineering step. For surface water, treatment usually begins with screening or coarse filtration to remove large particles. Then the water may be mixed with chemicals such as coagulants and flocculants. These chemicals help fine particles and microbes clump together into larger, easier-to-remove aggregates. The water then moves into a sedimentation or clarification basin, where the heavier particles settle out. Some systems also use rapid mixing and flocculation tanks before settling.
