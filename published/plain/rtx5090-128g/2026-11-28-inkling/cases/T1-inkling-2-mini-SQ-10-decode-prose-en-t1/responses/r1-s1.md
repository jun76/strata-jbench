I can explain the general architecture of a municipal water system, but I can't give a single city's exact source locations, treatment-plant layouts, or real-time pressure and sampling values — those are public-sector details that vary by jurisdiction and aren't something I can pull up live. So the examples will be representative and illustrative rather than an official record for one specific city.

Here's a detailed walk-through.

**1. Collection**
Water starts at a source: a river or lake intake, a groundwater well, or a reservoir. In a river city, the intake structure is built to draw water without trapping fish, debris, or silt. A city might draw from a large regulated reservoir that stores seasonal runoff, or from municipal wells in a shallow aquifer. Collection also includes a screening step — screens, weirs, or intake galleries keep large objects out. The flow is metered, since that is the baseline for later billing and compliance.

**2. Preliminary treatment**
Once water enters the plant, it is first measured and screened. It is commonly dosed with coagulants or oxidizing agents such as ferric chloride or lime to help particles bind together. For groundwater, the plant may use simple filtration rather than full flocculation. The plant records intake volume and chemistry at each stage — temperature, turbidity, chlorides, sulfates, and sometimes nitrate — so operators can see how the raw source is fluctuating.

**3. Clarification and filtration**
The clarified water moves through settling basins or lagoons. Fine particles settle out, and the water becomes clearer. Then it passes through filters — sand or gravel beds, sometimes with anthracite or activated carbon in finer polishing stages. The filtered water is stored in a cistern or clearwell, which is often the largest storage vessel in the system. This is the key buffer between raw source and distribution.

**4. Disinfection**
Before entering the network, water is disinfected. Most municipal plants use chlorine or chloramines; some use ultraviolet light or ozonation. Chlorine is common because it is cheap and effective. The plant monitors residual chlorine at the distribution line and at representative taps. Disinfection is not simply a chemical dose — it is also a controlled process with calibrated injection points and continuous readouts.
