City water supply moves from a source to a tap through several linked stages. A city first needs reliable water—usually from a river, reservoir, lake, aquifer, or treated reclaimed water—then removes or controls the contaminants that make it unsafe, stores it for demand variability, and delivers it through pipes, pumps, valves, and storage networks. The system is designed not only to deliver volume but also to maintain pressure, avoid contamination, and support public health and resilience.

### 1. Source collection

The source determines the baseline challenge. A river or reservoir may carry sediment, microorganisms, dissolved organic matter, and seasonal variability. A groundwater well may have lower biological load but can contain minerals, nitrates, metals, or naturally occurring contaminants. Reclaimed water has its own risks and may require advanced treatment or disinfection before reuse.

Concrete examples:

- **River source:** A municipal plant may take water from a river or reservoir through an intake structure. The intake has a screen or grate to prevent fish, debris, and large floating material from entering. In a high-turbidity season, inflow may include more suspended solids, so the plant adjusts coagulation and filtration settings.
- **Reservoir source:** A city near a reservoir might collect from a deeper intake to reduce seasonal algae or warm-water impacts. The reservoir provides storage capacity, but still needs treatment for pathogens and organic matter.
- **Groundwater:** A deep well pumping from a regional aquifer may avoid surface-water turbidity but must manage iron, manganese, hardness, ammonia, nitrate, or microbial contamination. If well water has high turbidity after pumping, a plant may still add filtration.
- **Reclaimed or recycled water:** Some cities use treated wastewater for irrigation, industrial use, or limited potable supply after additional disinfection and monitoring.

Collection is also tied to drought planning. Cities track seasonal flow, reservoir levels, well drawdown, and demand forecasts so they can stage treatment and pumping changes before shortages appear.
