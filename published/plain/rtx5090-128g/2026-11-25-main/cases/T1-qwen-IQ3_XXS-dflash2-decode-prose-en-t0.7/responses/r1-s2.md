The journey of a glass of water from a distant reservoir to a kitchen sink is a marvel of modern engineering, public health science, and logistical coordination. While turning the handle of a faucet is a trivial act for most individuals, the infrastructure behind it represents one of the most complex and critical systems in urban civilization. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the water’s path through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage involves specific technologies and regulatory standards designed to ensure that water is not only available but also safe, consistent, and reliable.

**Collection: The Source of Supply**

The process begins at the source, where a city identifies and secures its water supply. Most major cities rely on surface water sources, such as rivers, lakes, or reservoirs, though some utilize groundwater from aquifers. The choice of source depends on geography, population size, and environmental constraints. For example, New York City draws its water primarily from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds in the Catskill Mountains. This water is naturally filtered through layers of granite and soil as it flows downhill, reducing the need for extensive treatment. In contrast, Los Angeles relies heavily on imported water from the Colorado River and Northern California, requiring massive pumping infrastructure to move water across hundreds of miles.

Collection involves two primary methods: intake structures and wells. In surface water systems, intakes are large, submerged structures located at the edge of a lake or river. These intakes are designed to draw water from depths where sediment and surface debris are less prevalent. Screens at the intake prevent fish, leaves, and large debris from entering the system. In groundwater systems, collection occurs through wells drilled into underground aquifers. These wells pump water directly from porous rock layers, often requiring less initial physical filtration but more rigorous chemical testing to ensure the absence of natural contaminants like arsenic or radon. Regardless of the source, the raw water entering the system is rarely pure; it contains suspended solids, microorganisms, organic matter, and dissolved minerals.

**Treatment: Transforming Raw Water into Potable Water**

Once collected, the water enters a treatment plant, where it undergoes a series of physical and chemical processes to meet strict safety standards. The specific treatment steps depend on the quality of the source water, but a conventional surface water treatment plant follows a standard sequence: coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection.
