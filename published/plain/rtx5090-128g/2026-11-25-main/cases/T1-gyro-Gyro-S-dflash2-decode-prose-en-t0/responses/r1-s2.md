A city water supply is a complex, engineered system designed to deliver safe, potable water from natural sources to millions of households, businesses, and industrial facilities. The journey from a river, lake, or underground aquifer to the kitchen tap involves a series of critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. Each step is essential to ensure that the water is not only available but also safe for consumption, free from pathogens, contaminants, and excessive sediment. This detailed explanation explores how these systems function, using concrete examples such as the New York City water system, the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, and smaller municipal systems like those in Portland, Oregon, to illustrate the principles in action.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Water

The process begins with the collection of raw water from natural sources. Cities typically rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and historical infrastructure.

**Surface Water Collection:**
In many large cities, surface water is the primary source. For example, New York City relies on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast reservoir systems located in upstate New York. These reservoirs, such as the Ashokan Reservoir and the Cannonsville Reservoir, collect rain and snowmelt from the surrounding mountains. The water flows into these reservoirs via tributaries and streams. The reservoirs act as massive storage tanks, holding billions of gallons of water. The collection process involves intake structures—large pipes or tunnels that draw water from the reservoir into the treatment facilities. In New York, gravity-fed tunnels transport water from the reservoirs to treatment plants in the Bronx and Queens, minimizing the need for pumping and thus reducing energy costs.

**Groundwater Collection:**
Smaller cities or those in arid regions often rely on groundwater. For instance, Phoenix, Arizona, draws heavily from underground aquifers. Wells are drilled deep into the earth, sometimes hundreds of feet down, to access water stored in porous rock layers. Pumps extract this water, which is then transported to treatment facilities. Groundwater collection requires careful monitoring to prevent over-extraction, which can lead to subsidence (sinking of the ground) or depletion of the aquifer. In some cases, cities like Los Angeles use a mix of sources, including imported water from the Colorado River via aqueducts, local groundwater, and recycled water.
