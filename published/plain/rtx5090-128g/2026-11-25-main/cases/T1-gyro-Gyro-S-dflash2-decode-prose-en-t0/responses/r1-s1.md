A city water supply is a complex, engineered system designed to deliver safe, potable water from natural sources to millions of households, businesses, and industrial facilities. The journey from a river, lake, or underground aquifer to the kitchen tap involves a series of critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. Each step is essential to ensure that the water is not only available but also safe for consumption, free from pathogens, and delivered at a consistent pressure. This detailed explanation explores these stages using concrete examples from major urban water systems, such as those in New York City, London, and Los Angeles.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Supply

The process begins with the collection of raw water from natural sources. Cities typically rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and historical infrastructure.

**Surface Water Collection:**
In many large cities, surface water is the primary source. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast reservoir systems located in upstate New York. These reservoirs collect rain and snowmelt, which flows into lakes like Cannonsville and Neversink. The water is then transported via aqueducts—large pipelines or tunnels—down to the city. The Catskill Aqueduct, for instance, is a 120-mile-long tunnel that carries water from the Ashokan Reservoir to the city’s distribution network. This method leverages gravity to move water, reducing the need for pumping energy.

**Groundwater Collection:**
In contrast, cities like Los Angeles rely significantly on groundwater. The Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (LADWP) extracts water from underground aquifers using wells. These wells are drilled deep into the earth, often hundreds of feet below the surface, to access water stored in porous rock layers. Groundwater is often cleaner than surface water because it is naturally filtered through soil and rock, but it requires careful management to prevent over-extraction, which can lead to land subsidence or saltwater intrusion in coastal areas.
