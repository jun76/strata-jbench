I can explain the general process, but I can’t provide a step-by-step operational guide that could be used to bypass or compromise a specific city’s water system. What follows is a public-information overview of how municipal water systems work.

A city water supply begins at a source. That source may be a surface reservoir, a river, a lake, a spring, or a groundwater well. In a surface-water system, intake structures draw water from a river or reservoir through a screened intake. The screen keeps out large debris such as leaves, branches, and fish. In a groundwater system, wells draw water from an aquifer. The city may use one source or several, depending on reliability and seasonal availability.

Once water is collected, it is transported to a treatment plant. For surface water, this often means pumping through a pipeline or canal. For groundwater, the water may already be relatively clean but still require treatment for iron, manganese, hardness, nitrates, or other contaminants.

Treatment is designed to remove or reduce harmful substances and make water safe for drinking. A typical surface-water treatment process includes:

1. Coagulation and flocculation: Chemicals such as alum or ferric chloride are added. These help tiny particles clump together into larger “flocs.”
2. Sedimentation: The water slows down in a basin, allowing the flocs to settle out.
3. Filtration: The water passes through sand, gravel, or other media that trap remaining particles.
4. Disinfection: Chlorine, chloramine, ozone, or ultraviolet light is used to kill or inactivate bacteria, viruses, and other microorganisms.
5. Fluoridation or other additives: Some cities add fluoride to improve dental health, or adjust pH and hardness to reduce corrosion.

For example, a city drawing from a river might use coagulation to remove turbidity, then sand filtration to remove fine particles, then chlorine disinfection to prevent microbial contamination. A city relying on wells might instead use aeration, filtration, or chemical treatment to remove iron and manganese.
