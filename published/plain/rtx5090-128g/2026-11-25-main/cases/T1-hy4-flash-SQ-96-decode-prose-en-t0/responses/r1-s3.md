A city water supply is a vast, interconnected system that moves water from a natural source to millions of individual taps while keeping it safe, reliable, and at the right pressure. Although the exact design varies by city, most systems follow the same basic sequence: **source water collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring**. A useful way to understand the system is to follow a single drop of water on its journey.

Imagine a city of two million people located beside a large river and a set of hills. Its water may begin in a reservoir upstream, flow down a river, enter an intake structure, pass through a treatment plant, move into storage tanks, travel through miles of buried pipes, and finally emerge from a kitchen faucet. Every stage includes engineering controls designed to protect public health and maintain service during droughts, storms, pipe breaks, or changes in demand.

## 1. Source water collection: where the water comes from

The first step is obtaining raw water from a dependable source. Cities typically use one or more of the following:

- **Surface water**: rivers, lakes, and reservoirs  
- **Groundwater**: aquifers accessed through wells  
- **Imported water**: water transported from another region through aqueducts or pipelines  
- **Recycled or reclaimed water**: treated wastewater used for non-drinking purposes such as irrigation or industrial cooling  

Many large cities rely on a combination. For example, New York City gets much of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds. Los Angeles imports water from distant sources such as the Colorado River and Northern California, while also using local groundwater and recycled water. London relies heavily on the River Thames and River Lee, supplemented by groundwater.

### Surface water collection

If a city uses a river or lake, it needs an **intake structure**. This is usually a screened opening or tower located offshore or along the riverbank. Screens remove large debris such as branches, leaves, plastic, and fish. Intakes are often placed at a depth that avoids floating pollution and surface debris but does not stir up too much mud from the bottom.

For example, a city drawing from a river may have an intake crib in midstream. Water enters through submerged ports, passes through coarse bars, and then flows through a conduit or pump station to the treatment plant.
