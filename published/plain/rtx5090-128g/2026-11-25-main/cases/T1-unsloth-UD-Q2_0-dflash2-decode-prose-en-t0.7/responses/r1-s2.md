The journey of water from its natural source to the faucet in your home is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical engineering feats in modern society. While turning a handle seems like a trivial act, behind that simple motion lies a vast, interconnected network of civil engineering, chemical processing, hydraulic physics, and rigorous scientific monitoring. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the lifecycle of water through its five primary stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring. Each stage is designed not only to deliver water but to do so safely, consistently, and at the correct pressure.

**1. Collection: The Source and Intake**

The process begins with **collection**, where raw water is gathered from natural sources. Cities typically rely on one of two primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, or reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The choice of source dictates the subsequent infrastructure.

For example, many major metropolitan areas, such as New York City, rely on surface water stored in vast upstate reservoirs like the Croton or Catskill systems. Water is collected via massive intake structures that draw water from the reservoir into large-diameter pipes. These intakes are often equipped with bar racks or screens to prevent large debris, fish, and other organic matter from entering the system. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, which sit in arid regions, may rely heavily on groundwater or imported water via canals that feed into local storage tanks. Groundwater collection involves drilling deep boreholes into underground aquifers and using submersible pumps to bring the water to the surface.

A critical aspect of collection is **pre-treatment screening**. Before water even enters the treatment plant, it is assessed for turbidity (cloudiness caused by suspended particles like silt, algae, or clay). High turbidity can interfere with later chemical treatment processes. Therefore, intake systems often include preliminary settling basins where heavy sediments sink to the bottom, reducing the load on the treatment facilities. This initial step is crucial because it determines the chemical dosing rates required later. For instance, if a river is in flood season, turbidity spikes dramatically, requiring the water utility to adjust their operations immediately to maintain safety standards.
