A city water supply is a vast, interconnected system that moves water from a natural source to millions of individual taps while keeping it safe, reliable, and at the right pressure. Although the exact design varies from place to place, most urban systems follow the same basic sequence: source water is collected, treated to remove contaminants, stored, distributed through a network of pipes, pressurized so it reaches upper floors and distant neighborhoods, and continuously monitored for quality.

Below is a detailed explanation of each stage, with concrete examples.

---

## 1. Water collection: where the city’s water comes from

The first step is finding and collecting a dependable source of water. Cities usually rely on one or more of the following:

### Surface water
Surface water comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. Many large cities depend on reservoirs because they can store water from rainfall and snowmelt.

For example, New York City gets much of its water from a system of reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located more than 100 miles from the city. Rain falls in those protected watersheds, collects in streams, flows into reservoirs, and is then sent through aqueducts toward the city.

London, by contrast, historically relied heavily on the River Thames and its tributaries. Because rivers can contain sewage, industrial discharges, and agricultural runoff, river water usually requires more intensive treatment than protected reservoir water.

### Groundwater
Other cities use groundwater pumped from underground aquifers. Wells draw water from porous rock or sand layers that naturally filter some impurities as water moves through the ground.

For instance, many cities in the American Midwest use groundwater from aquifers. Munich, Germany, obtains a large share of its drinking water from groundwater wells located near the city, supported by infiltration of river water through gravel beds. Groundwater is often clear and cool, but it may contain naturally occurring minerals such as iron, manganese, arsenic, or hardness-causing calcium and magnesium.

### Desalination
In dry coastal regions, cities may use desalination, which removes salt from seawater or brackish water.

Dubai, Saudi Arabia, Israel, and parts of California rely significantly on desalination. The process usually involves reverse osmosis, where seawater is pushed through a semipermeable membrane that allows water molecules through but blocks most salts and contaminants. Desalination is reliable but energy-intensive and produces a concentrated brine that must be carefully disposed of.
