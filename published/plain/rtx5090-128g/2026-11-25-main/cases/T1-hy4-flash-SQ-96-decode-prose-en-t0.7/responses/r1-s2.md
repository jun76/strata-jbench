A modern city water supply is a coordinated system that moves water from a natural or engineered source through treatment, storage, pumping, distribution, and monitoring before it reaches a tap. The goal is not simply to “deliver water,” but to deliver a reliable, safe, pleasant-tasting supply at adequate pressure, every hour of every day, while wasting as little as possible. To understand how this works, it is useful to follow the journey of water step by step: from source to tap.

## 1. Sources of city water

The first question for any water utility is where the water comes from. Cities usually rely on one or more of the following sources:

- **Surface water**: rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and canals.
- **Groundwater**: aquifers accessed through wells.
- **Imported water**: water transported from another region through aqueducts or pipelines.
- **Recycled or reclaimed water**: treated wastewater used for non-potable purposes such as irrigation, industrial cooling, or toilet flushing in some systems.
- **Desalinated water**: seawater or brackish water treated to remove salt, common in arid coastal cities.

Many large cities use a mix. For example, New York City obtains most of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, with additional supply from the Croton system. London relies heavily on rivers such as the Thames and Lee, as well as groundwater. Singapore uses local reservoirs, imported Malaysian water, recycled water known as NEWater, and desalination. This diversified approach improves resilience: if one source is affected by drought or contamination, others can help maintain supply.

### Watershed protection

For surface water, the quality of the source matters enormously. Utilities often manage or influence the surrounding watershed to reduce pollution. This can include protecting forests, controlling agricultural runoff, limiting industrial discharges, maintaining wetlands, and monitoring upstream activities.

New York City is a well-known example. Instead of building a massive filtration plant for its Catskill and Delaware sources, the city invested in watershed protection: land acquisition, sewer upgrades in upstream communities, erosion control, and strict regulations. Because the source water is relatively clean, it requires less intensive treatment than a muddy river source.
