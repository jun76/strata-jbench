A modern city water supply is a chain of engineered systems that moves water from a natural or engineered source into homes, businesses, schools, and hospitals. The goal is not simply to “bring water in,” but to deliver enough safe drinking water at adequate pressure, every hour of every day, while protecting it from contamination as it travels. The process usually includes source selection and collection, treatment, storage, pumping and distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. A useful way to understand it is to follow a drop of water from a river, lake, aquifer, or reservoir to a kitchen tap.

## 1. Sources of city water

Cities use two broad categories of water sources: surface water and groundwater.

**Surface water** comes from rivers, lakes, streams, and reservoirs. Many large cities rely on reservoirs because they can store water collected from rainfall and snowmelt. For example, New York City gets much of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located outside the city. The water travels by gravity through large aqueducts toward the city. This works because the source area is at a higher elevation than much of the city.

**Groundwater** comes from underground aquifers, which are layers of permeable rock, sand, or gravel that hold water. Cities with aquifers may use wells to pump water to the surface. For example, many cities in the American Midwest and parts of Europe rely heavily on groundwater. Groundwater is often naturally filtered as it moves through soil and rock, but it can still contain dissolved minerals, bacteria, nitrates from fertilizer, or industrial contaminants.

Some cities use a mix of both. Others use less conventional sources, such as desalination or recycled water. Coastal cities like Singapore, Dubai, and parts of California use desalination, which removes salt from seawater through reverse osmosis or distillation. Singapore also uses “NEWater,” highly treated reclaimed wastewater, mainly for industrial use and as a supplement to drinking water supplies during dry periods.

The choice of source depends on availability, cost, water quality, geography, legal rights, environmental impact, and reliability during droughts or emergencies.
