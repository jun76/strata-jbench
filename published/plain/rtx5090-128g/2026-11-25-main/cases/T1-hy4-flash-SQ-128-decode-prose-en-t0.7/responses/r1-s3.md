A city water supply is one of the most complex yet essential public infrastructure systems. It is designed to collect water from natural or engineered sources, treat it to make it safe for human consumption, store it in sufficient quantities, distribute it through an extensive pipe network, maintain adequate pressure so water reaches homes and businesses, and continuously monitor quality from source to tap. Although the system is often invisible to residents—water simply appears when a tap is opened—it involves careful engineering, public health safeguards, energy use, and round-the-clock management.

Below is a detailed explanation of how a municipal water supply works, following the journey of water from its source to the tap.

---

## 1. Water Sources and Collection

The first step in any city water supply is finding a reliable source of water. Cities typically use one or more of the following:

- Surface water: rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams  
- Groundwater: aquifers accessed through wells  
- Imported water: water transported from distant watersheds  
- Desalinated water: seawater converted to freshwater, common in arid coastal regions  
- Recycled water: treated wastewater used for non-potable purposes, and in some places indirectly integrated into drinking water supplies after advanced treatment  

### Surface Water Collection

Many large cities rely on rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. For example, New York City obtains much of its drinking water from reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located upstate. These reservoirs collect rainfall and snowmelt from large protected watersheds. The water then flows by gravity through tunnels and aqueducts toward the city.

A reservoir is often created by building a dam across a valley. The dam stores water during wet periods and releases it during dry periods. This helps regulate supply and reduce flooding downstream. Reservoirs also allow sediment to settle and provide a relatively stable source compared with a fast-flowing river.

Cities that draw from rivers often use intake structures. These are engineered openings or screens placed in the river or lake to withdraw water while keeping out large debris such as branches, leaves, and fish. For example, a city on a large river may have an intake crib offshore or a screened channel along the riverbank. Pumps then move the raw water to a treatment plant.
