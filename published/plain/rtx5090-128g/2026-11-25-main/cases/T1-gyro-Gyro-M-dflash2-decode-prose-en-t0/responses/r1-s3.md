The journey of a drop of water from a distant reservoir to the kitchen tap in a modern city is a marvel of civil engineering, public health science, and logistical precision. It is not merely a simple flow of liquid; it is a complex, multi-stage process designed to ensure that water is safe, clean, and available on demand. This system, often referred to as the "water cycle" of urban infrastructure, involves several critical phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. To understand this fully, we must trace the path of water through these stages, using concrete examples from major metropolitan systems like New York City, London, or Singapore.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Supply

The process begins at the source. Cities rarely rely on a single source; instead, they utilize a mix of surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and population density.

For example, New York City draws approximately 90% of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast, protected forested areas in upstate New York. These watersheds act as natural filters, collecting rain and snow that flows into reservoirs like the Ashokan Reservoir. Because the water is sourced from pristine, protected lands, it requires less intensive treatment than water drawn from heavily polluted rivers. In contrast, cities like London rely heavily on the River Thames and groundwater from the chalk aquifers beneath the city. Groundwater is often cleaner initially but can be susceptible to contamination from agricultural runoff or industrial waste if not managed carefully.

The collection phase involves intake structures. In surface water systems, large intake pipes or towers are placed in reservoirs or rivers to draw water into the treatment plant. These intakes are designed to avoid drawing in debris, algae, or sediment. In groundwater systems, wells are drilled deep into the earth to access aquifers. The water is then pumped or allowed to flow via gravity into the treatment facilities.

### 2. Treatment: Making Water Safe

Once collected, the water is rarely safe to drink directly. It may contain pathogens (bacteria, viruses, parasites), suspended solids, organic matter, and dissolved minerals. The treatment process is designed to remove these contaminants. The specific steps vary depending on the source water quality, but a typical conventional treatment plant follows a sequence of coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection.
