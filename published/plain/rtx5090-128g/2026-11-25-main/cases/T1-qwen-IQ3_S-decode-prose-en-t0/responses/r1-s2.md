The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, critical, and often invisible engineering feats of modern civilization. It is a continuous, high-stakes logistical operation that involves massive infrastructure, precise chemical engineering, rigorous biological monitoring, and sophisticated hydraulic management. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This process ensures that water is not only available but also safe, palatable, and reliable for millions of people every day.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be either surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends heavily on geography and geology. For example, New York City relies primarily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast, protected forested areas that feed into reservoirs. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely heavily on the Colorado River, while many Midwestern cities draw from deep aquifers.

Surface water collection typically involves large intake structures located in reservoirs or rivers. These intakes are designed to draw water from specific depths to avoid sediment, algae blooms, or debris. For instance, in a reservoir like Lake Mead, intake towers extend deep into the water column to draw cooler, clearer water from below the thermocline, where temperature and oxygen levels are more stable. Groundwater collection, on the other hand, involves pumping from wells. In cities like San Antonio, Texas, which sits atop the Edwards Aquifer, massive well fields pump water from deep limestone formations. The key challenge here is sustainability; over-pumping can lead to land subsidence or saltwater intrusion in coastal areas.

Once collected, the raw water is transported via large-diameter pipelines or aqueducts to the treatment plant. The Los Angeles Aqueduct, for example, transports water over 200 miles from the Owens Valley, relying on gravity to move millions of gallons per day without the need for pumping stations along much of the route. This initial transport phase is critical because it sets the baseline for the quality of water entering the treatment process.
