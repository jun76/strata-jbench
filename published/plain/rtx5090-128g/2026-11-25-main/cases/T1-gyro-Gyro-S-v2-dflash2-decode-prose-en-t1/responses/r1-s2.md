A modern city water supply is a complex engineering feat that transforms raw, potentially contaminated natural sources into safe, potable water delivered instantly to household faucets. This process involves a sequence of collection, purification, storage, distribution, and rigorous quality control, all orchestrated to ensure public health and operational reliability. To understand this system, we must trace the journey of water through six distinct stages, using concrete examples from major urban infrastructure systems.

### Stage 1: Collection – The Source of the Water

The journey begins with the collection of raw water from natural sources. Cities typically rely on a combination of surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers tapped by wells). The choice of source depends heavily on geography, population density, and historical infrastructure development.

For instance, New York City relies on the Catskill and Delaware Aqueducts, which bring water from reservoirs in the Catskill Mountains and the Delaware River basin. This water is drawn from protected watersheds where conservation laws prohibit development to maintain high purity levels. In contrast, cities in arid regions, such as Las Vegas, rely heavily on groundwater extracted from deep boreholes in the Las Vegas Valley aquifer, supplemented by surface water imported from Lake Mead via the Colorado River system.

The collection process involves intake structures—large pipes or tunnels that draw water from the source. In New York’s Ashokan Reservoir, massive conduits carry water under gravity flow for hundreds of miles, minimizing the need for pumping and saving energy. In groundwater systems, electric pumps draw water from deep within the earth, often from depths exceeding 100 meters, to access aquifers that are less susceptible to surface contamination. The raw water at this stage contains suspended solids, organic matter, dissolved minerals, bacteria, viruses, and potential chemical pollutants from agricultural runoff or urban sewage.

### Stage 2: Treatment – Purification and Conditioning

Raw water cannot be distributed directly to homes; it must undergo rigorous treatment to remove contaminants and meet strict regulatory standards, such as the U.S. Safe Drinking Water Act or the European Union Drinking Water Directive. The treatment plant is the heart of the water supply system, where physical and chemical processes transform raw water into safe water.
