The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, critical, and often overlooked engineering feats of modern civilization. It is a continuous, high-stakes logistical operation that involves hydrology, chemistry, civil engineering, and data science. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of water through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This process ensures that millions of people receive safe, potable water on demand, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which is typically either surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The choice of source depends heavily on geography and climate. For instance, a city like New York relies on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast, protected forested areas that feed into reservoirs. In contrast, a city like Phoenix, Arizona, relies heavily on the Colorado River and deep groundwater wells due to its arid environment.

Collection involves more than just scooping up water; it requires careful intake design to prevent contamination and damage to infrastructure. Surface water intakes are often located in deep, cold parts of a reservoir to avoid algae blooms and sediment. Screens are installed at the intake points to keep out fish, leaves, and debris. For groundwater, wells are drilled hundreds of feet into the earth to reach aquifers. These wells are lined with steel or concrete to prevent surface contaminants from seeping down. In many cities, the collection phase is also where the first line of defense against pollution occurs. Watershed protection programs, such as those in Seattle or Portland, involve purchasing land and enforcing strict zoning laws to prevent industrial runoff or agricultural pesticides from entering the water supply. This "source water protection" is far cheaper and more effective than trying to remove pollutants later in the treatment process.

### 2. Treatment: The Chemical and Physical Transformation

Once collected, raw water is rarely safe to drink. It contains suspended solids, organic matter, bacteria, viruses, and potentially harmful chemicals. The treatment plant is where the transformation from "raw water" to "potable water" occurs. This process is a multi-step cascade of physical and chemical operations.
