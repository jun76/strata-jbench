A city water supply is a engineered system that moves water from a natural source to homes, businesses, and public taps. It typically includes several linked stages: **source collection**, **treatment**, **storage**, **distribution**, **pressure management**, and **quality monitoring**. The exact design varies by city, but the basic goal is the same: provide enough safe water at adequate pressure, every hour of every day.

Below is a detailed walkthrough of the process, using concrete examples such as a river-based system, a groundwater system, and a large regional reservoir supply.

---

## 1. Source collection: where the water comes from

A city’s water supply begins with a **source**. Common sources include:

- **Surface water**: rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams  
- **Groundwater**: aquifers accessed through wells  
- **Imported water**: water transported from another region through canals or pipelines  
- **Recycled or alternative sources**: in some cities, treated wastewater is reused for non-potable purposes or replenishes aquifers

### Surface water example

A city located beside a river may draw water through **intake structures**. These structures are built along the riverbank or in the river channel. They include screens that keep out large debris such as branches, leaves, fish, and trash.

For example, a mid-sized city might operate a river intake that pulls in 80 million liters per day. The intake has:

- A steel screen with openings small enough to block debris
- A pumping station that lifts water into a treatment plant
- A flow meter that records how much water is being collected
- A sampling point where raw water quality is tested

If the river has seasonal changes, the city may also monitor turbidity after storms. Heavy rain can increase sediment and runoff, making the raw water muddier and harder to treat.

### Reservoir example

Many cities rely on reservoirs. A reservoir is a large artificial lake created by damming a river or stream. It stores water from rainfall and snowmelt, then releases it gradually during dry periods.

For instance, a metropolitan area might receive water from a reservoir 60 kilometers away. Water flows from the reservoir through an outlet works structure into a transmission pipeline. The reservoir acts as both a source and a long-term storage buffer. During a dry summer, the city may manage reservoir releases carefully to avoid running low.
