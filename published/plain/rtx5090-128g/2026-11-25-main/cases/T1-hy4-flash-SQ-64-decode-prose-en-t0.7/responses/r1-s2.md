A city water supply is a chain of systems that moves water from a natural or engineered source to the tap in a home, school, hospital, or business. The chain usually includes **source water collection**, **treatment**, **storage**, **distribution**, **pressure management**, and **quality monitoring**. Each stage is designed to protect public health, maintain reliable service, and deliver water at the right quantity and pressure.

Below is a detailed explanation of how a typical municipal water supply works, from source to tap.

---

## 1. Source water collection

The first step is finding and collecting raw water. Cities use different sources depending on geography, climate, and local infrastructure.

Common sources include:

- **Rivers and streams**
- **Lakes and reservoirs**
- **Groundwater wells**
- **Springs**
- **Desalinated seawater** in coastal or arid regions
- **Imported water** transported from another region

For example, a city like Denver, Colorado, relies heavily on mountain reservoirs fed by snowmelt. A city like Phoenix, Arizona, uses a mix of groundwater, Colorado River water, and reclaimed water. Coastal cities such as Perth, Australia, use desalination plants to supplement traditional supplies.

### Surface water collection

If a city uses surface water, water is collected from rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. Reservoirs are often built by damming rivers. They store water during wet seasons and release it during dry periods.

For instance, New York City gets much of its drinking water from reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds. These reservoirs collect rainfall and snowmelt, and the water flows through streams into large storage basins before being sent to treatment plants.

Surface water intake structures are designed to draw water from a controlled depth. This helps avoid sediment, floating debris, and contaminated surface runoff. At a reservoir, an intake tower may have several openings at different depths. Operators can choose the best-quality layer of water depending on seasonal conditions.

### Groundwater collection

Groundwater is collected through wells. Water percolates through soil and rock into underground aquifers. Wells pump water to the surface using submersible or vertical turbine pumps.

For example, many smaller cities in the American Midwest rely on groundwater aquifers. In Minneapolis, groundwater is part of the city’s supply, drawn from buried glacial sand and gravel aquifers.

Groundwater often has fewer visible impurities than surface water, but it can contain dissolved minerals such as iron, manganese, calcium, arsenic, or nitrate
