The journey of a glass of water from a distant reservoir to a kitchen tap is one of the most complex, reliable, and often invisible feats of modern civil engineering. While the end result appears simple—a clear, odorless liquid flowing from a faucet—the process involves a sophisticated network of infrastructure, chemical science, hydraulic physics, and rigorous regulatory oversight. This system, known as municipal water supply, operates through several distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is critical to ensuring that the water delivered is not only safe for consumption but also available on demand, regardless of the time of day or the elevation of the user’s home.

The process begins with **collection**, where water is sourced from either surface water or groundwater. Surface water sources include rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. For example, the city of New York relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware aqueduct systems, which transport water from reservoirs located over 100 miles away. These systems utilize gravity to move water through massive tunnels and pipes, minimizing the need for energy-intensive pumping. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely on the Colorado River, where water is diverted via canals and pumped uphill into storage facilities. Groundwater collection involves drilling wells into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing permeable rock or unconsolidated materials. Cities like San Antonio, Texas, draw a significant portion of their supply from the Edwards Aquifer. Groundwater is often naturally filtered through soil and rock layers, which can reduce the initial treatment burden, but it may contain higher levels of minerals like iron or manganese that require specific removal processes.

Once collected, the raw water enters the **treatment** phase, a multi-step process designed to remove contaminants, pathogens, and undesirable physical characteristics. The first step is typically screening, where large debris such as leaves, fish, and trash are removed using bar screens. Following this, the water undergoes coagulation and flocculation. Chemicals called coagulants, such as aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride, are added to the water. These chemicals neutralize the negative electrical charges of suspended particles, allowing them to clump together into larger, heavier particles known as flocs. In a concrete example, the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power uses alum in its treatment plants to effectively bind with turbidity-causing particles.
