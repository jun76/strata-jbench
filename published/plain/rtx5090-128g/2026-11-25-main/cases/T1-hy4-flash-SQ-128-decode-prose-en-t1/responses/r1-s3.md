A city water supply is one of the most essential public infrastructure systems. It must deliver safe, reliable, and adequate water to homes, businesses, hospitals, schools, and industries every hour of every day. Although a tap may appear to be the simple endpoint of a pipe, the water reaching it has usually traveled through a long chain of engineered processes: source selection and collection, treatment, storage, pumping, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. A failure at any stage can affect public health, economic activity, and public confidence. The following explanation traces the full journey of municipal water from source to tap, with concrete examples along the way.

## 1. Water sources and collection

The first step is finding a dependable source of raw water. Cities typically use one or more of the following: rivers, lakes, reservoirs, groundwater aquifers, springs, or, in some regions, desalinated seawater or recycled water for non-potable uses. The choice depends on geography, climate, population size, cost, legal rights, and water quality.

For example, New York City obtains much of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located well outside the city. These reservoirs collect rainfall and snowmelt from large forested areas. Because the watershed is carefully managed, the raw water is relatively clean before treatment. In contrast, a city like London relies heavily on the Thames and Lee rivers as well as groundwater. River water can be more variable because it is affected by upstream discharges, rainfall, agricultural runoff, and urban stormwater. Coastal cities such as Singapore, Israel’s metropolitan areas, or parts of California use desalination as a supplementary source, especially where freshwater is scarce.

### Surface water collection

Surface water systems collect water from rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. A reservoir may be formed by damming a river, creating a large storage basin that captures seasonal rainfall and snowmelt. Water is then drawn from the reservoir through intake structures. These intakes often have screens to block fish, debris, and large floating objects.

For instance, a reservoir intake may include a vertical tower with ports at different depths. Operators can draw water from the level that has the best quality. In warm weather, surface water may develop algae blooms, while deeper water may be colder and lower in algae but sometimes lower in dissolved oxygen or higher in certain dissolved metals. Selecting the right depth is an operational decision.
