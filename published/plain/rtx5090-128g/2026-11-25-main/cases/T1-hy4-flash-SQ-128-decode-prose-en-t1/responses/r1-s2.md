A modern city water supply is a large, interconnected system that moves water from natural or engineered sources to homes, businesses, hospitals, schools, and fire hydrants. Although the water that comes out of a tap may appear simple and clear, getting it there safely requires careful planning, engineering, treatment, monitoring, and constant maintenance. A city water system generally includes six major stages: source collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is designed to protect public health and provide reliable service, even during droughts, storms, equipment failures, or sudden changes in demand.

Below is a detailed explanation of how a city water supply works from source to tap, with concrete examples.

---

## 1. Water Sources and Collection

The first step in any city water supply is finding and collecting enough water to meet demand. Cities usually rely on one or more of the following sources:

- Surface water: rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams  
- Groundwater: aquifers accessed through wells  
- Imported water: water transported from another region through pipelines or canals  
- Recycled or reclaimed water: treated wastewater used for non-drinking purposes  
- Desalinated water: seawater or brackish water treated to remove salt, used in some coastal cities  

### Surface Water Collection

Many large cities use rivers, lakes, or reservoirs as their main drinking water source. For example, New York City gets much of its drinking water from reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located outside the city. These reservoirs collect rainfall and snowmelt from protected forested lands. The water then flows by gravity through tunnels and aqueducts toward the city.

Surface water is often collected by building a dam across a river or stream to create a reservoir. The reservoir serves several purposes: it stores water during wet periods, helps control flooding, and provides a relatively stable source during dry periods. Water can be drawn from different depths of the reservoir, because temperature and sediment levels vary. Operators often use intake structures with screens to keep out leaves, fish, and large debris.

For instance, a reservoir intake may have a vertical tower with openings at several elevations. In summer, warmer water may sit near the top while colder, denser water stays lower. Operators can choose the intake depth that provides the best raw water quality.
