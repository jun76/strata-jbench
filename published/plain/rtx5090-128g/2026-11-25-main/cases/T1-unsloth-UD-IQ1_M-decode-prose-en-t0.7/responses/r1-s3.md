The journey of a glass of water from its natural origin to the kitchen tap is one of the most complex, invisible, and critical infrastructural achievements in modern civilization. It is a continuous, high-volume logistical operation that blends civil engineering, chemistry, physics, and public health policy. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through several distinct phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This process is designed not only to deliver water but to do so safely, consistently, and with sufficient pressure to overcome gravity and friction over long distances.

The journey begins at the **source**, which can vary significantly depending on geography and available resources. Common sources include surface water (lakes, rivers, and reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers tapped by wells). For example, a city like New York City draws much of its supply from Catskill and Delaware reservoirs, while Phoenix, Arizona, relies heavily on groundwater and the Colorado River. The choice of source dictates the subsequent treatment requirements. Surface water is generally easier to access in large volumes but is more susceptible to contamination from runoff, algae blooms, and pathogens. Groundwater, conversely, is naturally filtered through soil and rock layers, offering higher initial clarity and lower turbidity, but it may contain dissolved minerals like iron, manganese, or radon, requiring specific treatment methods.

Once the raw water is collected, it enters the **treatment plant**, a massive industrial facility often resembling a city within a city. The primary goal here is to remove physical, chemical, and biological contaminants. The first step is usually **screening and coagulation**. Large debris, such as leaves, sticks, and fish, are removed by bar screens. The water then moves into rapid mix basins where chemicals, typically aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride, are added. These chemicals act as coagulants, neutralizing the negative electrical charges that keep microscopic particles suspended in the water. As the water flows into flocculation basins, slow paddles gently stir the water, allowing the destabilized particles to clump together into larger, heavier aggregates called "flocs."

The next critical stage is **sedimentation**. The water flows into huge clarifier tanks, often circular or rectangular, where the flow velocity slows dramatically.
