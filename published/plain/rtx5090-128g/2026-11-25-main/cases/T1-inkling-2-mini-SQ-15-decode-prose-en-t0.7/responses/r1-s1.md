I can explain the general flow of a municipal water supply in an educational way.

A city water supply begins at the source. That source may be a surface reservoir, a river, a lake, a spring, or a deep groundwater well. In a city, the source is often chosen for reliability and distance from pollution. For example, a reservoir may be built upstream in a protected watershed to capture rainfall runoff, while a groundwater well may be drilled into an aquifer beneath the city.

Collection is the first physical step. Surface water may be drawn from a reservoir or river through intake structures with screens or grates to keep out large debris. Groundwater is pumped from wells. The collected water is then moved to a treatment plant.

Treatment removes contaminants and makes the water safe for drinking. The exact process depends on the source. For surface water, treatment often includes coagulation and flocculation, where chemicals cause small particles to clump together; sedimentation, where those clumps settle; filtration, where water passes through sand or other media; and disinfection, often with chlorine, chloramine, or ultraviolet light. For groundwater, treatment may be simpler, but still include filtration, disinfection, and sometimes iron or manganese removal, softening, or pH adjustment. Concrete example: a city that draws from a river may add aluminum sulfate to help dirt and algae particles clump, then filter the water through sand beds before disinfecting it. A city relying on wells may mainly need filtration and disinfection, plus monitoring for nitrates or arsenic if those are present.

Storage comes next. After treatment, water is held in reservoirs, elevated tanks, or underground storage facilities. Storage smooths out demand changes. A city’s water use is highest in the morning and evening, so the system stores extra treated water during low-demand periods. Elevated tanks also provide pressure because gravity helps push water through the pipes.

Distribution is the network of pipes that carries treated water from storage to homes, schools, hospitals, and businesses. Pipes are often arranged in a looped grid rather than a single line, so if one section is damaged, water can still reach nearby areas through another route. Along the way, valves and meters control flow and allow crews to isolate sections for maintenance.
