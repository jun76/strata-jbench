A city water supply is a chain of engineered systems designed to turn raw water into a reliable, safe, and continuously available resource. The journey begins at the source, whether that is a river, reservoir, lake, groundwater aquifer, or a combination of these. Once collected, water must be transported to a treatment facility, processed to remove or reduce contaminants, stored in reservoirs or tanks, and then distributed through a network of pipes to homes, businesses, and public buildings. Along the way, engineers manage pressure, monitor quality, and plan for demand changes.

Collection is the first stage. For surface water, cities often draw from rivers or reservoirs. For example, many large urban systems use a reservoir upstream to create a stable supply even during dry periods. In groundwater-dependent cities, wells are drilled into aquifers and pumped to the surface. The source must be protected because contamination at the intake can affect the entire system. That is why watersheds are managed, intakes are located away from pollutants, and catchment areas are monitored. A city may also have multiple sources so that if one is disrupted, the system can still operate.

After collection, water is transported to the treatment plant. This is often done by gravity when elevation allows, or by pumping when it does not. The treatment process is designed to make water safe for drinking and to meet public health standards. In a conventional surface-water plant, the first step is often screening or coarse filtration to remove leaves, debris, and large particles. Then water may be mixed with chemicals such as coagulants and flocculants. These chemicals cause fine particles and colloids to clump together, forming larger flocs that can be removed. Next, the water passes through clarifiers or sedimentation basins where the flocs settle out. After that, water goes through filtration, commonly through sand or other media, to trap remaining particles. Finally, it is disinfected, often with chlorine, chloramine, or ultraviolet light, to kill or inactivate microorganisms. Some systems also add fluoride or other substances, or remove excess minerals, depending on local needs.

Groundwater systems often require simpler treatment, but not no treatment. They may need filtration, disinfection, iron or manganese removal, or treatment for specific contaminants such as nitrates or arsenic.
