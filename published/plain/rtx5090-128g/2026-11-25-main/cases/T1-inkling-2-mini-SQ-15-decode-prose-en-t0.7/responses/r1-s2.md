A city water supply begins with a source. That source might be a river, a lake, a reservoir, groundwater from a well, or a combination of these. In many cities, the source is chosen for reliability and distance from heavy pollution. A river provides a large volume, but it may carry sediment, nutrients, and seasonal changes. A reservoir stores rainfall and runoff, giving the city a buffer when rain is low. Groundwater is often cleaner in terms of visible turbidity, but it can be vulnerable to contamination from nearby land use, especially if the aquifer is shallow. For example, a city near a river might draw water upstream of industrial discharge zones, while a city in a dry region might rely on a mountain-fed reservoir or deep wells.

The first major step is collection. At the source, intake structures are built to capture water without drawing in too much sediment or debris. River intakes may use screens to keep fish, leaves, and large debris out. Wells use casings and screens to allow water to enter while limiting the entry of sand and surface contaminants. Reservoirs use controlled outlets or pumps to move water into the treatment system. Collection is not simply about gathering water; it is about protecting the quality of the water before it enters the process. A poorly placed intake can increase treatment costs and reduce the reliability of the supply.

Once collected, water moves to treatment. Treatment is designed to remove or neutralize substances that could harm public health or make water unsuitable for drinking. The exact process depends on the source. River water often requires coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection. In coagulation, chemicals such as alum are added to cause tiny particles to clump together. In flocculation, the clumps grow into larger flocs. Sedimentation lets these flocs settle out of the water. Filtration then passes the water through sand, gravel, or other media to remove remaining particles. Disinfection follows, often with chlorine, chloramines, ultraviolet light, or other methods, to kill or inactivate bacteria, viruses, and other pathogens.
