The journey of water from a natural source to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical engineering feats in modern society. It is not merely a matter of opening a valve; it is a sophisticated system involving hydrology, chemistry, mechanical engineering, and public health policy. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring. Each stage relies on concrete infrastructure and specific operational protocols to ensure that the water delivered is safe, reliable, and consistent.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, population size, and local geology. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are protected forested areas that provide high-quality raw water with minimal treatment needs. In contrast, Los Angeles draws from the Colorado River and local groundwater basins, requiring more extensive treatment due to higher salinity and sediment loads.

**Surface Water Collection**
For surface water, collection involves intake structures located in reservoirs or rivers. These intakes are designed to draw water from specific depths to avoid surface debris or bottom sediment. A concrete example is the intake tower in a reservoir like Lake Mead. Water flows through large-diameter pipes, often called "raw water mains," to the treatment plant. These pipes can be miles long. In some cases, such as in arid regions, water must be pumped uphill, requiring significant energy. The intake must also include screens to prevent fish, leaves, and other debris from entering the system. If the source is a river, the intake might be located downstream of urban runoff to minimize contamination, but upstream of industrial discharge zones.

**Groundwater Collection**
Groundwater collection involves wells drilled into aquifers. A typical municipal well field might consist of dozens of wells, each pumping water from depths ranging from 100 to 1,000 feet. For instance, in Phoenix, Arizona, the city relies on deep wells tapping into the Salt River aquifer. These wells use submersible pumps powered by electricity to lift water to the surface.
