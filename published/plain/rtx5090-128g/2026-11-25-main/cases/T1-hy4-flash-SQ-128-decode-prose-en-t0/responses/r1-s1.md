A modern city water supply is a vast, interconnected system that moves water from natural sources to homes, businesses, hospitals, and factories. Although a person may turn on a tap and see clean water appear instantly, that water has usually traveled through a complex chain of infrastructure: source collection, treatment, storage, pumping, distribution, pressure control, and continuous quality monitoring. The system must operate every hour of every day, meet strict health standards, and handle changing demand, droughts, storms, pipe breaks, and contamination risks. Below is a detailed explanation of how a city water supply works from source to tap, with concrete examples.

## 1. Water sources: where the supply begins

City water systems use two main categories of sources: surface water and groundwater.

### Surface water

Surface water comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. Many large cities rely on reservoirs because they can store large volumes of water and provide a relatively stable supply. For example, New York City gets much of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located outside the city. These reservoirs collect rainfall and snowmelt, and the water naturally filters through forests and soils before reaching the reservoir.

Rivers are also common sources. A city along a large river may withdraw water upstream, treat it, and deliver it to customers. However, rivers can vary greatly with seasons and may carry pollution from farms, towns, industry, and urban runoff. For example, a city drawing from a river downstream of agricultural areas may need extra treatment for pesticides, fertilizers, and sediment.

### Groundwater

Groundwater is stored underground in aquifers, which are layers of porous rock, sand, or gravel that hold water. Cities access groundwater through wells. Groundwater is often naturally filtered as it moves through soil and rock, so it may require less treatment than surface water. However, it can contain dissolved minerals such as iron, manganese, calcium, magnesium, arsenic, or nitrates.

For example, many cities in Florida rely heavily on groundwater from the Floridan Aquifer. In parts of India, cities such as Bengaluru have historically depended on groundwater wells, though rapid growth has led to over-extraction and declining water tables.
