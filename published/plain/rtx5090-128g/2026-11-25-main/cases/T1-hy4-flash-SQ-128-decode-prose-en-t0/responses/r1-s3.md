A city water supply is one of the most complex yet essential public systems. It is designed to collect water from natural or engineered sources, treat it to safe drinking-water standards, store enough water to meet daily and emergency demand, distribute it through a vast network of pipes, maintain adequate pressure so water reaches homes and businesses, and continuously monitor quality from source to tap. Although the exact design varies by city depending on geography, climate, population, and available water sources, the basic journey is similar almost everywhere.

Below is a detailed explanation of how a modern urban water supply works, from the source to the tap.

## 1. Water Sources: Where the Water Comes From

The first step in any city water supply is finding a reliable source. Cities typically use one or more of the following:

### Surface water
Surface water comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. Many large cities depend on rivers or reservoirs because they can provide large volumes of water.

For example:
- New York City gets much of its water from reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds.
- London uses water from the River Thames and River Lee, as well as groundwater.
- Singapore uses local reservoirs but also imports water from Malaysia and produces reclaimed and desalinated water.

Surface water is relatively easy to collect in large quantities, but it is more vulnerable to pollution from farming, industry, urban runoff, sewage, and storms. It also varies with seasons: droughts can reduce reservoir levels, while heavy rain can increase turbidity and wash pollutants into rivers.

### Groundwater
Groundwater is stored underground in aquifers, which are layers of porous rock, sand, or gravel that hold water. Cities access groundwater through wells.

Examples:
- Many cities in the American Midwest use groundwater from aquifers.
- Mexico City historically relied heavily on groundwater, though over-extraction has caused land subsidence.
- Bangalore, India, has long depended on groundwater, although rapid growth has strained supplies.

Groundwater is often naturally filtered as it moves through soil and rock, so it may require less treatment than surface water. However, it can contain dissolved minerals such as iron, manganese, arsenic, fluoride, or nitrates. It can also be contaminated by septic systems, fertilizers, industrial chemicals, or leaking underground tanks.
