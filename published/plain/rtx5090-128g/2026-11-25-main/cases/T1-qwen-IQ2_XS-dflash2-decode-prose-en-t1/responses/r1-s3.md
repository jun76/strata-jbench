A city’s water supply system is one of the most complex and invisible pieces of infrastructure that supports modern urban life. While a simple turn of a faucet delivers clear, clean water, this process involves a vast network of engineering, chemistry, biology, and physics. The journey begins far outside the city limits, often in protected natural areas, and ends at the point of use within individual households. This path can be broken down into six critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. To understand how this system functions, we must trace the water through its entire lifecycle, using concrete examples from major cities like New York City, London, and Tokyo, as well as regional systems in places like the Midwest or the Southwestern United States.

### 1. Collection: Source Water and Intake

The first stage is the acquisition of source water. Cities generally rely on one of two primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, or reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers tapped via wells). The choice of source dictates the subsequent treatment processes. For instance, New York City relies primarily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds. These are protected, pristine mountainous areas where water flows down through reservoirs such as the Ashokan Reservoir. Because the source water is exceptionally clean, low-turbidity, and low in microbial load, New York’s system requires minimal treatment, relying mostly on disinfection. In contrast, cities like St. Louis, which rely heavily on the Mississippi River, face turbid water prone to seasonal flooding, sediment buildup, and pollution runoff. This requires robust physical and chemical treatment to remove solids and contaminants.

The physical mechanism of collection depends on the source. For surface water, intake structures are designed to prevent debris, fish, and sediment from entering the system. In Chicago, the intake structures for Lake Michigan are massive pipes located in the harbor, drawing water that is naturally filtered by the lake’s depth and low turbidity, though it still requires treatment for pathogens. For groundwater, collection occurs via well fields. In cities like Phoenix, Arizona, extensive networks of deep wells pump water from underground aquifers, such as the Ogallala Aquifer or the Salt River Basin. The pumping rate and well depth are engineered to prevent land subsidence and to ensure sustainable yield over decades. In many urban areas, the collection point is simply a large water main that draws from a municipal well field, such as those seen in suburban areas of California.
