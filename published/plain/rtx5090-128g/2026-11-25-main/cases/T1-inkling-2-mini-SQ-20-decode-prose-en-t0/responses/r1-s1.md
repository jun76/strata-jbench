A city water supply is best understood as a chain of engineered systems, each designed to move water safely from a natural source to homes, businesses, and public buildings. The process begins with collection, continues through treatment and storage, and ends with distribution through a network of pipes, valves, pumps, and pressure-control devices. Along the way, operators monitor water quality, pressure, flow, and system performance to prevent contamination, shortages, and service interruptions.

The first stage is collection. Cities obtain water from surface sources such as rivers, lakes, reservoirs, or streams, or from groundwater through wells. A surface-water intake may be located in a river where flow is relatively stable and sediment is manageable. For example, a city near a large river might draw water from a protected intake structure that prevents debris, fish, and large floating objects from entering the treatment plant. A reservoir can also serve as a collection and storage point, allowing a city to store water during wet periods and use it during dry weather.

Groundwater collection works differently. Wells are drilled into aquifers, which are underground layers of permeable rock, sand, or gravel that hold water. A well may be shallow or deep depending on the geology. In a city with limited surface water, groundwater may be the primary source. However, groundwater can be affected by nearby land use, such as agriculture, industrial activity, or old contaminated sites, so cities often monitor wells carefully and may use multiple wells to reduce risk.

Once water is collected, it enters the treatment process. The exact treatment steps vary by source and local regulations, but the general goal is to remove or neutralize contaminants. For surface water, treatment often begins with screening or coarse filtration to remove leaves, sticks, trash, and large debris. Then the water may be mixed with chemicals such as coagulants, which help tiny particles clump together. This process is called coagulation and flocculation. After that, the water moves into sedimentation basins, where the clumped particles settle out.

Next comes filtration. Water passes through layers of sand, gravel, or other media that trap smaller particles. This step removes turbidity, which is the cloudiness caused by suspended solids.
