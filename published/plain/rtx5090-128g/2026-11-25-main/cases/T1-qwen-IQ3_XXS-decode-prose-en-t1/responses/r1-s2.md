The journey of clean, potable water from a natural source to the kitchen tap is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critically important engineering feats of modern civilization. It is a chain of custody that involves hydrology, chemistry, civil engineering, computer science, and public health regulations. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the lifecycle of water through its six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This process ensures that every drop reaching a consumer’s faucet is not only available but also safe, consistent, and reliable.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source. Most cities rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). For example, a city like Chicago relies heavily on Lake Michigan, while cities in the American Southwest, such as Phoenix or Las Vegas, depend on Colorado River water or groundwater from the Ogallala aquifer.

Collection begins with an **intake structure**. In the case of surface water, large concrete structures are built near the shoreline to draw water away from the shore where sediment and pollutants might be higher. These intakes often feature trash racks—large metal grids that prevent debris, fish, and large objects from entering the system. The water is then pumped into **raw water mains**.

A critical aspect of collection is seasonal and environmental variability. A river in spring might carry high loads of silt from snowmelt, while a lake in summer might experience algal blooms that affect taste and odor. Groundwater collection involves drilling deep wells and using submersible pumps to lift water from hundreds of feet underground. Groundwater is generally cleaner initially but may contain minerals like iron, manganese, or dissolved salts that require specific treatment.

### 2. Treatment: Transformation into Potable Water

Once collected, the raw water travels through massive pipelines to a **Water Treatment Plant (WTP)**. Treatment is not a single step but a cascading series of physical and chemical processes designed to remove pathogens, turbidity, and chemical contaminants.

For surface water, the process typically follows these steps:

*   **Screening and Settling:** Large screens remove debris. The water enters large sedimentation basins where gravity causes suspended solids and sediments to settle to the bottom. This is often aided by adding coagulants like aluminum sulfate (alum), which bind tiny particles together into larger "flocs" that settle more easily.
