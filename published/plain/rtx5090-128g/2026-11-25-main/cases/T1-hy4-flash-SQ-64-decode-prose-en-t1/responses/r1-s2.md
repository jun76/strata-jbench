**The Hidden Engineering Behind Every Drop: How City Water Travels from Source to Tap**

When you turn on a faucet in your kitchen, the water that flows out seems effortless. Yet behind that simple act lies one of the most elaborate engineered systems in any modern city: a vast network of collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. A metropolitan water supply is not a single pipe or reservoir but an integrated chain of infrastructure, often spanning hundreds of kilometers and involving multiple agencies. To understand how it works, we can follow the journey of a single droplet — from a mountain reservoir to a glass on a kitchen table — using concrete examples drawn from real systems such as New York City, London, Singapore, and a mid-sized city like Portland, Oregon.

---

### 1. Collection: Finding and Gathering the Raw Water

The journey begins at the **source**. Cities rarely draw water from a single place; they use a portfolio of sources to reduce risk. Common sources include:

- **Surface water**: rivers, lakes, and reservoirs  
- **Groundwater**: aquifers accessed via wells  
- **Rainwater harvesting** (in some regions)  
- **Recycled water** (in water-scarce cities like Singapore)

**Example: New York City.** NYC’s water comes primarily from the **Catskill and Delaware watersheds**, located up to 200 km north of the city. The city owns or protects roughly 1,900 km² of land in these watersheds. Nineteen reservoirs and three controlled lakes store about 150 billion liters of water. Water flows by gravity through the **Catskill Aqueduct** and **Delaware Aqueduct** — the latter is 135 km long and tunnels deep beneath the Hudson River. About 90% of NYC’s water is surface water; the remaining 10% comes from groundwater in Queens and Staten Island.

**Example: London.** Thames Water draws roughly 70% of its supply from the **River Thames** and its tributaries, storing water in reservoirs such as Queen Mary Reservoir (near Staines). Groundwater from chalk aquifers supplies the rest.
