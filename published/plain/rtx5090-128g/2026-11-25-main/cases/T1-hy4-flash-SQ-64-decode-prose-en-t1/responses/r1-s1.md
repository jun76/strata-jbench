A city water supply is a engineered system that moves water from a natural source to every household, business, and public tap. Although the exact design varies by city, the basic sequence is the same: **collection → treatment → storage → distribution → pressure management → quality monitoring**. Below is a detailed explanation with concrete examples.

---

## 1. Collection: Getting water from the source

The first step is finding and collecting enough water to serve the city. Cities typically use one or more of the following sources:

### Surface water
Surface water comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. For example, a city like Denver, Colorado, relies heavily on snowmelt from mountain reservoirs. Water is collected in reservoirs, where it is stored until needed. A reservoir acts like a large natural bank: winter snowmelt and spring runoff fill it, then the city releases water during dry summer months.

Rivers are also common sources. A city located beside a river may install **intake structures** that draw water from the river into the treatment system. For instance, a river intake might use screens to keep out fish, leaves, branches, and large debris before the water enters pipes leading to a treatment plant.

### Groundwater
Many cities pump water from underground aquifers. Groundwater is stored in porous rock or sediment layers beneath the surface. Wells are drilled into these layers, and pumps bring the water upward.

For example, a city in Florida might draw water from the Floridian aquifer. In many cases, groundwater is naturally filtered as it passes through soil and rock, but it can still contain minerals such as calcium, magnesium, iron, or arsenic. Therefore, it still needs treatment.

### Recycled or reclaimed water
Some cities also use reclaimed water for non-drinking purposes. This water has already been used once, then treated to a lesser standard and reused for irrigation, industrial cooling, toilet flushing, or street cleaning. For example, cities in arid regions such as Phoenix or Las Vegas use reclaimed water to irrigate golf courses, parks, and landscaped areas. This reduces demand on drinking-water sources.

### Collection infrastructure
The collection stage includes:

- Reservoirs and dams  
- Wells and pump stations  
- River or lake intakes  
- Raw water pipelines  
- Screens and initial filters
