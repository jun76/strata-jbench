A city water supply is a closed, engineered system designed to move water from a natural or purchased source to homes and businesses while reducing risk of contamination and ensuring stable service. It is best understood as a chain of control points: capture, treat, stabilize, store, distribute, regulate pressure, and monitor quality.

1. Source collection
The source may be a river, lake, reservoir, spring, aquifer, or purchased regional supply. For surface water, intake structures draw water from a river or reservoir. These structures may include intake screens, gates, and stilling basins. Screens prevent large debris from entering; gates allow controlled flow during maintenance or high-flow conditions. For groundwater, wells tap aquifers, and pumps draw water upward. In both cases, the goal is to collect enough water for expected demand while minimizing introduction of contaminants or sediment.

Example: A city drawing from a river might place its intake upstream of heavy urban runoff or industrial discharge. A city dependent on groundwater might monitor wells regularly for changes in chemistry, such as increased salinity, nitrate, or dissolved metals.

2. Preliminary treatment
Before advanced treatment, water is often pretreated to remove large solids and begin chemical conditioning. Screening removes branches, leaves, and debris. In some plants, coagulation is introduced: chemicals cause fine suspended particles to clump together into larger “floc.” This makes them easier to remove. Flocculation then gently stirs the water so particles aggregate into larger flocs.

Example: In a river-fed plant, fine silty clay may make the water cloudy. Coagulants help bind those particles so they can be settled or filtered later.

3. Sedimentation or flotation
After coagulation, water moves to a settling basin or clarifier. Here, heavier particles sink to the bottom and are removed as sludge. Some plants use flotation, where air bubbles attach to lighter contaminants and lift them to the surface for removal. Sedimentation is common in conventional surface-water treatment because it is simple and effective for removing turbidity.
