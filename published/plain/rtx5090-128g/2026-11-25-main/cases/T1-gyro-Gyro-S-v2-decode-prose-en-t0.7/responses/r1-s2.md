A city water supply is a complex, multi-stage engineering marvel that transforms raw, often contaminated environmental water into a safe, potable commodity delivered to household taps. This process involves a continuous loop of collection, purification, storage, distribution, and quality control. To understand how this works, we must follow the journey of a single drop of water from its origin in nature to its destination in your kitchen sink.

### 1. Collection: The Source
The journey begins with the identification of reliable water sources. Cities typically rely on three main types of sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs), groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells), or desalinated seawater. For most large metropolitan areas, surface water and groundwater are the primary sources.

**Concrete Example:** Consider a city like Chicago. Its primary source is Lake Michigan, a massive surface water body. Alternatively, a city like Phoenix, Arizona, relies heavily on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers beneath the desert. In coastal cities like Tel Aviv, desalination plants convert seawater into fresh water. The collection process involves intake structures. For surface water, this might be a submerged pipe or a pumping station located near the shoreline. For groundwater, wells are drilled hundreds of feet into the earth, and pumps draw the water up. At this stage, the water is raw and untreated. It may contain sediment, organic matter, bacteria, viruses, chemicals from runoff, and dissolved minerals.

### 2. Treatment: Purification
Once collected, the water is transported to a water treatment plant. This is where the magic happens. The goal is to remove contaminants, pathogens, and unwanted chemicals while retaining beneficial minerals. The treatment process is highly regulated and follows a specific sequence.

**Step-by-Step Treatment:**
1.  **Screening and Coagulation:** Water first passes through coarse screens to remove large debris like leaves, branches, and plastics. Then, chemicals called coagulants (such as aluminum sulfate) are added. These chemicals cause tiny particles of dirt and organic matter to clump together into larger flakes called "flocs."
2.  **Flocculation and Sedimentation:** The water is gently stirred in large basins. As the flocs grow, they become heavy enough to sink to the bottom of the basin due to gravity. This process removes suspended solids, significantly clarifying the water.
3.  **Filtration:** The clearer water flows through filters, usually layers of sand, gravel, and activated carbon
