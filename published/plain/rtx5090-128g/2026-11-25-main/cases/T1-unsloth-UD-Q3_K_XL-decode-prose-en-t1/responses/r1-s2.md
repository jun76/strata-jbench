The journey of a glass of water from a natural source to a kitchen tap is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical feats of modern civil engineering. It is not a simple flow of liquid but a meticulously managed industrial process involving hydraulic physics, chemical engineering, microbiology, and sophisticated data analytics. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the lifecycle of water through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is designed to ensure that water is not only available in sufficient quantities but is also safe for human consumption, aesthetically pleasing, and delivered reliably to millions of people simultaneously.

The process begins with **collection**, where raw water is extracted from a natural source. Cities typically rely on one of three primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs), groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells), or a hybrid of both. For example, New York City sources much of its water from upstate reservoirs like the Ashokan and Catskill systems, relying on gravity to transport water hundreds of miles. In contrast, cities in arid regions like Phoenix, Arizona, rely heavily on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers and imported via massive canal systems like the Central Arizona Project. The choice of source dictates the subsequent treatment needs. Surface water is susceptible to seasonal changes, leaf litter, and agricultural runoff, requiring robust removal of suspended solids. Groundwater, while generally clearer, may contain high levels of minerals like iron, manganese, or naturally occurring arsenic, necessitating different treatment strategies. The collection point is often guarded by intake screens to prevent fish and debris from entering the pipeline, marking the official transition from the natural environment to the municipal infrastructure.

Once collected, the water enters the **treatment** phase, a multi-step purification process that transforms raw, often turbid water into potable water. This is the heart of the water supply system. The first step is usually coagulation and flocculation. Chemicals called coagulants, such as aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride, are added to the water and mixed vigorously. These chemicals neutralize the negative electrical charges of dirt and other suspended particles, causing them to stick together. In a sedimentation basin, the water then flows slowly, allowing the clumps of dirt, now called "floc," to settle to the bottom due to gravity.
