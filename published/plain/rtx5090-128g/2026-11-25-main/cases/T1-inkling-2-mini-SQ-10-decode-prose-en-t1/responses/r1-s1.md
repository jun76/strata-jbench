I can explain the general systems, but I can’t invent a specific city’s source, treatment plant, or tap-level results. Below is a concrete, realistic example using a generic urban supply, clearly labeled as an illustrative case rather than a real city.

A city water supply works as a chain: raw water is collected, conditioned, stored, pumped through pressure zones, delivered to taps, and continuously tested. The source determines what has to be removed; treatment determines whether the water is safe to drink; storage and pressure management determine whether it reaches buildings reliably; and monitoring determines whether the system stays compliant.

**1. Collection: source and intake**

In the illustrative case, the city draws from a river-fed reservoir in a nearby basin. The reservoir is not “potable” by itself—it carries sediment, algae, bacteria, road runoff, and occasional pollutants. The intake structure sits low in the river or reservoir and uses a screened intake to keep large fish and debris out while drawing water into a protected pipeline.

Collection starts with a raw-water intake. A skimming or intake weir measures how much water is entering. If the city uses groundwater as a backup, an adjacent well could also supply water when the river intake is restricted. The city’s engineers choose the source based on capacity, reliability, and quality. A river may give large flow but require more treatment; groundwater may be cleaner but smaller in scale and vulnerable to local contamination.

A concrete example: a city with an average demand of 80,000 cubic meters per day (about 800,000 people equivalent) might draw 90,000 cubic meters per day from the reservoir, leaving 10,000 cubic meters per day for drought or emergency reserve. If the intake is contaminated—say, after a heavy rain with fertilizer runoff—the city can close that intake and switch to the reserve well.

**2. Treatment: removal of sediment, organisms, and contaminants**

Raw water typically passes through primary treatment. In the illustrative plant, it enters a basin where large solids are settled or removed. Then the water is dosed with a coagulant such as alum or ferric chloride, which helps tiny particles clump together.
