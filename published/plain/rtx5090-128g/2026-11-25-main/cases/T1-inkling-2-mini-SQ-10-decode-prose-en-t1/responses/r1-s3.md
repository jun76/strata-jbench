I can explain the general process, but I can’t provide operational details for an actual city’s water supply that could be used to interfere with or damage it. Here is a detailed, non-specific explanation from source to tap.

Collection begins at a source. Rivers and reservoirs are tapped through intake structures, while groundwater is drawn from wells. Concrete intake walls, screens, weirs, and galleries keep fish, debris, and sediment out of the pipe. In a river-fed system, pumps draw water continuously even when the flow is low; in a reservoir-fed system, storage provides buffering against drought. A city near a large river might collect at a regulated intake near the dam, while a city in a dry region may rely on a network of deep wells.

Treatment follows several stages. In the simplest systems, the water is aerated or mixed with flocculants so that suspended particles form larger particles. The treated water then passes through a clarifier or settling basin, where the particles settle out. Filters—sand, gravel, or sometimes anthracite—remove finer material and turbidity. A disinfectant, often chlorine, is added to kill microorganisms; fluoride may also be added in some places to strengthen tooth enamel. In more complex systems, chemical dosing is followed by activated carbon, membrane filtration, or other polishing steps for odor control and contaminant reduction.

The treated water is stored in tanks. A distribution system depends on buffers: elevated storage towers, concrete reservoirs, and underground chambers. These reservoirs equalize supply when the pumps are off and give a backup when a leak occurs. In concrete terms, a reservoir may hold hundreds or thousands of cubic meters, and a small municipal tower may hold only a few hundred. The storage capacity is selected so the city can withstand a multi-day outage or maintenance event.

Distribution is then controlled by a network of pipes. Trunk mains carry water out from the treatment plant, and smaller branches feed neighborhoods. A looped network—where multiple routes connect a neighborhood—is more resilient than a simple line layout. A city with a dense older grid may have more pressure fluctuations and more leakage than a newer city with larger, better-connected mains. Pressure is maintained through pump stations, pressure-reducing valves, and regulated storage.
