The journey of a single drop of water from a distant reservoir to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical engineering feats in modern civilization. It is a silent, continuous miracle that most people take for granted, yet it involves a sophisticated interplay of civil engineering, chemistry, physics, and rigorous regulatory oversight. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of water through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring. Each stage is designed to ensure that the water not only reaches the consumer but does so safely, reliably, and at the correct pressure.

### Stage 1: Collection and Intake

The process begins at the source. Depending on the geography of the city, water is collected from either surface water sources (rivers, lakes, or reservoirs) or groundwater sources (aquifers accessed via wells). For the sake of this explanation, let us consider a typical mid-sized city, which we will call "Metropolis," that relies primarily on a large surface reservoir located in a nearby mountain range.

The collection process starts at the intake structure. This is often a submerged pipe or a series of screens located deep within the reservoir. The depth is crucial; by drawing water from deeper layers, the utility avoids surface debris, algae blooms, and temperature fluctuations that can affect taste and odor. In Metropolis, the intake is protected by coarse screens that prevent large objects like logs or fish from entering the system. From here, raw water flows into a massive conduit, often a large-diameter pipe or an open channel, which transports it by gravity or pumping to the treatment plant.

In some cities, such as those in arid regions like Phoenix or Las Vegas, collection might involve a mix of local groundwater and imported water from distant rivers, such as the Colorado River. In these cases, the collection phase is heavily dependent on energy-intensive pumping stations that lift water hundreds of feet to overcome elevation differences. Regardless of the source, the water arriving at the treatment plant is considered "raw water." It contains suspended solids, organic matter, bacteria, viruses, and potentially dissolved minerals. It is not yet safe for human consumption.
