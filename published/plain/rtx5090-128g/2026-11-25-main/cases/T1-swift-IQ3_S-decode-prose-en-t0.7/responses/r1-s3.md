The journey of a glass of water from a distant reservoir to a kitchen tap is a marvel of modern engineering, public health science, and logistical coordination. It is a continuous, high-volume process that operates silently in the background of urban life, ensuring that millions of residents have access to clean, pressurized water 24 hours a day. To understand this system, we must trace the path through six critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

### Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which varies by geography. Most major cities rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). For example, New York City draws a significant portion of its water from the Catskill and Delaware reservoirs, massive man-made lakes in upstate New York. Los Angeles, situated in a semi-arid region, relies on a complex network of imported water from the Colorado River and the Sierra Nevada mountains via aqueducts.

The collection phase involves intake structures designed to draw water without harming the ecosystem. In surface water systems, intake towers extend deep into the reservoir to draw water from cooler, oxygen-rich layers, avoiding surface algae blooms and warmer, less oxygenated top layers. These towers are fitted with trash racks—large screens that prevent fish, logs, and debris from entering the pipes. A critical component here is the screening technology. Modern plants use traveling screens that rotate continuously, capturing debris and lifting it out of the water before it enters the main trunk lines. This initial filtration is vital; a single leaf or fish can clog downstream pumps, causing costly shutdowns.

### Treatment: The Science of Purification

Once collected, the raw water travels through massive transmission mains to a water treatment plant. Here, it undergoes a rigorous purification process designed to remove pathogens, particulates, and dissolved contaminants. The standard sequence for surface water treatment involves coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection.

**Coagulation and Flocculation:** Raw water often contains suspended particles that are too small to settle naturally. Chemicals called coagulants, typically aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride, are added. These chemicals neutralize the negative electrical charges of the particles, allowing them to clump together.
