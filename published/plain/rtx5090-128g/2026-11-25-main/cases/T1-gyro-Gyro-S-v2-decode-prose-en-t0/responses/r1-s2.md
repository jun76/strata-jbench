A city water supply is one of the most complex and critical infrastructure systems in modern civilization. It is a continuous, multi-stage process that transforms raw, often contaminated water from natural sources into safe, potable water delivered to millions of households, businesses, and institutions. This journey involves engineering, chemistry, biology, and rigorous quality control. To understand how water gets from a river or aquifer to your kitchen tap, we must examine the entire lifecycle: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Supply

The journey begins at the source. Cities typically rely on two primary types of water sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers).

**Surface Water Collection:**
For cities like New York or London, surface water is the dominant source. Water is drawn from large reservoirs or rivers via intake structures. These intakes are designed to minimize the entry of debris, fish, and sediment. For example, the New York City water system draws water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds. The water travels through massive aqueducts—tunnels and pipelines—over hundreds of miles to reach the city. In some cases, gravity alone moves the water, eliminating the need for pumping and saving energy. However, in other systems, such as those relying on the Colorado River for Las Vegas, pumping stations are essential to lift water from lower elevations to higher ones.

**Groundwater Collection:**
In regions where surface water is scarce or unreliable, groundwater is extracted from underground aquifers. This is done using deep wells equipped with pumps. For instance, many cities in the American Southwest, such as Phoenix, Arizona, rely heavily on groundwater. The water is pumped from depths ranging from 100 to 1,000 feet. Groundwater is often cleaner than surface water because it has been naturally filtered through layers of soil and rock, but it can contain dissolved minerals like calcium, magnesium, and iron, which require specific treatment steps.
