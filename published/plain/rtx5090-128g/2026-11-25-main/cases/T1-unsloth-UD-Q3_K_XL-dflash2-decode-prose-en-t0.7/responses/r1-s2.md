The journey of water from a distant natural source to the kitchen faucet is one of the most complex, critical, and largely invisible engineering feats of modern civilization. While turning a handle yields an immediate, clean stream of water, this convenience masks a sprawling infrastructure network that operates 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. This system must ensure not only the physical availability of water but also its safety, consistent pressure, and reliability. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the water’s path through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and finally, the complex management of pressure and quality that ensures the water remains safe until the moment it is consumed.

The process begins with **collection**, where water is sourced from either surface water bodies or groundwater aquifers. The choice of source depends heavily on the geography and climate of the city. For instance, a city like New York relies heavily on a vast system of reservoirs and lakes, such as the Ashokan Reservoir, which collects runoff from the Catskill Mountains. In contrast, cities in arid regions like Phoenix or Las Vegas may rely more heavily on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers or imported via massive pipelines from distant rivers like the Colorado. Collection is not merely about taking water; it involves protecting the watershed. This means managing land use to prevent runoff containing pesticides, fertilizers, or sewage from entering the source. Intake structures, often located far from urban centers, are equipped with screens to prevent large debris, fish, and other aquatic organisms from entering the pipeline. These intakes are designed to draw water from specific depths where the temperature and quality are most consistent, avoiding the warmer, oxygen-rich surface layers that might encourage algal blooms or the colder, stagnant bottom layers that may contain dissolved sediments.

Once collected, the raw water enters the **treatment** phase, which is designed to remove physical, chemical, and biological contaminants. This process varies depending on the quality of the source water but generally follows a standard sequence of steps. The first step is typically screening and grit removal, where trash, leaves, and sand are filtered out. Next, the water undergoes coagulation and flocculation. Chemicals such as aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride are added to the water. These coagulants neutralize the negative electrical charges of suspended particles, allowing them to clump together into larger particles called "flocs."
