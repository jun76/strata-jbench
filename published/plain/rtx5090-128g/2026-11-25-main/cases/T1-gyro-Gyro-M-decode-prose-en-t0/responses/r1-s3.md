The journey of a drop of water from a distant reservoir to the kitchen tap is one of the most complex, critical, and invisible engineering feats in modern civilization. While we often take the act of turning a faucet for granted, the system behind it involves a sophisticated chain of physical infrastructure, chemical processes, and regulatory oversight. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring. Each stage is designed to ensure that the water delivered is not only available but also safe, pressurized, and consistent.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can vary significantly depending on the geography and climate of the city. Common sources include surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are protected forested areas that feed into reservoirs. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely on a mix of surface water from the Salt River and groundwater pumped from deep aquifers.

Once the water is identified, it must be collected. For surface water, this involves intake structures located at the edge of a reservoir or river. These intakes are designed to draw water from specific depths to avoid surface debris or sediment. Concrete examples include the intake towers at the Lake Mead reservoir, which draw water from different elevations depending on the water level and quality. For groundwater, collection involves drilling wells that penetrate layers of soil and rock to reach the water table. Pumps are installed at the bottom of these wells to lift the water to the surface.

A critical aspect of collection is the protection of the source. Cities often establish "source water protection plans" to prevent contamination from agricultural runoff, industrial waste, or urban sewage. For instance, the city of Seattle has strict regulations regarding the use of pesticides and fertilizers in the watershed areas that feed its drinking water supply. This proactive approach reduces the burden on the treatment plant later in the process.
