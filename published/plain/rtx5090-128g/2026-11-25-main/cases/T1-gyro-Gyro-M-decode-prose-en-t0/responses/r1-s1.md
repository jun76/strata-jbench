The journey of a single drop of water from a distant reservoir to the kitchen faucet is one of the most complex, engineered, and critical systems in modern civilization. While it may seem mundane to turn a handle and receive clean water instantly, this process involves a sophisticated chain of physical infrastructure, chemical engineering, hydraulic physics, and rigorous regulatory oversight. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring. Each stage is designed to ensure that the water is not only safe for human consumption but also delivered with sufficient pressure and reliability to millions of users simultaneously.

### Stage 1: Collection and Source Acquisition

The process begins at the source. Cities typically rely on two primary types of water sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The choice of source depends heavily on geography, climate, and population density. For example, New York City relies primarily on the Catskill and Delaware aqueduct systems, which transport water from reservoirs located hundreds of miles away. In contrast, many cities in the American Southwest, such as Phoenix, Arizona, rely heavily on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers or diverted from the Colorado River.

Collection involves more than just drawing water; it requires careful management of the watershed. Surface water is collected via intake structures, which are often screened to prevent debris, fish, and large particulates from entering the treatment plant. These intakes are strategically placed to avoid areas with high turbidity or pollution. Groundwater collection involves drilling wells that penetrate layers of soil and rock to reach the water table. In cities like Los Angeles, a mix of imported surface water from the Sierra Nevada mountains and local groundwater is used to meet demand.

A critical aspect of collection is the protection of the source. Contamination from agricultural runoff, industrial discharge, or urban sewage can compromise the raw water quality. Therefore, municipalities often establish "source water protection plans" that regulate land use around reservoirs and aquifers. For instance, the city of Seattle protects its Lake Washington and Lake Union sources by managing stormwater runoff and limiting development near the shoreline. This proactive approach reduces the burden on the treatment plant, as cleaner source water requires less intensive processing.
