A city water supply is a complex engineering feat that transforms raw, potentially hazardous natural resources into safe, potable water delivered through a network of pipes, valves, and pumps. To understand how water travels from a remote source to your kitchen tap, we must examine the journey in stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is critical to ensuring reliability, safety, and efficiency.

### 1. Collection: The Source
The journey begins at the source, which typically falls into two categories: surface water (lakes, rivers, reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers). For many major cities, surface water is the primary source because it is easier to access in large volumes. For example, New York City relies heavily on reservoirs in the Catskill Mountains, while Los Angeles draws water from the Colorado River via aqueducts. Groundwater sources, like the Ogallala Aquifer used in parts of the Midwest, require drilling wells into underground rock formations.

Collection involves intake structures. In surface water systems, intake pipes extend into the lake or river. These intakes often have screens to prevent debris, fish, and large particles from entering the system. In groundwater systems, pumps draw water from boreholes. The choice of source depends on local geography, climate, and demand. A critical factor here is seasonal variation; droughts can reduce surface water levels, forcing utilities to rely more on groundwater or imported sources.

### 2. Treatment: Purification and Conditioning
Raw water is rarely safe to drink directly. It may contain bacteria, viruses, parasites, sediments, organic matter, and chemical contaminants. The treatment process is designed to remove these hazards. The standard sequence involves several steps:

**Coagulation and Flocculation:** Chemicals like aluminum sulfate (alum) are added to the water. These chemicals bind with suspended particles and organic matter, forming larger clumps called "flocs." This makes the particles easier to remove.

**Sedimentation:** The water flows into large basins where the flocs settle to the bottom due to gravity. The clear water on top is skimmed off, while the sediment is removed periodically.

**Filtration:** The water passes through layers of sand, gravel, or activated carbon. This removes smaller particles that didn’t settle during sedimentation. Carbon filters are particularly effective at removing taste and odor compounds, as well as certain organic chemicals.
