City water supply systems move water from a source to buildings and homes through several coordinated stages. The exact route depends on the city, but the basic logic is the same: find enough clean water, make it safe to drink, hold it in storage, move it through pipes, maintain usable pressure, and test it regularly.

A common example is a city that draws from a river or reservoir. Imagine a medium-sized city near a river. The intake structure sits in the river or a nearby reservoir. Water is filtered or strained so that large debris—leaves, branches, trash, and fish—does not enter the plant. From there, the water is pumped toward a treatment facility. In other cities, the source may be a groundwater well. A groundwater system may be simpler because the soil already acts as a natural filter, but it is still vulnerable to pollution and must be monitored carefully.

Once water reaches the treatment plant, the city removes contaminants. In a surface-water plant, the process often begins with coagulation and flocculation. Chemicals such as alum or ferric chloride are added, causing tiny particles and clumps of dirt to coalesce into larger, heavier flocs. These flocs can be removed more easily. The water then passes through sedimentation, where it sits in basins long enough for solids to settle to the bottom. In some plants, rapid mixers and flocculators are used before sedimentation, allowing the chemistry and physics to work together quickly.

After sedimentation, water moves through filtration. Many cities use sand, gravel, or anthracite coal-based filtration beds. As water passes through these layers, remaining particles are trapped in the filter material. Some plants use membrane filtration instead, such as reverse osmosis or microfiltration. Filtration is critical because drinking water must be free from visible cloudiness and small-scale contaminants that could carry disease.

Disinfection is usually the final major step. Chlorine is one of the most common disinfectants because it is effective against bacteria and viruses, inexpensive, and relatively easy to dose. Ozone and ultraviolet light are also used in some systems. Ozone can break down organic matter and disinfect water, while ultraviolet light inactivates microorganisms without adding a persistent chemical residue. Some cities use a combination, such as chlorine plus ultraviolet light, to improve safety and reduce taste or odor
