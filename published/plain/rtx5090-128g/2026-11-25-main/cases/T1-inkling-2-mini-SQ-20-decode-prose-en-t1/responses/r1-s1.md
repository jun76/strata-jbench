A city water supply system moves water from a source to homes, businesses, and public buildings through a sequence of processes. The exact steps vary by region, but the overall path is: source → collection → pre-treatment → treatment → storage → pumping and pressure management → distribution network → customer tap.

### 1. Source and collection

Water can come from surface sources, such as rivers, lakes, reservoirs, or wetlands, or from groundwater sources, such as wells tapping aquifers. Surface water is often collected through intakes that draw water into a treatment plant. Groundwater is typically pumped from wells into a supply system.

For example, a city beside a river may build a dam or reservoir to create a large storage area and ensure a steadier flow. A city in a dry region may rely on deep wells instead. Municipalities also identify multiple sources when possible, because using only one source can make the system vulnerable to drought, contamination, or infrastructure failure.

Collection points must be located away from likely contamination sources such as wastewater outfalls, industrial sites, or heavy sediment-generating areas. In practice, source protection is a major part of public health planning: local authorities may regulate land use, monitor runoff, or restrict development near water intakes.

### 2. Pre-treatment

Before serious chemical or physical treatment, water often goes through pre-treatment. Its goal is to remove large debris and reduce the burden on later processes.

Common pre-treatment steps include:

- Screening, to catch leaves, branches, and trash.
- Grit removal, to settle or remove sand and dense particles.
- Coagulation or flocculation, where chemicals cause small particles to clump together.
- Sedimentation, where heavier particles settle out.
- Filtration, where the water passes through media such as sand or activated carbon.

For example, river water entering a plant may pass through screens to remove sticks and debris, then into a coagulation tank where aluminum sulfate or another coagulant helps suspended particles form larger clusters. These clusters settle into a sludge layer, while clearer water moves onward.
