A city water supply is a vast, interconnected engineered system that moves water from a natural source—often tens or hundreds of miles away—into homes, businesses, hospitals, and fire hydrants. Although the water that emerges from a tap may seem simple, delivering it safely and reliably requires careful work at every stage: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. A useful way to understand the system is to follow one hypothetical drop of water from a river or reservoir to a kitchen sink.

## 1. Source and collection: where the water comes from

The first step is securing a dependable source. Cities typically use one or more of the following:

- **Surface water**: rivers, lakes, and reservoirs  
- **Groundwater**: wells and aquifers  
- **Imported water**: water transported from another watershed through canals or pipelines  
- **Desalinated water**: seawater or brackish water treated to remove salt, common in arid coastal cities

Many large cities rely on surface water because it can supply large volumes. New York City, for example, receives much of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located well outside the city. Los Angeles imports water from distant sources such as the Colorado River and Northern California through large aqueducts. Coastal cities such as San Diego have invested in desalination to diversify supply.

For surface water systems, collection often begins at a reservoir or river intake. An **intake structure** draws water from a selected depth, because water quality can vary depending on depth, season, and runoff conditions. Screens at the intake remove large debris such as leaves, branches, fish, and trash. The water then enters a raw water pipeline or canal that carries it to a treatment plant.

Groundwater systems work differently. Instead of drawing from a river or lake, the city drills wells into underground aquifers. Pumps bring water to the surface. Groundwater often has fewer visible impurities than surface water and may naturally filter through soil and rock, but it can contain dissolved minerals such as iron, manganese, arsenic, or nitrates from agricultural runoff.

Collection also involves water rights, environmental regulation, and watershed protection. A city cannot simply take unlimited water. It must balance drinking water needs with agriculture, industry, ecosystems, and drought planning. For that reason, many utilities maintain multiple sources and long-term supply plans.
