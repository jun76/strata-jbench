A modern city water supply is a vast, engineered system that moves water from a natural source to millions of individual taps while keeping it safe, reliable, and at the right pressure. Although the exact design varies from city to city, most systems follow the same basic sequence: **source water collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring**. Each stage depends on the others. A failure at the source can affect treatment; a treatment problem can affect storage; a pressure issue can affect delivery; and a monitoring failure can allow contamination to go unnoticed.

Below is a detailed explanation of how a city water supply works, using concrete examples throughout.

---

## 1. Source Water Collection: Where the Water Comes From

The first step is obtaining raw water from a source. Cities typically use one or more of the following:

- **Rivers and streams**
- **Lakes and reservoirs**
- **Groundwater aquifers**
- **Imported water from distant watersheds**
- **Desalinated seawater**, in some coastal or arid regions

The choice of source depends on geography, climate, population size, cost, and legal water rights.

### Surface Water Sources

Many large cities rely on rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. For example, New York City gets much of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located roughly 100 to 200 miles away from the city. These watersheds include reservoirs such as the Ashokan Reservoir and the Cannonsville Reservoir. Rain and snowmelt collect in the watershed, flow into streams, and are stored in reservoirs before being sent to treatment plants or, in some cases, directly into the distribution system if the water quality is exceptionally high.

London, by contrast, historically relied heavily on the River Thames and its tributaries. Water is abstracted from the river, treated, and distributed to homes and businesses. Because river water can contain sewage discharges, agricultural runoff, industrial pollutants, and wildlife waste, it usually requires more intensive treatment than protected reservoir water.

### Groundwater Sources

Other cities depend on underground aquifers. Groundwater is stored in porous rock, sand, or gravel layers beneath the surface. It is accessed through wells. Cities such as Phoenix, Arizona, use groundwater as an important supply, although the region also relies on imported Colorado River water and reclaimed water.
