The journey of water from a natural source to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical engineering feats in modern society. It is not merely a matter of opening a valve; it is a sophisticated system involving hydrology, chemistry, mechanical engineering, and public health policy. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring. Each stage relies on concrete infrastructure and specific operational protocols to ensure that the water delivered is safe, reliable, and consistent.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, population size, and environmental regulations. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are protected forests that feed reservoirs like the Ashokan Reservoir. In contrast, many cities in the American Southwest, such as Phoenix, Arizona, rely on the Colorado River, requiring massive pumping infrastructure to move water hundreds of miles uphill.

The physical collection involves intake structures. For surface water, this often means a dam or a reservoir. The water is drawn from the deepest part of the reservoir, where the temperature is stable and sediment has settled. This is crucial because drawing water from the surface can introduce algae, debris, and seasonal temperature fluctuations that complicate treatment. For groundwater, collection involves wells. A well is drilled into an aquifer, and pumps draw water up to the surface. Groundwater is generally cleaner than surface water because the earth acts as a natural filter, but it can contain high levels of minerals like iron or manganese, which require specific treatment.

Concrete example: In Chicago, the intake for Lake Michigan water is located at the South Shore Water Treatment Plant. The water is drawn through large pipes that extend deep into the lake. These intakes are designed to avoid drawing in sediment from the bottom or surface contaminants. The sheer volume of water required for a city of millions necessitates massive infrastructure; the pipes are often several feet in diameter, capable of moving millions of gallons per hour.
