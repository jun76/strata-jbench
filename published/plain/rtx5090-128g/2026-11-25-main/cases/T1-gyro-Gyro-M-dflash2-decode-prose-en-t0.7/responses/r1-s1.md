The journey of water from a distant mountain reservoir to the kitchen faucet in a modern city is one of the most complex, critical, and invisible engineering feats in human civilization. It is a system that operates continuously, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, balancing massive volumes of liquid against strict safety standards, varying demand patterns, and the physical laws of gravity and pressure. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the flow through five distinct stages: collection (source intake), treatment (purification), storage (reservoirs and tanks), distribution (pipes and pumping), and finally, monitoring and quality assurance at the point of use.

### 1. Collection and Source Intake

The process begins at the source. Cities generally rely on two types of sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice depends on geography, climate, and local geology. For example, New York City primarily relies on the Catskill and Delaware aqueduct systems, which transport water via gravity from reservoirs in the mountains, while Los Angeles combines imported surface water from the Colorado River and Owens Valley with local groundwater.

**Gravity-Driven Intake:**
In many cases, the source is located at a higher elevation than the city. This allows water to be collected and transported using gravity alone, saving massive amounts of energy. A prime example is the Aqueducts of Rome, which were ancient marvels, but modern equivalents like the Catskill Aqueduct in New York still use this principle. Water is drawn from reservoirs through large intake towers. These towers are designed to draw water from specific depths to avoid sediment layers at the bottom or floating debris at the surface. From the intake tower, water enters massive concrete or steel pipes—some exceeding 10 feet in diameter—that carry it over dozens or even hundreds of miles to treatment facilities.

**Groundwater Extraction:**
If a city relies on groundwater, it uses wells. These are not the small hand-dug wells of rural areas but industrial-scale boreholes drilled hundreds of feet into the earth, tapping into porous rock layers called aquifers. Pumps at the surface draw water up, often requiring significant energy because the water table may be deep. Cities like Phoenix or Tucson, which are heavily reliant on groundwater, must manage extraction rates carefully to prevent the aquifer from depleting or the ground from subsiding (sinking).
