The journey of a city’s water supply is a marvel of modern engineering, public health science, and municipal infrastructure. It is a complex, multi-stage process that transforms raw, often contaminated water from a natural source into the clean, pressurized water that flows from a residential tap. This system is not merely a series of pipes; it is a carefully orchestrated network designed to ensure safety, consistency, and reliability for millions of users. To understand how this works, we must trace the water’s path through five critical phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring.

### Phase 1: Collection and Intake

The process begins at the source. Most cities rely on one of two primary sources: surface water (lakes, rivers, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The choice of source dictates the initial challenges the utility faces. For example, a city like New York draws primarily from reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware systems, where the water is relatively clean due to strict watershed protection laws. In contrast, a city like Los Angeles, which relies on imported surface water from the Colorado River and local reservoirs, deals with higher turbidity and sediment loads.

The intake process involves drawing water from the source into the treatment plant. For surface water, this is done through massive intake structures, such as the intake towers at the Las Vegas Water Treatment Plant or the screen intakes used by the City of Chicago on Lake Michigan. These intakes are critical because they prevent large debris—like logs, plastic, or aquatic animals—from entering the system. Large screens or trash racks are installed at the intake points to filter out this debris. If the source is groundwater, wells are drilled deep into the earth to access aquifers. These wells must be carefully managed to prevent contamination from surface runoff or nearby industrial activities.

At this stage, raw water is not yet safe for consumption. It may contain algae, sediment, bacteria, viruses, parasites (such as *Cryptosporidium* or *Giardia*), and chemical contaminants. The goal of the next phase is to remove these hazards.
