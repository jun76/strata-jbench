A city water supply is a chain of systems that moves water from a natural or engineered source to the tap in a home, school, hospital, or business. The chain usually includes **collection**, **treatment**, **storage**, **distribution**, **pressure management**, and **quality monitoring**. Each stage is designed to make water safe, reliable, and available at the right pressure. A useful way to understand the system is to follow one drop of water through a typical city.

Imagine a mid-sized city called Riverton, with about 300,000 residents. Riverton gets much of its water from a river and a reservoir upstream, with groundwater wells as a backup during droughts. On a normal day, the city draws about 45 million liters of raw water. That water may come from a river that receives rainfall and snowmelt from a watershed, or from a reservoir that stores seasonal runoff. Groundwater sources, such as aquifers, are also common in many cities. For example, a city like Minneapolis uses groundwater as a major source, while cities such as New York City rely heavily on protected reservoir systems. Coastal cities may also use desalination, as places like Singapore or parts of California do.

The first stage is **collection**. Surface water is collected from rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. A city may operate intake structures that draw water from a river or reservoir. These intakes often have screens to keep out fish, leaves, branches, and large debris. In Riverton, the river intake is located upstream of the city, where the water is less affected by urban runoff. The city also has a reservoir that captures water during wet months and releases it during dry months. Reservoirs act like natural banks of water storage: they collect rainfall and snowmelt, then release water slowly through controlled outlets. Groundwater collection uses wells. Wells pump water from underground aquifers, which are layers of porous rock or sediment that hold water. A city may test wells regularly because groundwater can contain minerals such as iron, manganese, calcium, or arsenic, depending on the local geology.

Once water is collected, it is sent to a **treatment plant**. Treatment is necessary because raw water can contain dirt, organic matter, bacteria, viruses, parasites, chemicals, and unpleasant tastes or odors. The exact treatment process depends on the source. Surface water usually needs more treatment than groundwater because it is more exposed to pollution.
