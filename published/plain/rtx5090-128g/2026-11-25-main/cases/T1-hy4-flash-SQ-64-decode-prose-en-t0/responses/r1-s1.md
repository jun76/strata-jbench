A city water supply is a chain of systems that moves water from a natural or engineered source to the tap in a home, school, hospital, or business. The chain usually includes **source water collection**, **treatment**, **storage**, **distribution**, **pressure management**, and **quality monitoring**. Each stage is designed to make water safe, reliable, and available at the right pressure. A useful way to understand the system is to follow one drop of water through a typical city.

For example, imagine a city of 500,000 people that draws water from a river and an underground aquifer. The river supplies most of the water during wet months, while groundwater provides backup during droughts. The city’s water utility operates intake structures, a treatment plant, reservoirs, pumping stations, miles of pipes, and a laboratory that tests water quality. The goal is not simply to move water, but to deliver water that meets health standards at every tap.

## 1. Source water collection

The first step is finding and collecting raw water. Cities use several kinds of sources: rivers, lakes, reservoirs, springs, wells, and sometimes recycled or desalinated water.

### Surface water sources

Many large cities rely on rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. A river intake might be a structure on the riverbank with screens that keep out leaves, fish, and large debris. Water is pumped or diverted into a channel leading to the treatment plant.

For example, a city on a river may have an intake tower with bar screens and traveling screens. The screens trap sticks, plastic, and aquatic life. The water then flows into a raw water pumping station, where large pumps lift it into the treatment plant. If the river is muddy after heavy rain, the utility may adjust the intake depth or temporarily rely more on reservoir water to avoid high turbidity.

Reservoirs are artificial lakes created by dams. They store runoff from a watershed, which is the land area that drains into the reservoir. A city such as New York City, for instance, gets much of its water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds. Protecting the watershed reduces pollution and can lower treatment costs. In such systems, water may travel many miles through tunnels and aqueducts before reaching a treatment plant.
