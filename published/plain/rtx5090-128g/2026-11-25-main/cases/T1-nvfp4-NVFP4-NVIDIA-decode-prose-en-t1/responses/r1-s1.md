The journey of drinking water from a remote natural source to the tap in a modern urban home is one of the most complex, critical, and often invisible feats of modern civil engineering. It is a process that blends hydrology, chemistry, biology, and massive-scale logistics to ensure public health. This system, often referred to as the municipal water supply, operates as a continuous, pressurized network that must function reliably 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. To understand this system, we must trace the path of a single drop of water through its various stages: source collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and final quality monitoring.

The process begins at the source, which is typically one of two types: surface water or groundwater. In many large metropolitan areas, such as New York City or parts of Los Angeles, surface water is the primary source. This water is collected from reservoirs, lakes, or rivers that are usually located far from the city center to protect them from urban pollution. For example, the Catskill and Delaware Aqueduct systems supply water to New York City by gravity, transporting water over 200 miles through mountains and valleys without the need for pumps. This reliance on gravity is a hallmark of older, well-engineered systems, as it reduces energy costs and operational complexity. In contrast, cities in arid regions, such as Las Vegas or Phoenix, may rely heavily on groundwater extracted from deep aquifers via electric pumps. In coastal cities like Singapore, desalination plants reverse the natural osmosis process to remove salt from seawater, providing a drought-resistant supply. Regardless of the origin, the raw water entering the treatment plant is never clean; it contains sediment, organic matter, bacteria, viruses, and sometimes industrial pollutants.

Once collected, the raw water enters the treatment facility, where it undergoes a rigorous multi-stage purification process. The first stage is usually preliminary screening, where large debris like leaves, sticks, and trash are removed by coarse screens to protect downstream equipment. Following this, the water enters a coagulation and flocculation basin. Here, chemicals such as aluminum sulfate or ferric chloride are added to the water. These coagulants neutralize the negative electrical charges of suspended particles, allowing them to stick together to form larger clumps called "flocs." The water is gently stirred to encourage these flocs to grow heavier and larger.
