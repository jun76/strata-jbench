The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the tap in a modern kitchen is one of the most complex, engineered, and critical public health achievements in human history. It is a continuous, high-stakes logistical operation that spans thousands of kilometers of pipe, millions of liters of storage, and a sophisticated network of sensors and pumps. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace this path through six distinct phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage relies on specific engineering principles and real-world examples to ensure that the water is not only potable but also available at the precise moment and pressure required by consumers.

**1. Collection: Sourcing the Raw Water**
The process begins with the collection of raw water, which typically comes from one of two primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The choice of source dictates the subsequent treatment complexity. For instance, New York City relies primarily on the Catskill and Delaware Aqueduct systems, which capture high-quality surface water from mountainous regions. Because this water is naturally filtered through the bedrock and forest floors before entering the reservoirs, it requires minimal chemical treatment compared to cities relying on turbid river systems like St. Louis, which draws from the Mississippi River.

Collection infrastructure varies by source. Surface water collection often involves large intake structures positioned deep below the waterline to avoid surface debris, ice, and seasonal fluctuations. In contrast, groundwater collection utilizes a network of municipal wells. Cities like Tucson, Arizona, depend heavily on groundwater because of their arid climate. Here, large electric submersible pumps are lowered into drilled shafts that can reach depths of several hundred feet. These wells must be carefully managed to prevent over-extraction, which can lead to land subsidence and depletion of the aquifer. The raw water collected at this stage is referred to as "source water" and is transported to treatment facilities via large-diameter transmission mains, often ranging from 24 to 100 inches in diameter, using gravity flow where elevation differences allow, or powerful booster pumps where they do not.
