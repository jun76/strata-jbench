A city water supply is a complex, engineered system designed to deliver safe, potable water from natural sources to millions of households, businesses, and industrial facilities. The journey from a river, lake, or underground aquifer to the kitchen tap involves a series of critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is essential for ensuring that the water is not only available but also safe for consumption and reliable in delivery. Below is a detailed explanation of how this system works, using concrete examples to illustrate the processes.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Water

The process begins with the collection of raw water from natural sources. Cities typically rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and infrastructure.

**Surface Water Collection:**
In many large cities, water is drawn from rivers or reservoirs. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast reservoir systems located in upstate New York. These reservoirs collect rainwater and snowmelt, which flows into the reservoirs via tributaries. The water is then transported through aqueducts—large pipelines or tunnels—to the city. The Catskill Aqueduct, for instance, is a 120-mile-long tunnel that carries water from the Ashokan Reservoir to Brooklyn. This method leverages gravity to move water without pumping, reducing energy costs.

**Groundwater Collection:**
In areas where surface water is scarce or polluted, cities tap into groundwater. Phoenix, Arizona, relies significantly on groundwater from the Salt River aquifer. Wells are drilled into the aquifer, and pumps extract the water. Groundwater is often cleaner than surface water because it is filtered naturally through soil and rock layers, but it can be vulnerable to contamination from industrial runoff or agricultural chemicals.

**Intake Structures:**
At the source, intake structures are built to draw water into the treatment plant. These structures often include screens to prevent debris, fish, and large particles from entering the system. For example, the intake at the Lake Michigan treatment plant in Chicago uses submerged screens to filter out algae and debris before the water enters the pipes.

### 2. Treatment: Purifying the Water

Once collected, raw water must be treated to remove contaminants, pathogens, and particulate matter. Treatment plants are sophisticated facilities that use a combination of physical, chemical, and biological processes.
