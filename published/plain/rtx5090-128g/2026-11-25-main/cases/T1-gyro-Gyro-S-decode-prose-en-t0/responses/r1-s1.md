A city water supply is a complex, engineered system designed to deliver safe, potable water from natural sources to millions of households, businesses, and industrial facilities. The journey from a river, lake, or underground aquifer to the kitchen tap involves a series of critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. Each step is essential to ensure that the water is not only available but also safe for consumption, free from pathogens, and delivered at a consistent pressure. This detailed explanation explores these stages using concrete examples, such as the systems found in major metropolitan areas like New York City, London, or Singapore.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Water

The process begins with the collection of raw water from natural sources. Cities typically rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and historical infrastructure.

**Surface Water Collection:**
In many large cities, surface water is the primary source. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast reservoir systems located in upstate New York. These reservoirs collect rain and snowmelt, which flows into rivers like the Hudson and Delaware. The water is then transported via aqueducts—large pipelines or tunnels—down to the city. The Catskill Aqueduct, for instance, is a 120-mile-long tunnel that carries water from the Ashokan Reservoir to the city. This method leverages gravity, reducing the need for pumping and thus lowering energy costs.

**Groundwater Collection:**
In areas where surface water is scarce or polluted, groundwater is extracted from underground aquifers. Cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely significantly on groundwater pumped from wells. These wells are drilled deep into the earth, often hundreds of feet, to access water stored in porous rock layers. The water is pumped to the surface using electric pumps. Groundwater collection requires careful monitoring to prevent over-extraction, which can lead to land subsidence or depletion of the aquifer.

**Desalination:**
In coastal cities with limited freshwater resources, desalination plants convert seawater into potable water. Singapore, for example, uses desalination plants to supplement its water supply, especially during droughts. Seawater is drawn from the ocean and processed through reverse osmosis, a technology that forces water through a membrane to remove salts and impurities.
