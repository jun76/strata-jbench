A city’s water supply is one of the most complex and critical infrastructure systems in modern civilization. It transforms raw water from rivers, lakes, aquifers, or wells into a safe, potable product that reaches the tap of millions of households, businesses, and institutions. This process is a sophisticated chain of physical, chemical, and administrative steps that ensures not only cleanliness but also reliability and pressure stability. To understand how water travels from the source to the tap, we must examine the lifecycle of water in five key stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring, with pressure management serving as the operational backbone throughout the distribution phase.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The journey begins with the source. Cities rely on different types of water sources depending on geography. Surface water sources, such as rivers and reservoirs, are common for large metropolitan areas. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware systems, which draw water from reservoirs in the Catskill Mountains. Groundwater sources, such as aquifers, are pumped directly from underground. In arid regions like Phoenix, Arizona, the city relies on a combination of groundwater wells and water transported via aqueducts from the Colorado River.

The collection process involves intake structures. For surface water, intake pipes are submerged in the reservoir or river. These pipes are designed to draw water from the middle depths of the water body to avoid surface scum (algae, debris) and bottom sediment (sand, silt). Intake screens prevent large objects like logs or fish from entering the system. However, they cannot filter microscopic particles or bacteria. Therefore, the water collected at this stage is "raw" and potentially contaminated. It may contain turbidity (cloudiness), pathogens, organic matter, and dissolved minerals. For instance, a river source might contain high levels of manganese or iron, which must be removed later. Groundwater collection involves drilling wells and installing pumps. These wells must be carefully maintained to prevent contamination from surface runoff or septic tanks.

### 2. Treatment: Purification and Conditioning

Once collected, the raw water is transported to a water treatment plant. The goal of treatment is to remove contaminants, pathogens, and undesirable chemicals while ensuring the water meets regulatory standards set by agencies like the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in the United States. The treatment process typically follows a sequence: coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection.
