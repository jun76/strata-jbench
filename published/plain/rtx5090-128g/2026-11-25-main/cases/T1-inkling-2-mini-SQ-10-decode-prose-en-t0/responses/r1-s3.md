What I can do is give a detailed, concrete overview of how a city water supply works from source to tap.

Collection: A city draws water from a source such as a river, reservoir, groundwater well, or treated surface water. In a river-fed system, intake structures capture water upstream of the city; in a groundwater system, wells extract from aquifers. The source is chosen for reliability and quality, and the intake is designed to avoid drawing contaminated or low-flow water.

Treatment: Once collected, water is treated to remove or reduce contaminants. Typical steps include screening and filtration, coagulation and flocculation to settle particles, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection. In concrete terms, a city might use a river intake, add chemicals to bind fine particles, let the water settle, pass it through sand or other filters, and then add chlorine or another disinfectant. Some systems also adjust pH, remove hardness or specific contaminants, or add fluoride where required.

Storage: Treated water is stored in reservoirs, tanks, or elevated storage facilities. Storage provides buffer capacity for demand peaks, emergency use, and pressure stability. A city may have a large underground reservoir, a raised tank, or a combination of both. Storage also allows operators to maintain a reserve for firefighting and to smooth out supply fluctuations.

Distribution: From storage, water moves through a network of pipes, valves, and pumps. The network is designed to reach homes, businesses, schools, and hospitals. Distribution is often divided into zones or pressure districts so that different parts of the city can be served at appropriate pressure. Concrete examples include a main trunk line from a reservoir to a neighborhood, branch lines to streets, and service connections to individual buildings.

Pressure management: Pressure is controlled by pumps, valves, pressure zones, and storage levels. If demand rises, pumps increase flow; if pressure drops, valves and zone controls adjust the system. Elevated tanks can provide gravity-fed pressure in some areas, while pumped systems maintain pressure in others. Pressure management is critical because too little pressure can cause service interruptions, while too much can stress pipes and fixtures.
