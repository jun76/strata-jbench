The journey of a glass of drinking water is a marvel of engineering, chemistry, and urban planning. It begins in a natural reservoir—perhaps a mountain lake, a river, or an aquifer deep underground—and ends at a kitchen tap, clean, safe, and pressurized. This process, known as municipal water supply, is a complex chain of collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. To understand how water reaches your home, we must trace its path step by step, using concrete examples from major metropolitan systems.

### 1. Collection and Intake

The first stage involves sourcing water from natural bodies. Most cities rely on either surface water (lakes, rivers, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers tapped by wells). Surface water is often more accessible but requires extensive treatment due to exposure to pollutants, runoff, and seasonal changes. Groundwater, protected by layers of soil and rock, is often cleaner but can be contaminated by industrial waste or excessive pumping.

**Concrete Example:** The city of Seattle relies heavily on the Lake Washington system. Water is drawn from Lake Washington through intake pipes located underwater. These intakes are designed to prevent debris and aquatic life from entering the system. For cities like Phoenix, Arizona, which face arid conditions, water is transported over hundreds of miles from the Colorado River via canals like the Central Arizona Project, demonstrating how collection can involve massive infrastructure beyond the city limits itself.

In urban settings, intakes must be strategically placed to avoid contamination sources. For instance, intake pipes are typically positioned upstream of sewage discharge points. Environmental regulations often dictate buffer zones to ensure raw water meets baseline quality standards before treatment begins.

### 2. Preliminary Screening and Coagulation

Once raw water arrives at the treatment plant, it undergoes preliminary screening. This stage removes large debris such as leaves, branches, and trash. Water flows through screens that capture physical objects. This is crucial for protecting downstream equipment from damage.

The next step is **coagulation**, a critical chemical process. Raw water contains suspended particles and colloids that are too small to settle naturally. Chemical coagulants, such as aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride, are added. These chemicals neutralize the negative charges on particles, allowing them to clump together into larger aggregates called **flocs**. This process is highly sensitive to water chemistry; if the pH or alkalinity is too low, coagulation fails, leading to murky water.
