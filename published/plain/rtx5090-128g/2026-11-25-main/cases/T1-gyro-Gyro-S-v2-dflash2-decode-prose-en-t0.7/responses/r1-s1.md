A modern city water supply system is a marvel of civil engineering, public health, and infrastructure management. It transforms raw, potentially contaminated water from nature into a safe, reliable, and pressurized resource that reaches millions of households, businesses, and institutions every day. This journey—from source to tap—involves a complex chain of collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and rigorous quality monitoring. Each stage is critical; a failure at any point can compromise public safety or disrupt essential services. To understand how this works, we must walk through the entire lifecycle of water as it moves through a typical municipal system.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Supply

The process begins long before water enters a treatment plant. Municipalities rely on two primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers). The choice depends on geography, climate, and local geology.

**Surface Water Collection:**
Cities like Chicago rely on Lake Michigan, while New York City draws from the Catskill and Delaware watershed systems. These sources are vast, but they are vulnerable to pollution from agricultural runoff, industrial discharge, and urban runoff. Collection involves intake structures. In large cities, these might be submerged pipes or pumping stations located near shorelines. For example, the Chicago Water Tower serves as an iconic landmark, but its function is tied to intake structures that draw lake water into pumping stations.

Groundwater collection is different. Wells are drilled into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing permeable rock. In arid regions like parts of California, groundwater is often the primary source. Wells can be shallow or deep, depending on the aquifer’s depth. Pumping groundwater requires careful management to avoid depleting the aquifer faster than it can recharge, a phenomenon known as overdraft. In Las Vegas, for instance, groundwater pumping is supplemented by imported water from Lake Mead via pipelines, illustrating how cities may combine multiple sources to ensure resilience.
