The journey of water from its natural origin to the kitchen sink is one of the most complex, critical, and invisible engineering marvels in modern urban life. While it may seem mundane to simply turn a handle, the process involves a sophisticated network of infrastructure, chemical science, physical engineering, and regulatory compliance. This article details the intricate path a city water supply takes, moving from collection and raw water intake through rigorous treatment, massive storage, complex distribution networks, precise pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. To illustrate this, we will examine common practices and draw parallels with real-world systems such as those found in New York City, Los Angeles, and Chicago, which represent diverse challenges ranging from surface water protection to long-distance conveyance and seismic resilience.

### Collection and Intake: The Source of the Supply

The first stage in the water supply chain is collection, also known as raw water intake. The method of collection depends heavily on the geographic location of the city and its available natural resources. Most major cities rely on either surface water sources, such as rivers, lakes, and reservoirs, or groundwater sources, such as aquifers.

Surface water collection typically involves drawing water from a reservoir or river through intake structures. These intakes are designed to prevent large debris, fish, and other aquatic organisms from entering the treatment system. For example, the Catskill and Delaware water supply system, which provides roughly 90% of New York City’s drinking water, collects surface water from upstate reservoirs. Because this water comes from protected forested watersheds, it remains remarkably clean, allowing the city to largely avoid the use of chlorine disinfection at the intake stage, a rare privilege in modern urban water management.

In contrast, cities in arid regions or those dependent on underground reserves often rely on groundwater. Groundwater collection involves pumping water from wells drilled into aquifers. This process is energy-intensive because water must be lifted from significant depths. In cities like Los Angeles, which relies on a mix of local groundwater and imported surface water, groundwater pumping helps maintain the structural integrity of the land, preventing subsidence (sinking of the ground) that occurs when aquifers are depleted. Regardless of the source, the water entering the system at this stage is considered "raw water." It may contain sediments, organic matter, nutrients, naturally occurring minerals, and potential contaminants like bacteria or industrial runoff.
