The journey of water from its natural origin to the kitchen sink is one of the most complex, critical, and often invisible infrastructural systems in modern civilization. While we often turn on a faucet and expect clean water to flow instantly, this convenience is the result of a meticulously engineered chain of events known as the urban water cycle. This process begins with source protection and collection, moves through rigorous physical and chemical treatment, enters vast storage networks, travels through a pressurized distribution grid, and undergoes continuous quality monitoring. To understand how a city water supply truly works, we must trace the path of a single drop of water, using concrete examples from major global cities like New York City, London, and Singapore to illustrate the distinct methodologies required by different geographical and technological contexts.

The first stage is source selection and collection. Cities must identify water sources that are abundant, renewable, and sufficiently pure to be treated economically. These sources are generally categorized as surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). For instance, New York City relies heavily on surface water sourced from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are located approximately 125 miles away from Manhattan. Because the watershed is forested and minimally developed, the water arrives with relatively low turbidity and organic matter, allowing the city to use a unique "pass-through" treatment system for much of its supply. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely on surface water from the Colorado River and Salt River, which carry high sediment loads and require extensive pre-treatment. Groundwater sources, such as those used by the San Antonio Water System in Texas, draw from the Edwards Aquifer. Here, collection involves drilling deep wells into underground rock formations to pump water to the surface. The choice of source dictates the complexity of the subsequent treatment processes. Source protection is equally vital; if a river is polluted by industrial runoff or agricultural pesticides, the cost and difficulty of treatment skyrocket. Therefore, many cities implement strict zoning laws in their catchment areas to prevent contamination, a strategy famously employed by London, which manages its Thames and Lee river catchments to protect water quality upstream.

Once collected, the raw water is transported to a water treatment plant. This stage is the heart of the water supply system, designed to remove physical, chemical, and biological contaminants. Treatment follows a standard multi-barrier approach, though specific methods vary by source quality. Physical treatment begins with screening to remove large debris like leaves and branches.
