The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, continuous, and critical engineering feats in modern society. It is a process that blends civil engineering, environmental science, chemistry, and fluid dynamics into a seamless service that most people take for granted. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of water through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is vital; a failure in any one of them can lead to shortages, contamination, or system collapse.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Life

The process begins at the source, which can broadly be categorized into two types: surface water and groundwater. The choice of source depends largely on geography, climate, and population density.

**Surface Water Sources**
In many major metropolitan areas, surface water is the primary source. This includes rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware water supply systems, which draw water from a network of reservoirs and lakes in upstate New York. The water here is collected via large intake structures located deep within the reservoirs to avoid surface debris and ice. These intakes are equipped with trash racks and screens to prevent fish, leaves, and other organic matter from entering the pipeline. The sheer scale is immense; the City of Los Angeles, for instance, draws water from the Colorado River over 200 miles away via the Los Angeles Aqueduct, demonstrating how cities often need to look far beyond their immediate borders to sustain their populations.

**Groundwater Sources**
Conversely, cities in arid regions or areas with abundant aquifers often rely on groundwater. Cities like Phoenix, Arizona, or many municipalities in the American Midwest drill deep wells into underground aquifers. These wells can penetrate hundreds of feet into the earth to reach saturated zones where water is held in porous rock or soil. Groundwater is generally cleaner and less susceptible to surface contamination than surface water, but it can contain higher levels of minerals like iron, manganese, and calcium, which require specific treatment steps later in the process.
