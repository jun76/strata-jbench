The journey of water from its natural source to a household tap is one of the most complex, engineered, and essential systems in modern civilization. While it may seem simple to turn on a faucet and see clear water flow, this process involves a sophisticated chain of infrastructure known as the municipal water supply system. This system is not merely about moving water; it is about ensuring safety, maintaining pressure, and guaranteeing reliability. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through five critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring, with a specific focus on pressure management, which acts as the heartbeat of the network.

The journey begins at the **source**, where water is collected or extracted. Cities typically rely on two primary types of sources: surface water and groundwater. Surface water sources include rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. For example, New York City draws much of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast upland areas that collect rain and snowmelt. Groundwater sources, on the other hand, are accessed through wells drilled into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing permeable rock. Cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely heavily on the Colorado River (surface) and deep groundwater wells. The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and population density. In arid regions, cities may also invest in desalination plants, such as those in San Diego or Singapore, which convert seawater into potable water through reverse osmosis. Regardless of the source, the water entering the system is raw, containing sediment, organic matter, pathogens, and sometimes industrial pollutants.

Once collected, the water enters the **treatment** phase, which is arguably the most critical step for public health. Modern water treatment is a multi-barrier process designed to remove contaminants and disinfect the water. The first stage is usually coagulation and flocculation. Chemicals called coagulants, such as aluminum sulfate (alum), are added to the water. These chemicals neutralize the negative charges of suspended particles, causing them to clump together into larger particles called flocs. This process is vital because it removes microscopic particles that could shield bacteria from disinfectants. Next, the water undergoes sedimentation, where it flows into large settling basins.
