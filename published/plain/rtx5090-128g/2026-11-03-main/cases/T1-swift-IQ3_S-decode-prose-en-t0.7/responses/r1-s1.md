The journey of water from its natural source to the tap in a residential kitchen is a marvel of modern engineering, public health, and logistical coordination. It is a continuous, complex cycle that involves massive infrastructure, rigorous scientific protocols, and real-time data management. To understand how a city water supply works, one must trace the path through six critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is designed not only to deliver water efficiently but also to ensure it remains safe, clean, and reliable for millions of consumers.

The process begins with **collection**, where the city identifies and secures its raw water sources. These sources generally fall into two categories: surface water and groundwater. Surface water is collected from rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. For example, the city of New York relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware Aqueducts, which transport water from upstate reservoirs over 100 miles to the city. This is a gravity-fed system, meaning the water flows downhill naturally, requiring minimal energy for transport. In contrast, groundwater is accessed through wells drilled into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing rock. Cities in arid regions, such as those in the American Southwest, often rely on deep aquifers. The collection point is typically a intake structure, such as a screening facility on a riverbank, which prevents large debris, fish, and sediment from entering the pumping system. In a city like Chicago, water is drawn directly from Lake Michigan through massive intake cribs located miles offshore, ensuring that the water is relatively free of shoreline pollution and silt.

Once collected, the raw water undergoes **treatment**, a multi-step process designed to remove contaminants and pathogens. This is the heart of the water supply system. The first step is usually coagulation and flocculation. Chemicals such as aluminum sulfate (alum) are added to the water. These chemicals neutralize the negative charges of dirt and clay particles, allowing them to clump together into larger masses called "flocs." In a typical treatment plant, large mixing paddles gently stir the water to encourage these flocs to form. Next, the water moves to sedimentation basins, where the heavy flocs settle to the bottom, leaving clearer water on top. The sludge at the bottom is periodically removed.
