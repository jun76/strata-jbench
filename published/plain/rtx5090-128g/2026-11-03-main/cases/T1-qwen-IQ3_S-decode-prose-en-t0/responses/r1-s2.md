The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, critical, and often invisible engineering feats in modern civilization. It is a continuous, high-stakes logistical operation that involves massive infrastructure, precise chemical engineering, rigorous biological monitoring, and sophisticated hydraulic management. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is designed not only to deliver water but to ensure it is safe, palatable, and available at the exact moment a resident turns on a tap.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be a surface water body like a river, lake, or reservoir, or groundwater extracted from deep aquifers. The choice of source dictates the subsequent treatment requirements. For example, a city like New York relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are protected forested areas that provide relatively clean surface water. In contrast, a city like Phoenix, Arizona, relies on the Colorado River, which carries significant sediment and agricultural runoff, requiring more intensive treatment.

Collection involves intake structures that draw water from the source. These intakes are often located at specific depths to avoid surface debris, ice, or bottom sediments. For instance, in many coastal cities, desalination plants collect seawater, but for the vast majority of inland cities, the intake is a submerged pipe or a screened structure. These screens are crucial; they prevent fish, leaves, and other large debris from entering the system. A concrete example is the intake at the Lake Michigan water treatment plants in Chicago. Here, massive submerged pipes extend into the lake, drawing water that is then pumped uphill to the treatment facilities. The energy required for this initial pumping is significant, as water must be lifted from the source elevation to the treatment plant elevation, often involving hundreds of feet of vertical lift.

### 2. Treatment: The Science of Purification

Once collected, the water enters the treatment plant, where it undergoes a series of physical, chemical, and biological processes to remove contaminants and pathogens. This is the heart of the water supply system. The specific treatment train depends on the source water quality, but a typical surface water treatment process includes coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection.
