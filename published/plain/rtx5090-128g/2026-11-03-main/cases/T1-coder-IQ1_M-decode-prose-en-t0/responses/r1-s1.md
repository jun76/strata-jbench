The journey of water from a natural source to a household tap is a complex, engineered process that represents one of the most critical infrastructure systems in modern urban planning. This system, often referred to as the municipal water supply network, is not merely a series of pipes but a sophisticated integration of hydrology, chemistry, engineering, and public health monitoring. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the lifecycle of water through six distinct phases: collection (source acquisition), treatment (purification), storage (reservoirs), distribution (mains and service lines), pressure management (pumping and gravity), and quality monitoring (regulatory compliance). Each phase involves specific technologies, challenges, and concrete examples that illustrate the scale and complexity of urban water management.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The journey begins at the source, which can be a natural spring, a river, a lake, or an aquifer. For most major cities, the primary source is a large body of water located upstream from the urban center. A classic example is New York City, which relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware Aqueducts, drawing water from reservoirs in the Catskills Mountains and the Delaware River basin. Another example is Los Angeles, which imports water from the Owens Valley and the Colorado River via the Los Angeles Aqueduct.

The "collection" phase involves the physical intake of raw water. This is rarely a simple scoop; it is a massive engineering feat. In the case of the Catskill Aqueduct, water is collected from reservoirs like Ashokan Reservoir. The intake structures are designed to prevent debris, algae, and sediment from entering the system. Large screens and filters are installed at the intake points to catch leaves, branches, and other organic matter. This is crucial because untreated organic matter can clog pumps and introduce biological contaminants that are difficult to remove later.

However, collection is not just about grabbing water; it is about managing the environment. Cities often face the challenge of changing water levels due to drought or flood. Therefore, intake structures are adjustable. In many modern systems, such as those in Singapore or Israel, the "source" might also include treated wastewater or rainwater harvesting, but for traditional municipal supplies, the focus remains on natural sources. The water collected is "raw" or "turbid," meaning it contains suspended solids, bacteria, and chemical impurities from the natural environment. It is not safe for consumption at this stage.
