A city water supply is one of the most complex and critical infrastructure systems in modern civilization. It is a continuous, multi-stage process that transforms raw, often contaminated natural resources into safe, potable water delivered to millions of households, businesses, and industrial facilities. The journey from source to tap involves engineering, chemistry, biology, and rigorous quality control. To understand this system, we must trace the path of water through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be a surface water body (river, lake, or reservoir) or groundwater (aquifers). For example, a city like Chicago relies heavily on Lake Michigan, while a city like Phoenix draws from the Colorado River via aqueducts. Groundwater sources, such as those in parts of Texas or Florida, involve drilling wells into underground aquifers.

The collection phase involves intake structures. For surface water, this might be a submerged intake pipe or a pumping station located near the shoreline. These intakes are designed to draw water from depths where sediment and debris are less likely to be present. In groundwater systems, pumps are installed in wells to lift water to the surface. A critical aspect of collection is the initial screening. Large debris, such as branches, plastic, or aquatic life, must be removed before the water enters the treatment plant. This is often done using coarse screens or bar racks. Additionally, intake locations are chosen to avoid areas prone to algal blooms or industrial runoff, ensuring the raw water quality is manageable.

### 2. Treatment: Purification and Conditioning

Once collected, the water is transported to a water treatment plant. Here, it undergoes a series of physical and chemical processes to remove contaminants, pathogens, and undesirable tastes or odors. The treatment process is typically divided into several steps:

**Coagulation and Flocculation:** Raw water often contains suspended particles, clay, and organic matter. To remove these, chemicals like aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride are added. These chemicals neutralize the electrical charges of particles, causing them to clump together into larger aggregates called "flocs." This step is crucial because it makes subsequent filtration possible.
