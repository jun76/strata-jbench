A city water supply is a complex, engineered system designed to deliver safe, potable water from natural sources to millions of consumers through a network of pipes, pumps, and treatment facilities. The journey from a river, lake, or underground aquifer to the kitchen tap involves several critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is essential for ensuring that water is not only available but also safe for human consumption. Below is a detailed explanation of how this system works, using concrete examples to illustrate the processes.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Water

The process begins with the collection of raw water from natural sources. Cities typically rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and infrastructure.

**Surface Water Collection:**
In many large cities, water is drawn from rivers or reservoirs. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are reservoirs located in upstate New York. These reservoirs collect rainwater and runoff from surrounding forests and mountains. The water flows into intake structures, which are large pipes or tunnels that draw water from the reservoir. These intakes are often equipped with screens to prevent debris, fish, and large particles from entering the system.

**Groundwater Collection:**
In areas where surface water is scarce or polluted, cities may rely on groundwater. For instance, Phoenix, Arizona, draws significant portions of its water from underground aquifers. Wells are drilled into the ground, and pumps extract water from porous rock layers. Groundwater is often cleaner than surface water because it has been naturally filtered through soil and rock, but it can contain minerals like iron or manganese that require treatment.

**Concrete Example:**
Consider the city of Denver, Colorado. It collects water from the South Platte River and various reservoirs like Lake Dillon. The intake structures are designed to handle seasonal variations in water flow. During heavy rains, the river may carry sediment and organic matter, which must be managed during the treatment phase.

### 2. Treatment: Purifying the Water

Raw water is rarely safe to drink directly. It may contain pathogens, chemicals, sediments, and organic matter. Treatment plants are designed to remove these contaminants through a series of physical and chemical processes.
