A city’s water supply system is one of the most complex and invisible pieces of infrastructure that supports modern urban life. It functions as a living organism, constantly breathing, circulating, and filtering to ensure that millions of residents have access to safe, potable water whenever they turn on their tap. This process begins long before the water enters the home, starting at the source and ending only after the water has traveled through miles of pipes, pumping stations, and treatment facilities. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the journey from the natural environment to the faucet, examining collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

**Collection: From Source to Headworks**

The journey begins at the source. Cities generally rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, or reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The method of collection depends on the geographical location and the scale of the city. For example, New York City is famous for its reliance on gravity-fed surface water from the Catskill and Delaware reservoir systems, located nearly 100 miles north of the city. In contrast, Los Angeles relies heavily on groundwater and imported water from the Colorado River, requiring massive pumping stations to lift water to the city’s elevation.

In a typical surface water system, water is collected via large concrete conduits or tunnels. At the source, there is an intake structure that controls the flow, preventing debris and sediment from entering the system. This structure is known as a headworks. The headworks are critical because they act as the first line of defense against physical contaminants. For instance, in many cities, screens at the intake prevent fish, leaves, and large debris from entering the treatment process. In groundwater systems, collection involves a network of wells drilled into underground aquifers. These wells are often grouped in "well fields," where pumps extract water from underground. The collection phase is purely mechanical but requires careful engineering to ensure that the extraction rate does not deplete the aquifer or cause land subsidence, a common problem in cities like Houston or Jakarta.

**Treatment: Making Nature Potable**

Once collected, the raw water enters the treatment plant, where it undergoes a rigorous series of physical, chemical, and biological processes. The specific treatment steps depend on the quality of the source water, but the general goal is to remove pathogens, sediment, dissolved minerals, and taste-and-odor compounds.
