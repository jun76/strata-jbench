A city water supply system is one of the most complex, invisible, and essential pieces of infrastructure that supports modern urban life. It transforms raw, natural resources into safe, potable water that travels hundreds of miles through pipes, tunnels, and pumping stations to reach a household tap. To understand this process in detail, we must trace the journey of water from its source, through rigorous treatment, storage, and distribution, while managing pressure and quality along the way.

### 1. Collection: Source Water and Intake

The journey begins with **source water**, which can be groundwater (aquifers) or surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs). The choice depends on geography, climate, and local geology. For example, New York City primarily relies on reservoirs in the Catskill Mountains, while many arid cities like Phoenix depend heavily on groundwater pumping from deep aquifers or imported surface water from distant sources like the Colorado River.

**Groundwater Collection:**
Groundwater is accessed via wells. These wells are drilled hundreds to thousands of feet deep into porous rock or sediment layers that store water underground. Pumping stations draw water up from these aquifers. However, groundwater extraction must be carefully managed to avoid depletion. If too much water is pumped, the water table drops, potentially causing land subsidence (ground sinking) or saltwater intrusion in coastal areas. For instance, in Las Vegas, extensive groundwater pumping from Nevada’s aquifers has required monitoring to prevent long-term ecological damage.

**Surface Water Collection:**
Surface water is collected via intakes—structures built at reservoirs or rivers. These intakes often include screens or gates to prevent debris, fish, and large particles from entering the system. In large cities like Chicago, water is drawn from Lake Michigan through massive intake structures located in the lake. The water then flows through tunnels buried beneath Lake Michigan or along the shore to treatment plants. These tunnels can be tens of miles long and are critical because they prevent contamination from shoreline developments and ensure consistent supply.
