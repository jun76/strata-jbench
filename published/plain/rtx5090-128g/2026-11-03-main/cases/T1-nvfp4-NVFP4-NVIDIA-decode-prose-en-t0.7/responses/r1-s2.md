The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the faucet in a modern kitchen is a marvel of civil engineering, hydraulic physics, and rigorous public health management. This complex system, known as the municipal water supply, operates as a continuous, invisible infrastructure that sustains urban life. To understand how a city water supply works, one must trace the path of water through six critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage involves specific technologies and protocols designed to ensure that the water is not only safe to drink but also available on demand, at the correct pressure, and with consistent quality.

The process begins with **collection**, where raw water is extracted from natural sources. These sources are generally categorized into surface water and groundwater. Surface water is drawn from rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware aqueduct systems, which transport water from high-elevation reservoirs in the Catskill Mountains. In this scenario, collection involves large intake structures or submerged pipes that draw water into the system. Groundwater, conversely, is sourced from underground aquifers via wells. Cities like Phoenix, Arizona, utilize deep wells to tap into the Central Arizona Project’s groundwater reserves. The choice of source dictates the subsequent treatment needs; surface water is more susceptible to contamination by runoff, leaves, and microorganisms, whereas groundwater is naturally filtered through soil but may contain high levels of dissolved minerals like calcium or iron. Regardless of the source, the raw water is transported via massive conduits or aqueducts to the treatment plant. These conduits are often gravity-fed to save energy, relying on the elevation difference between the source and the city, as seen in the Roman-inspired aqueduct systems still utilized in parts of Southern Europe and the United States.

Once the raw water arrives at the treatment facility, it undergoes a rigorous, multi-step **treatment** process designed to remove physical, chemical, and biological contaminants. The first step is typically **screening**, where large debris such as sticks, leaves, and fish are removed using mechanical screens. This is followed by **coagulation and flocculation**. In this chemical stage, coagulants like aluminum sulfate or ferric chloride are added to the water. These chemicals neutralize the negative charges of suspended particles, causing them to clump together into larger masses called "floc."
