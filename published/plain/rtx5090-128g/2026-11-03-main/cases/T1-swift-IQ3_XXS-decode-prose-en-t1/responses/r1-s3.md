The journey of a glass of water from a distant reservoir to a kitchen faucet is one of the most complex, invisible, and critical infrastructure feats of the modern era. It is a process that blends civil engineering, chemical science, hydraulic physics, and rigorous public health standards. To understand this system fully, we must trace the water’s path through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This narrative will explore each phase using concrete examples, illustrating how cities ensure that the water coming out of the tap is not only clean and safe but also reliable in supply and consistent in pressure.

### 1. Collection: Sourcing the Raw Material

The first stage of the water supply chain is collection, which involves capturing raw water from natural sources. Cities are not monolithic in their sourcing; they rely on a mix of surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and population density.

For example, consider **New York City**, which famously sources approximately 90% of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located over 100 miles north of the city. These are primarily surface water sources fed by rain and snowmelt. The collection process here is largely passive: water flows via gravity through massive underground tunnels, such as the City Water Tunnel No. 3, which is part of a 240-mile network of aqueducts and pipes. The raw water enters the system through intake structures where screens prevent large debris, like leaves and fish, from entering the pipes.

In contrast, **Los Angeles** relies heavily on imported surface water from the Colorado River and California State Water Project, as well as local groundwater and recycled water. Groundwater collection involves drilling deep wells into aquifers, where pumps draw water up to the surface. A concrete example of groundwater collection is found in **Phoenix, Arizona**, where municipal wells tap into the alluvial aquifers of the Salt River. Because groundwater is often deeper and more stable than surface water, it requires significant pumping energy to bring it to the surface, highlighting the role of mechanical infrastructure from the very start.

Collection systems must also account for seasonal variations.
