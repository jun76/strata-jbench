The journey of water from a distant mountain reservoir to the kitchen faucet in a modern city is one of the most complex, critical, and invisible engineering feats in human civilization. It is a system that operates 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, balancing physics, chemistry, biology, and economics to ensure that every time a resident turns a handle, clean, safe, and pressurized water is available. This process involves several distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and rigorous quality monitoring. To understand how this works, we must follow a single drop of water through the infrastructure of a hypothetical major city, such as New York City or London, though the principles apply universally.

### 1. Collection: The Source

The process begins at the source. Cities rarely draw water directly from a single river or lake; instead, they rely on vast watersheds. A watershed is an area of land where all precipitation and runoff drain into a common outlet, such as a river, lake, or ocean. For a city like New York, the source is the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which cover over 2,000 square miles. This area is protected as a forested, undeveloped zone to prevent contamination from urban runoff, industrial waste, or agricultural pesticides.

In many cities, the collection phase involves massive reservoirs. These are not just pools of water; they are engineered containment systems designed to store billions of gallons. For example, the Ashokan Reservoir in New York holds enough water to supply the city for several months. In cities with lower rainfall or different topographies, such as Los Angeles, collection might involve diverting water from distant rivers via aqueducts, like the Los Angeles Aqueduct, which brings water from the Owens River hundreds of miles away. The primary goal at this stage is quantity and initial quality assurance. By keeping the watershed clean, the city reduces the burden on later treatment processes. If the source water is highly polluted, the cost and complexity of treatment skyrocket. Therefore, source protection is the first and most cost-effective layer of defense in the water supply chain.
