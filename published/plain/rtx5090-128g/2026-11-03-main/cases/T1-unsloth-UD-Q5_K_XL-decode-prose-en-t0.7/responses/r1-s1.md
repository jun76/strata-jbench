The journey of a single glass of water from its natural origin to the kitchen faucet is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical feats of modern civil engineering. It is a silent, invisible infrastructure that underpins public health and urban stability. This process involves a sophisticated interplay of hydrology, chemistry, physics, and data science, designed to transform raw, often contaminated natural water into a safe, palatable, and consistent commodity. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and continuous quality monitoring.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source. Cities typically rely on one of two primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The choice of source dictates the subsequent treatment requirements. For instance, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware aqueduct systems, which transport surface water from protected mountain reservoirs. In contrast, many cities in the American Midwest, such as Omaha, Nebraska, rely on deep groundwater wells drilled into the Ogallala Aquifer.

In surface water systems, collection involves massive intake structures located in the reservoir or river. These intakes are often submerged to draw water from cooler, less turbulent layers, avoiding surface debris and ice. Large screens prevent fish, leaves, and large floating objects from entering the system. A critical component here is the "screening" process. Modern intakes use traveling screens—huge conveyor-belt-like mesh barriers that rotate to catch debris while allowing water to pass through. If the water source is a river prone to seasonal flooding, the intake might be located hundreds of feet away from the bank in a deep trench to ensure consistent access.

For groundwater systems, collection is more direct but equally complex. Municipal wells can be hundreds of feet deep, lined with steel or concrete to prevent contamination from surface soil. Pumps, often submersible electric pumps powered by high-voltage lines, lift the water to the surface. Groundwater is generally easier to treat than surface water because it is naturally filtered by layers of soil and rock, meaning it lacks the turbidity (cloudiness) and biological pathogens common in rivers. However, it may contain dissolved minerals like iron, manganese, or arsenic, which require specific removal processes.
