The journey of a drop of water from its natural source to the tap in your kitchen is one of the most complex, engineered, and critical processes in modern infrastructure. It is a seamless blend of natural science, chemical engineering, hydraulic physics, and public policy. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of water through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring. This process ensures that millions of households receive safe, potable, and reliable water 24 hours a day.

### 1. Collection: From Nature to Infrastructure

The journey begins at the source. For most cities, the primary source is surface water, such as rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. Some regions, particularly in the western United States, rely heavily on groundwater pumped from aquifers. Regardless of the source, the first step is **collection**.

**Example:** Consider the city of Chicago, which relies on Lake Michigan. The water is collected via huge intake structures located far offshore in the lake. These structures act as screens, filtering out large debris, weeds, and sometimes fish or birds. In contrast, a city like Denver, which draws from the South Platte River, might use a dammed reservoir (like Chatfield Reservoir) where water is collected by gravity into a pipe network leading to the treatment plant.

The key challenge in collection is protection. Since surface water is exposed to weather and human activity, it must be protected from contamination. This is where **source protection** comes into play. Cities often designate "water quality protection areas" around the reservoir or river. These areas restrict industrial development, require strict runoff controls, and monitor for pollutants like pesticides or heavy metals. For instance, the EPA’s Safe Drinking Water Act mandates that states establish source water assessment programs to identify potential threats.

### 2. Treatment: Purifying the Water

Once collected, the water is rarely clean enough for consumption. It may contain suspended solids, pathogens (bacteria and viruses), dissolved minerals, and organic matter. The treatment plant is where these contaminants are removed through a series of physical, chemical, and biological processes.
