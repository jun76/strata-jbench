The journey of a single drop of water from a distant river or aquifer to the kitchen tap is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical logistical feats in modern civilization. While the tap turns on and water flows out instantly, this seamless experience is the result of a vast, hidden infrastructure network involving civil engineering, hydraulic physics, chemical engineering, and rigorous public health protocols. This process can be broken down into six distinct stages: source collection, treatment, storage, transmission, distribution, and quality assurance.

### 1. Source Collection: Intake and Extraction

The first stage is obtaining the raw water. Cities typically rely on two primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers, wells).

**Concrete Example:** Consider the water supply for New York City. Approximately 90% of its water comes from surface sources in the Catskill, Delaware, and Croton watersheds. These are reservoirs where water accumulates naturally. To collect this water, the city uses intake structures—often called "gatehouses." These are submerged intake pipes located at specific depths in the reservoir. Engineers choose the depth carefully because water quality varies by layer; the upper layers might contain debris or algae, while deeper layers are colder, oxygen-rich, and cleaner. The water flows through these intakes by gravity or pumps into a primary conduit line.

In groundwater systems, such as those in Tucson, Arizona, the process involves drilling deep wells into aquifers—geological formations of permeable rock that hold water. Hundreds of pumps are installed at these well sites, extracting water from hundreds of feet underground. This extraction must be managed carefully to prevent the aquifer from depleting or collapsing, which could cause land subsidence. Regardless of the source, the water at this stage is known as "raw water" and is unfit for human consumption due to biological contaminants, sediments, and chemical impurities.
