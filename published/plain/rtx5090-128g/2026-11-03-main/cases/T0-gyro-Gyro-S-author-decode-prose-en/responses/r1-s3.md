A city’s water supply is not merely a plumbing system; it is a complex, engineered infrastructure designed to transform raw environmental resources into a safe, reliable, and palatable commodity delivered to millions of consumers. The journey from a distant reservoir or aquifer to the kitchen tap involves a rigorous sequence of collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. This process ensures that water not only reaches the user but also meets strict health standards and functional requirements. To understand this system fully, one must examine each stage in detail, using concrete examples from major metropolitan areas such as New York City, London, and Singapore.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The first step in the water supply chain is the acquisition of raw water. Sources vary depending on geography and infrastructure. They generally fall into three categories: surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs), groundwater (aquifers tapped by wells), and desalinated seawater.

For example, New York City relies heavily on surface water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located over 100 miles north of the city. These reservoirs collect rain and snowmelt, which is channeled into massive storage basins. The water here is relatively clean because the watersheds are protected wilderness areas, minimizing pollution from agriculture or industry. In contrast, cities like London often rely on a mix of surface water from reservoirs like the River Thames and groundwater pumped from chalk aquifers.

The collection process involves intake structures—often submerged pipes or pumping stations—that draw water from the source. In surface water systems, intakes are designed to avoid drawing sediment or debris. In groundwater systems, wells are drilled deep into the earth, tapping into porous rock layers where water has been naturally filtered over decades. For instance, in Las Vegas, water is sourced from Lake Mead, a massive reservoir formed by damming the Colorado River. The intake pipes draw water from the lake, which then travels through pipelines to treatment facilities. Desalination plants, such as those in Singapore, collect seawater from the ocean, treating it as a raw resource that requires intensive processing to remove salt and impurities.
