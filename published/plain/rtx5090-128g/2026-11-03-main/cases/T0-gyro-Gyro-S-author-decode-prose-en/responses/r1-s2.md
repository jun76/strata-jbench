A city water supply is one of the most complex and critical infrastructure systems in modern urban life. It is a continuous engineering marvel that transforms raw, often polluted, natural water into safe, potable drinking water, delivered to millions of households and businesses with consistent pressure and temperature. To understand how this works, we must trace the journey of a single drop of water from its origin in nature to the faucet in your kitchen. This journey involves six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is vital, and the failure of any one can compromise public health or infrastructure stability.

### Stage 1: Collection and Intake

The process begins with the source. Cities typically rely on two primary sources: surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers tapped by wells). For example, New York City draws much of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watershed systems, vast reservoirs located hundreds of miles away. Los Angeles relies heavily on groundwater and imported surface water from distant reservoirs like Lake Powell via aqueducts.

The collection phase involves intake structures. For surface water, this often means large intakes located at the edge of a reservoir or river. These intakes are designed to draw water from specific depths to avoid surface pollutants or sediment. In groundwater systems, wells are drilled deep into the earth, sometimes hundreds of meters down. A pump lifts the water from the aquifer. At this stage, the water is raw. It may contain silt, debris, algae, bacteria, and dissolved minerals. The goal here is not to treat the water, but to gather it efficiently and begin the filtration process.

### Stage 2: Treatment and Purification

Once collected, the water is transported to a water treatment plant. This is where the transformation from raw to potable occurs. Treatment is a multi-step physical and chemical process designed to remove contaminants and pathogens.

First comes **coagulation and flocculation**. Chemicals, usually aluminum sulfate or ferric chloride, are added to the water. These chemicals neutralize the charge of suspended particles, causing them to clump together into larger, heavier aggregates called "flocs." This makes it easier to remove them later.

Next is **sedimentation**. The water flows into large basins where the flocs sink to the bottom due to gravity. The clearer water above is skimmed off. This removes a significant portion of the suspended solids and organic matter.
