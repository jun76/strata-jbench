The journey of water from a natural source to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, energy-intensive, and critical engineering feats in modern society. It is not merely a matter of opening a valve; it is a sophisticated system involving hydrology, chemistry, mechanical engineering, and public health policy. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring. Each stage relies on concrete infrastructure and specific operational protocols to ensure that the water delivered is safe, reliable, and consistent.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, population size, and environmental regulations. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are protected forests that feed reservoirs like the Ashokan Reservoir. In contrast, many cities in the American Southwest, such as Phoenix, Arizona, rely on the Colorado River, requiring massive pumping infrastructure to move water hundreds of miles uphill.

The physical collection involves intake structures. For surface water, these are often submerged pipes or screens located at the deepest, cleanest part of a reservoir or lake. These intakes are designed to avoid drawing in sediment, algae, or debris. Concrete example: In Chicago, the intake tunnels extend deep into Lake Michigan, where the water is colder and less prone to biological contamination than surface water. For groundwater, collection occurs via wells. A well is drilled into an aquifer, and pumps draw water up to the surface. The depth of the well matters; deeper wells often access older, more stable water, but they require more energy to pump.

A critical aspect of collection is the protection of the watershed. If a city relies on surface water, the land surrounding the reservoir must be managed to prevent runoff from farms, factories, or urban areas. This is why many cities have strict zoning laws around their water sources. If a factory dumps chemicals into a river upstream, the city’s water treatment plant must work harder to remove those contaminants, increasing costs and risks.
