The journey of a glass of water from a distant reservoir to your kitchen tap is one of the most complex, invisible, and critical engineering feats in modern society. It is a continuous, high-stakes logistical ballet that must operate 24 hours a day, seven days a week, ensuring that billions of people have access to safe, clean, and potable water. This process, known as the urban water cycle, is not merely a linear path but a dynamic system involving hydrology, chemical engineering, structural mechanics, and rigorous regulatory compliance. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the water’s path through five distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring.

The story begins at the **source**, which is rarely a single point but rather a diverse portfolio of water bodies. Cities typically rely on surface water sources such as rivers, lakes, and reservoirs, or groundwater sources like aquifers tapped by wells. For example, New York City draws much of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, vast natural areas where rainfall and snowmelt accumulate in reservoirs. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely heavily on the Colorado River and deep groundwater wells due to the arid climate. The initial step is **collection and intake**. Large intake structures, often located in the deepest part of a reservoir to avoid surface ice or debris, use gravity or powerful pumps to draw raw water into the system. In surface water systems, trash racks—large metal grates—are installed at the intake to prevent large debris like branches, fish, or plastic from entering the pipes. This raw water, however, is far from drinkable; it contains suspended solids, organic matter, bacteria, viruses, and potentially harmful chemicals like pesticides or industrial runoff.

Once collected, the water undergoes rigorous **treatment** to transform it from a natural resource into a public health product. While processes vary based on the source quality, most modern surface water treatment plants follow a standard sequence: coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection. The first stage, **coagulation**, involves adding chemicals such as aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride to the raw water. These chemicals neutralize the negative electrical charges of suspended particles, which naturally repel each other, allowing them to clump together. This rapid mixing creates small, sticky clusters called "floc." Next, in the **flocculation** stage, the water is gently stirred in large basins.
