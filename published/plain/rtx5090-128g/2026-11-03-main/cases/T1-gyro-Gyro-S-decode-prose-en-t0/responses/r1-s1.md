A city water supply is one of the most complex and critical infrastructure systems in modern civilization. It is a continuous, multi-stage process that transforms raw, often contaminated natural resources into safe, potable water delivered to millions of households, businesses, and institutions. The journey from source to tap involves a sophisticated chain of engineering, chemistry, and logistics designed to ensure reliability, safety, and efficiency. This process can be broken down into six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Supply

The journey begins at the source, which varies depending on the geography and infrastructure of the city. Most cities rely on either surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers).

**Surface Water Collection:**
In many large metropolitan areas, such as New York City or Los Angeles, water is sourced from distant reservoirs. For example, New York City draws water from the Catskill and Delaware systems, located hundreds of miles away. These reservoirs are fed by rainfall and snowmelt. The water is collected via intake structures, which are submerged pipes or tunnels that draw water from the reservoir. These intakes are designed to avoid drawing sediment or debris from the bottom of the lake. In some cases, rivers are used directly. The Chicago River, for instance, has historically been a source, though modern systems often rely on Lake Michigan.

**Groundwater Collection:**
In regions where surface water is scarce or unreliable, cities rely on groundwater. This involves drilling wells into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing permeable rock or unconsolidated materials. Cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely heavily on groundwater pumped from deep wells. The water is extracted using pumps powered by electricity. Groundwater is often cleaner than surface water because it has been naturally filtered through soil and rock layers, but it can contain minerals like iron, manganese, or calcium, which require specific treatment steps.

**Pre-Treatment at the Source:**
Before water enters the main treatment plant, preliminary measures are taken. Intake screens prevent large debris, fish, and vegetation from entering the system. In some systems, pre-chlorination is applied at the intake to prevent algae growth and biofilm formation in the raw water pipelines.
