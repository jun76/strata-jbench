A city water supply is a complex, engineered system designed to deliver safe, potable water from natural sources to millions of households, businesses, and industrial facilities. The journey from a river, lake, or underground aquifer to the kitchen tap involves a series of critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage is essential for ensuring that the water is not only available but also safe for consumption. Below is a detailed explanation of this process, using concrete examples to illustrate how these systems function in real-world scenarios.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Water

The process begins with the collection of raw water from natural sources. Cities typically rely on surface water (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and infrastructure availability.

**Surface Water Collection:**
In many large cities, surface water is the primary source. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast reservoir systems located in upstate New York. These reservoirs collect rainwater and runoff from surrounding forests and mountains. The water flows into large lakes like the Ashokan Reservoir, where it is stored before being transported to the city. Similarly, Los Angeles draws much of its water from the Colorado River via aqueducts, while also relying on local reservoirs like Lake Powell and Lake Mead.

**Groundwater Collection:**
In areas where surface water is scarce or unreliable, groundwater is pumped from underground aquifers. For instance, Phoenix, Arizona, relies significantly on groundwater wells drilled into the Salt River aquifer. These wells extract water from porous rock layers beneath the surface. Groundwater collection requires careful monitoring to prevent over-extraction, which can lead to land subsidence or depletion of the aquifer.

**Intake Structures:**
Once the source is identified, intake structures are built to draw water into the treatment system. These may include submerged pipes, pumps, or gravity-fed channels. In New York City, the Catskill Aqueduct uses gravity to move water from the reservoirs to the city, reducing the need for energy-intensive pumping. In contrast, cities like Houston use large pumping stations to lift groundwater from wells into storage tanks.
