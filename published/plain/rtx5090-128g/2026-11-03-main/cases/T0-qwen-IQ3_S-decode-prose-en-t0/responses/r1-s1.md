The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, critical, and often invisible engineering feats of modern civilization. It is a continuous, high-stakes logistical operation that involves massive infrastructure, precise chemical engineering, rigorous biological monitoring, and sophisticated hydraulic management. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This process ensures that water is not only available but also safe, palatable, and delivered with sufficient force to reach every household, regardless of elevation or distance.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be a surface water body like a river, lake, or reservoir, or groundwater extracted from deep aquifers. The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and population density. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are protected forested areas that feed into reservoirs. In contrast, cities in arid regions like Phoenix, Arizona, rely on the Colorado River and deep groundwater wells.

Collection involves intake structures designed to draw water without sucking in debris, fish, or sediment. In surface water systems, intake pipes are often placed at specific depths to avoid surface scum or bottom sediment. For instance, the intake for the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power draws from the Colorado River via the Colorado River Aqueduct, a 242-mile system that moves water across deserts and mountains. Groundwater collection uses wells drilled hundreds of feet into the earth. These wells are lined with steel or concrete to prevent collapse and contamination. The water is then pumped into a raw water transmission main, a large-diameter pipe that acts as the highway for untreated water. This stage is purely about volume and location; the water is still "raw," containing suspended solids, organic matter, and potential pathogens.

### 2. Treatment: The Science of Purification

The specific methods vary based on the source water quality, but the general sequence is consistent.
