The journey of a glass of water from its natural origin to the kitchen faucet is one of the most complex, engineered, and vital systems in modern civilization. It is a process that involves civil engineering, chemistry, biology, and rigorous digital monitoring, all working in concert to ensure that every citizen has access to safe, clean, and reliable water. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of water through its distinct phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. While the specific infrastructure varies from city to city, the underlying principles remain remarkably consistent, whether in New York City, Tokyo, or London.

The process begins at the source, where water is collected or extracted. For many large metropolitan areas, this means surface water drawn from rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. A prime example is New York City, which relies on a vast network of reservoirs located in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, roughly 100 miles from Manhattan. These waters are captured in protected basins where sedimentation begins to occur naturally, allowing larger particles to settle to the bottom. Other cities, like Los Angeles, rely on a mix of local groundwater and imported water from distant sources, such as the Colorado River and the Sierra Nevada snowpack, transported via massive aqueducts. In contrast, cities like Phoenix depend heavily on groundwater extracted from deep aquifers through high-capacity wells. Regardless of the source, the raw water entering the system contains various impurities: suspended solids like silt and clay, organic matter, dissolved minerals, bacteria, viruses, and sometimes industrial pollutants. The goal of the initial collection phase is to gather this raw water in sufficient volume to meet the city’s peak demand, which often occurs during morning and evening hours.

Once collected, the water enters the treatment plant, a facility designed specifically to remove contaminants and make the water safe for human consumption. While treatment processes can vary based on the quality of the source water, the standard conventional treatment process involves several key steps: coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, and disinfection. The process starts with coagulation, where chemicals such as aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride are added to the water. These positively charged chemicals neutralize the negative charges of suspended particles like clay and organic matter, allowing them to clump together.
