The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, critical, and often invisible engineering feats of modern civilization. It is a continuous, high-stakes logistical operation that involves hydrology, chemistry, civil engineering, and rigorous public health protocols. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of water through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This process ensures that the water reaching your tap is not only clean and safe but also available at the right pressure and temperature, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be either surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). The choice of source depends heavily on geography and climate. For example, a city like New York relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware aqueduct systems, which transport water from mountain reservoirs hundreds of miles away. In contrast, a city like Phoenix, Arizona, relies on the Colorado River and deep groundwater wells.

The collection phase involves intake structures designed to draw water into the treatment facility while minimizing the entry of debris, fish, and sediment. In surface water systems, large screens or bar racks are installed at the intake point to prevent large objects from entering the pipes. For instance, the intake for the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power’s Colorado River Aqueduct uses massive trash racks to keep logs and other debris out of the system. In groundwater systems, collection involves drilling wells into aquifers. These wells are lined with steel or concrete to prevent contamination from surface runoff and are equipped with submersible pumps that draw water up from depths that can range from 100 to over 1,000 feet.

A critical aspect of collection is source protection. Cities often establish "source water protection areas" around reservoirs and wells, restricting industrial development, agriculture, and residential construction to prevent contamination. For example, the city of Seattle protects its watershed in the Cascade Mountains by owning the land and limiting human activity, ensuring that the water entering the system is already of high quality, reducing the burden on the treatment plant.
