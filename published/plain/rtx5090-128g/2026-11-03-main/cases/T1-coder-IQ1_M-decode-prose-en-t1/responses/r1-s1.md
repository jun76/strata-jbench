The journey of a drop of water from its natural source in a mountain spring or underground aquifer to the crisp, cold splash in a kitchen sink is one of the most complex engineering feats in modern urban infrastructure. This process, often referred to as the "water cycle" within a municipality, is not merely a sequence of pipes; it is a coordinated system involving geology, chemistry, mechanical engineering, and public health regulations. To understand how a city water supply functions, we must dissect it into its six primary operational phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each phase involves distinct challenges, specific technologies, and regulatory frameworks that ensure both the physical delivery of water and its safety for consumption.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins with the collection of raw water, often called "source water" or "raw water." Cities do not typically manufacture their own water; they harvest it from nature. The source can vary widely depending on the geographic location of the city. For instance, New York City relies heavily on reservoirs in the Catskills and Catskill Mountains, which are fed by rain and snowmelt, while Las Vegas might rely on underground aquifers, and coastal cities like Los Angeles depend on imported water from the Colorado River or Owens Valley.

The collection phase involves several sub-components: catchment, filtration of natural debris, and intake infrastructure. In the case of a mountain source like the Catskills, water flows through streams and rivers into massive reservoirs. These reservoirs are often managed by state authorities rather than the city itself, which is why New York’s water has a unique history—it was famously "stolen" from the land, as depicted in the film *Chinatown*, where the water was diverted to Manhattan.

The intake structure is a critical piece of engineering. Most cities have a "filter gallery" or a large-scale screen system at the point where water enters the municipal system. This is crucial because river water often contains macro-debris: sticks, leaves, fish, and even larger contaminants like tires or plastic bottles. If these enter the pipe network, they can clog pumps or damage chemical treatment systems. The intake usually features large concrete or metal screens with gaps no larger than a few centimeters, designed to trap solids before they reach sensitive valves.
