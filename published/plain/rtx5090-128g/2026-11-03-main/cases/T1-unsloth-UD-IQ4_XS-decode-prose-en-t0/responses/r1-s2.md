The journey of a single drop of water from its natural origin to the glass on your kitchen counter is one of the most complex, critical, and often overlooked engineering feats of modern civilization. It is a continuous, high-stakes logistical operation that involves hydrology, chemistry, civil engineering, and data science. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of water through six distinct stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. This process ensures that millions of people have access to safe, clean, and reliable water every day, often without giving it a second thought.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can vary significantly depending on the geography of the city. Sources are generally categorized as surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) or groundwater (aquifers accessed via wells). For example, a city like New York relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware aqueduct systems, which transport water from mountain reservoirs hundreds of miles away. In contrast, a city like Phoenix, Arizona, draws heavily from the Colorado River and local groundwater basins.

The collection phase involves intake structures designed to draw water into the treatment facility while minimizing the entry of debris, aquatic life, and sediment. In surface water systems, large intake pipes extend into the reservoir or river. These intakes are often equipped with trash racks and screens to prevent leaves, fish, and other large objects from entering the system. For instance, the intake structures at the Lake Mead facility in Nevada use massive cylindrical screens to filter out debris before the water is pumped uphill to treatment plants. In groundwater systems, collection involves drilling deep wells into aquifers. Pumps located at the bottom of these wells draw water up to the surface. The choice of source dictates the subsequent treatment needs; surface water typically requires more extensive treatment due to higher levels of turbidity and biological contaminants, whereas groundwater may require less treatment but often needs specific adjustments for mineral content, such as iron or manganese removal.

### 2. Treatment: The Science of Purification

Once collected, the raw water is transported to a water treatment plant, where it undergoes a rigorous multi-step purification process. The goal is to remove physical, chemical, and biological contaminants to meet strict safety standards, such as those set by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in the United States.
