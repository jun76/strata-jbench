The journey of a drop of water from a distant reservoir to the kitchen tap in a modern city is a marvel of civil engineering, public health science, and logistical precision. It is not merely a simple flow of liquid; it is a complex, multi-stage process designed to ensure that water is safe, clean, and available on demand. This system, often referred to as the "water cycle" in urban infrastructure, involves several critical phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. To understand how this works, we must trace the path of water through these stages, using concrete examples from major metropolitan systems like New York City, London, or Singapore.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Supply

The process begins at the source. Cities rarely rely on a single source; instead, they utilize a mix of surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and population density.

For example, New York City draws approximately 90% of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast, protected forested areas in upstate New York. These watersheds act as natural filters, collecting rainwater and snowmelt that flows into reservoirs like the Ashokan Reservoir. Because the water is sourced from pristine, protected lands, it requires less intensive treatment than water drawn from heavily polluted rivers. In contrast, London relies heavily on the River Thames and the River Lee. Since these rivers pass through urban areas, the water collected is more susceptible to contamination from sewage, industrial runoff, and agricultural chemicals, necessitating a more rigorous treatment process.

Groundwater collection involves drilling wells into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing rock or sediment. Cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely significantly on groundwater from the Salt River and Verde River basins. However, groundwater extraction must be managed carefully to prevent aquifer depletion and land subsidence. In many coastal cities, such as Miami, groundwater is also vulnerable to saltwater intrusion, where rising sea levels push saltwater into freshwater aquifers, rendering the water unusable without desalination.

### 2. Treatment: Making Water Safe

Once collected, raw water is transported to treatment plants. The goal of treatment is to remove contaminants, pathogens, and particulates to meet strict health standards, such as those set by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) or the World Health Organization (WHO).
