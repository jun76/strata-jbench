The journey of a drop of water from a distant reservoir to the kitchen tap in a modern city is a marvel of civil engineering, hydraulic physics, and public health science. It is not merely a simple flow of liquid; it is a complex, multi-stage process designed to ensure that water is safe, clean, and delivered with sufficient pressure to every household, regardless of elevation or distance. This system, often referred to as the "water cycle" of urban infrastructure, involves several critical phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. To understand this process fully, we must trace the path of water through these stages, using concrete examples from major metropolitan systems like New York City, London, or Singapore.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Supply

The process begins at the source. Cities rarely rely on a single source; instead, they utilize a mix of surface water (rivers, lakes, and reservoirs) and groundwater (aquifers). The choice of source depends on geography, climate, and population density.

For example, New York City draws approximately 90% of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are vast, protected forested areas in upstate New York. These watersheds act as natural filters, collecting rainwater and snowmelt that flows into reservoirs like the Ashokan Reservoir. Because the water is sourced from pristine, protected lands, it requires less intensive treatment than water drawn from heavily polluted rivers. In contrast, London relies heavily on the River Thames and the River Lee. Since these rivers pass through urban areas and agricultural lands, the water collected is more susceptible to contamination, requiring more rigorous treatment processes.

Groundwater collection involves drilling wells into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing rock or sediment. Cities like Phoenix, Arizona, rely significantly on groundwater from the Salt River and Verde River basins. However, groundwater extraction must be managed carefully to prevent aquifer depletion and land subsidence. In many coastal cities, such as Miami, groundwater is also vulnerable to saltwater intrusion, necessitating careful monitoring of water tables.

### 2. Treatment: Making Water Safe

Once collected, raw water is rarely safe for consumption. It may contain pathogens (bacteria, viruses, parasites), suspended solids, organic matter, and chemical pollutants. The treatment plant is the heart of the water supply system, where raw water is transformed into potable water.
