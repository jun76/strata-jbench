The journey of a glass of water from a natural source to a household tap is one of the most complex, engineered, and vital systems in modern civilization. It is a process that relies on precise physics, rigorous chemistry, and massive civil infrastructure to ensure that every drop delivered is safe, consistent, and available on demand. This intricate network, known as the urban water supply system, can be broken down into several critical stages: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage presents unique engineering challenges that must be solved to prevent contamination, ensure adequate pressure, and maintain public health.

The process begins with collection, which varies significantly depending on the geography and hydrology of the region. Cities primarily rely on two sources: surface water and groundwater. Surface water sources, such as lakes, rivers, and reservoirs, are collected via large intake structures. For example, in New York City, the Catskill and Delaware Aqueducts transport water hundreds of miles from mountain reservoirs to the city. These intake structures often feature massive screens to prevent large debris, fish, and invasive species like zebra mussels from entering the treatment process. In contrast, groundwater sources are accessed through deep wells drilled into aquifers. In cities like Phoenix, Arizona, where surface water is scarce, extensive wellfields pump water from underground layers of porous rock. Groundwater is often naturally cleaner but may require specific treatment to remove dissolved minerals like iron or manganese, whereas surface water requires more aggressive treatment due to exposure to pathogens, algae, and organic matter.

Once collected, the raw water undergoes a rigorous treatment process designed to remove contaminants and pathogens. This is typically the most chemically intensive phase of the water cycle. The first step is usually coagulation and flocculation. Treatment plants add chemicals, such as aluminum sulfate or ferric chloride, to the raw water. These chemicals neutralize the negative electrical charges of tiny suspended particles, causing them to stick together into larger clumps called flocs. As the water moves through large basins where it is gently stirred, these flocs grow heavier and begin to sink. Following this is sedimentation, where gravity pulls the heavy flocs to the bottom of the tanks, removing up to 90% of suspended solids. The clearer water on top moves to the next stage: filtration.
