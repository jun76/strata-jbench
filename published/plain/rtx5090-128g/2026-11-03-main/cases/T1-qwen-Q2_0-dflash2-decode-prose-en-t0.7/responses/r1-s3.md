The journey of a single drop of water from its natural source to the faucet in a modern city is a marvel of engineering, chemistry, and public health policy. It is not merely a matter of moving liquid through pipes; it is a complex, multi-stage process designed to ensure that the water is safe, reliable, and available at the precise moment a resident turns on the tap. This system involves a vast network of infrastructure that must operate seamlessly 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the water through five distinct phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, and quality monitoring.

### 1. Collection: The Source of the Supply

The process begins with the acquisition of raw water, known as "raw water." Cities do not simply tap into a nearby river or lake; they rely on a variety of sources depending on geography, climate, and population density. There are generally two types of sources: surface water and groundwater.

**Surface Water Sources**
For many major cities, such as New York City or London, surface water is the primary source. This water comes from rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. For example, New York City’s water supply is sourced from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, which are hundreds of miles north of the city. The water flows through natural channels before being captured in reservoirs. This method is efficient for large populations but requires extensive infrastructure to transport the water over long distances.

**Groundwater Sources**
In regions where surface water is scarce or contaminated, cities rely on groundwater. This involves drilling deep wells into aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing permeable rock. In cities like Phoenix, Arizona, or Los Angeles, groundwater is a critical component of the supply. However, groundwater extraction is not without challenges. Over-pumping can deplete aquifers, leading to land subsidence or saltwater intrusion in coastal areas. Therefore, sustainable collection requires careful monitoring of water tables and recharge rates.

Regardless of the source, the raw water entering the system is rarely "clean." It contains sediments, organic matter, industrial pollutants, agricultural runoff, and potentially harmful microorganisms like bacteria, viruses, and parasites. This raw water must undergo rigorous treatment before it is fit for human consumption.
