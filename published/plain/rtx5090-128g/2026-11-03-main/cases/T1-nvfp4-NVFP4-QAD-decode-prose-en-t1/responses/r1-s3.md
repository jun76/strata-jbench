A city water supply system is one of the most complex, invisible, yet critical infrastructure networks in modern urban life. It functions as a giant, automated circulatory system for a metropolis, ensuring that every household, business, and public facility receives clean, safe, and reliably pressurized water on demand. This process, often referred to as the "water cycle" from the municipal perspective, begins long before a resident turns a faucet and continues long after the water is used. To understand this feat of engineering and public health, we must trace the journey of a single drop of water from its natural source through the intricate stages of collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring.

The journey begins at the **source**, which serves as the raw material provider for the entire system. Cities typically rely on one of two primary sources: surface water or groundwater. Surface water, drawn from rivers, lakes, or reservoirs, accounts for a significant portion of municipal supplies. For example, New York City relies heavily on the Catskill and Delaware aqueducts, which capture rainwater and snowmelt from vast upstate watersheds. These large reservoirs act as natural settling basins, allowing some suspended solids to settle out before the water even reaches the treatment plant. In contrast, cities like Phoenix, Arizona, often rely on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers. Groundwater has the advantage of being naturally filtered by layers of soil and rock, but it can contain high levels of minerals, such as iron, manganese, and calcium, as well as naturally occurring contaminants like arsenic or radon. Regardless of the source, the water is never truly "clean" when it arrives at the facility; it is raw, carrying sediment, organic matter, and potential biological pathogens.

Once collected, the water undergoes rigorous **treatment** to make it potable. This process varies depending on the source quality but generally follows a standardized sequence of physical and chemical operations. The first step in surface water treatment is typically screening, where large debris like leaves, branches, and fish are removed using bar screens. Following this, the water enters coagulation and flocculation basins. Here, chemicals such as aluminum sulfate (alum) or ferric chloride are added. These coagulants neutralize the negative electrical charges of suspended particles, causing them to stick together and form larger clumps known as "flocs."
