The journey of water from a natural source to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most critical and complex engineering feats of modern civilization. It is a continuous, multi-stage process that requires vast infrastructure, precise chemical control, and rigorous monitoring to ensure public health. This system, often referred to as the "water cycle" of a municipality, transforms raw, potentially contaminated water into safe, potable drinking water. To understand this process in detail, we must trace the path of a single drop of water through six distinct phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

### 1. Collection: The Source and Intake

The process begins at the source, which can be a surface water body, such as a lake or river, or a groundwater aquifer. Each source presents unique challenges. For example, a city like Chicago relies on Lake Michigan, a massive surface water source that is relatively clean but susceptible to algal blooms and runoff from rainstorms. In contrast, many cities in the Southwest United States, such as Phoenix, rely heavily on the Colorado River or deep wells tapping into the Ogallala Aquifer.

At the source, large intake structures, known as intakes, draw raw water into the system. These intakes are often located offshore or deep within the water body to avoid surface debris and ice. A critical component here is the **traveling screen**, a large, mesh-covered belt that moves continuously through the water. As the belt passes through the water, it scoops up water while filtering out large debris like leaves, fish, and logs. This prevents clogging in the downstream pipes. In some cases, pre-treatment occurs here; for instance, if the water source is prone to high levels of algae, operators might add copper sulfate or chlorine at the intake to kill algae before the water reaches the treatment plant, preventing taste and odor issues later.
