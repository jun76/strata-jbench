The journey of water from its natural source to the faucet in your kitchen is one of the most complex, critical, and engineered systems in modern urban infrastructure. It is a continuous cycle of collection, purification, storage, distribution, and monitoring that ensures public health and daily convenience. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the path of a single drop of water, examining the engineering marvels and regulatory safeguards at each stage. This process transforms raw, potentially hazardous environmental water into the clean, pressurized, and safe potable water that flows through millions of homes and businesses.

### Collection and Source Protection

The process begins long before water enters a treatment plant. It starts in the watershed, the land area that drains into a specific river, lake, or aquifer. For a city like New York, the source might be the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, vast reservoirs located hundreds of miles north of the city. For a city like Los Angeles, it may involve a complex network of rivers, reservoirs, and imported water from the Colorado River and State Water Project. In contrast, cities like Phoenix rely heavily on groundwater pumped from deep aquifers beneath the desert floor.

The first step is extraction. If the source is a surface water body like a lake or river, water is drawn through large intake structures. These intakes often include screens to prevent fish, debris, and large particulates from entering the system. For example, Chicago draws its water from Lake Michigan via massive intake cribs located miles offshore. These structures are designed to draw water from deeper, cooler, and cleaner layers of the lake, avoiding surface contamination and algae blooms. If the source is groundwater, wells are drilled hundreds of feet into the earth. Pumps then lift this water to the surface. Groundwater is generally easier to treat than surface water because it has been naturally filtered through layers of soil and rock, but it can contain high levels of minerals like iron, manganese, or naturally occurring arsenic, which must be removed during treatment.

### Treatment: The Science of Purification

Once raw water is collected, it is transported to a water treatment plant. The goal of treatment is to remove physical, chemical, and biological contaminants to meet strict safety standards set by agencies like the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in the United States.
