The journey of a drop of water from a distant reservoir to a household faucet is a marvel of modern engineering, public health science, and logistical planning. While turning a tap seems instantaneous, the infrastructure supporting this act is a complex, multi-stage system that spans hundreds of miles and involves rigorous chemical, biological, and mechanical processes. To understand how a city water supply works, we must trace the lifecycle of water through six critical phases: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

### 1. Collection: Securing the Source

The process begins with **collection**, where the city identifies and secures a reliable source of raw water. Sources vary significantly based on geography and population size. In many modern cities, such as Seattle or Portland, the source is surface water collected from rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. In contrast, cities like Phoenix or Austin rely heavily on groundwater extracted from deep aquifers via wells. In coastal or arid regions, such as San Diego or Tel Aviv, desalination plants treat seawater as the primary source.

Once the source is identified, the collection infrastructure is built. For surface water, this often involves intake structures located in large reservoirs. A concrete example is the Colorado River, which serves millions of people in the western United States. Intake pipes draw raw water from the river, filtering out large debris like branches, leaves, and animals through coarse screens. This initial screening is crucial because it protects downstream pumps from physical damage and reduces the organic load that would otherwise complicate later treatment stages.

For groundwater sources, wells are drilled hundreds of feet into the earth. Pumps installed in these wells lift the water to the surface. In cities like London, which historically relied on the Thames, the transition to groundwater and filtered river water required massive civil engineering projects to ensure sufficient volume during droughts. Regardless of the source, the collected water is referred to as "raw water," and it is almost never suitable for drinking in its natural state due to the presence of sediments, bacteria, pathogens, and dissolved minerals.

### 2. Treatment: Purifying the Water
