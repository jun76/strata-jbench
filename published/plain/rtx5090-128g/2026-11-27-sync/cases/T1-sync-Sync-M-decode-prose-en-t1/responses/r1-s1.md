A city’s water supply system is arguably one of the most complex and vital pieces of infrastructure in modern civilization. It is a continuous, engineered network that transforms raw natural resources into safe, potable water delivered to millions of consumers on demand. The journey from the source to the tap is a multi-stage process that relies on sophisticated engineering, rigorous science, and constant monitoring. To understand how this works, we must examine the entire lifecycle of water: collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring, using concrete examples such as New York City, Los Angeles, and London to illustrate these principles.

### 1. Collection: The Source of Water

The journey begins with collection, which involves identifying and securing a reliable water source. Sources are generally categorized into two types: surface water and groundwater.

**Surface Water Collection**
Most large cities rely on surface water, which includes rivers, lakes, and reservoirs. For example, New York City’s water supply is primarily sourced from upstate reservoirs like the Catskill and Delaware systems. These reservoirs are created by damming rivers, allowing the water to settle naturally. The Catskill Aqueduct, a gravity-fed pipeline stretching over 150 miles, transports untreated water from the Ashokan Reservoir to storage facilities in Brooklyn. Gravity is a key component here; because the reservoir is at a higher elevation than the city, the water flows downhill without the need for pumping, significantly reducing energy costs.

In contrast, cities like Los Angeles, which sit in a semi-arid region, rely on imported surface water. The Colorado River Aqueduct brings water over 240 miles across deserts and mountains to Southern California. This requires massive pumping stations to lift water over ridges, demonstrating that collection infrastructure is often energy-intensive when geography is unfavorable.

**Groundwater Collection**
Some cities, or suburbs, rely on groundwater drawn from aquifers—underground layers of water-bearing rock or sediment. Cities like Austin, Texas, or portions of Phoenix, Arizona, utilize wells that pump water from deep aquifers. This water is often naturally filtered through layers of soil and rock, providing a high initial quality. However, over-pumping can lead to land subsidence or depletion, requiring careful management.
