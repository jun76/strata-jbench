A city water supply is one of the most complex yet essential public infrastructure systems. It is designed to collect water from natural or engineered sources, treat it to safe drinking-water standards, store it for reliability, distribute it through an extensive pipe network, manage pressure so water reaches every consumer, and continuously monitor quality from source to tap. Although the exact design varies by city depending on geography, climate, population, and available water sources, the basic journey is similar almost everywhere.

Below is a detailed explanation of how a modern urban water supply works, from the source to the tap.

---

## 1. Water Sources and Collection

The first step in any city water supply is finding a reliable source of water. Cities typically use one or more of the following:

### Surface water
Surface water comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. Many large cities depend on rivers or reservoirs because they can provide large volumes of water.

For example:
- New York City gets much of its water from reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds.
- London obtains a significant share of its water from the River Thames and River Lee.
- Tokyo relies heavily on rivers such as the Tone and Tama river systems.

Surface water is relatively easy to collect in large quantities, but it is more vulnerable to pollution from agriculture, industry, urban runoff, sewage, and storms. It also varies with seasons: droughts can reduce river flows and reservoir levels, while heavy rain can increase turbidity and wash pollutants into the source.

### Groundwater
Groundwater is stored underground in aquifers, which are layers of porous rock, sand, or gravel that hold water. Cities access groundwater through wells.

Examples:
- Many cities in India, such as Bengaluru and Chennai, have historically relied heavily on groundwater.
- Copenhagen uses a large proportion of groundwater for its drinking water.
- Parts of Los Angeles use groundwater basins, though the city also imports water from distant sources.

Groundwater is often naturally filtered as it moves through soil and rock, so it may require less treatment than surface water. However, it can be contaminated by sewage leaks, industrial chemicals, fertilizers, road salt, or naturally occurring substances such as arsenic or fluoride. Overpumping can also cause land subsidence or saltwater intrusion in coastal cities.
