A city water supply is one of the most complex yet essential public infrastructure systems. It is designed to collect water from natural or engineered sources, treat it to safe drinking-water standards, store it for reliability, distribute it through a vast network of pipes, maintain adequate pressure, and continuously monitor quality from source to tap. Although the exact design varies by city—depending on geography, climate, population, topography, and available water sources—the basic journey is similar everywhere.

Below is a detailed explanation of how a modern urban water supply works, using concrete examples along the way.

## 1. Water Sources and Collection

The first step is obtaining raw water. Cities typically use one or more of the following sources:

### Surface water
Surface water comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. Many large cities rely on reservoirs because they can store water and provide a relatively stable supply.

For example, New York City gets much of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located more than 100 miles from the city. The water travels by gravity through large aqueducts to the city. Because the watershed is carefully managed and largely undeveloped, the raw water is relatively clean, though it still requires treatment.

London, by contrast, draws a significant share of its water from the River Thames and its tributaries. River water is more variable: it can be affected by rainfall, agricultural runoff, sewage discharges, and urban pollution. It therefore requires more intensive treatment.

### Groundwater
Groundwater is stored underground in aquifers, layers of porous rock, sand, or gravel. Cities access it through wells. Groundwater is often naturally filtered as it moves through soil and rock, so it may require less treatment than surface water. However, it can contain dissolved minerals such as iron, manganese, calcium, arsenic, or nitrates.

Cities such as Amsterdam and much of Florida rely heavily on groundwater. In Florida, limestone aquifers store large volumes of water, but they are vulnerable to saltwater intrusion if too much is pumped near the coast.

### Desalination
In arid coastal cities, seawater desalination may be used. This process removes salt and impurities from seawater, usually through reverse osmosis. Cities such as Dubai, Singapore, and parts of California use desalination as part of their water portfolio.
