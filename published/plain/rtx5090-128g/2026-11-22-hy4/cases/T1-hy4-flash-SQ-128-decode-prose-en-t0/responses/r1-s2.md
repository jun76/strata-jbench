A city water supply is one of the most complex yet essential public systems. It is designed to collect water from natural or engineered sources, treat it to safe drinking-water standards, store enough water to meet changing demand, distribute it through a vast network of pipes, maintain adequate pressure, and continuously monitor quality from source to tap. Although the exact design varies by city, the basic journey is similar everywhere: source water is captured, transported, purified, stored, pumped, and delivered to homes, businesses, hospitals, schools, and industries.

Below is a detailed explanation of how a modern urban water supply works, using concrete examples along the way.

---

## 1. Water Sources and Collection

The first step is finding and collecting raw water. Cities usually rely on one or more of the following sources:

### Surface water
Surface water comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. Many large cities depend on reservoirs because they can store large volumes and provide a relatively stable supply.

For example, New York City gets much of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located more than 100 miles from the city. The water travels by gravity through large aqueducts to the city. Because the watershed is carefully managed and largely forested, the source water is relatively clean, though it still requires treatment.

London, by contrast, draws a significant share of its water from the River Thames and its tributaries. River water is more variable: it can be affected by rainfall, agricultural runoff, sewage discharges, and urban pollution. Therefore, it requires more intensive treatment.

### Groundwater
Groundwater is stored underground in aquifers, layers of porous rock, sand, or gravel. Cities access it through wells. Groundwater is often naturally filtered as it moves through soil and rock, so it may require less treatment than surface water. However, it can contain dissolved minerals such as iron, manganese, calcium, arsenic, or nitrates.

For example, many cities in the American Midwest use groundwater from glacial aquifers. In India, cities such as Bengaluru historically relied heavily on groundwater, though rapid growth has led to over-extraction and depletion in many areas.
