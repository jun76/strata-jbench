A modern city water supply is a vast, interconnected system designed to move water from natural sources to millions of taps safely, reliably, and at the right pressure. Although the water that emerges from a faucet may seem simple, getting it there involves careful engineering, continuous monitoring, and multiple layers of treatment and control. A useful way to understand the system is to follow the journey of water through several stages: source collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring. Each stage protects public health and ensures that water is available not only during normal conditions but also during droughts, storms, equipment failures, and emergencies.

## 1. Water Sources and Collection

The first step is finding a dependable source of water. Cities usually rely on a combination of surface water and groundwater.

**Surface water** comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. Large cities often depend on reservoirs because they can store water from rainy seasons or snowmelt for use during dry periods. For example, New York City receives much of its drinking water from reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located outside the city. Rain and snowmelt collect in these watersheds, flow into streams, and are impounded behind dams. The city then draws water from the reservoirs through intake structures.

**Groundwater** comes from underground aquifers, which are layers of porous rock, sand, or gravel that hold water. Water is extracted through wells equipped with pumps. Many smaller cities and suburbs rely heavily on groundwater. For instance, parts of California’s Central Valley and many Midwestern towns use groundwater for municipal supply. Groundwater is often naturally filtered as it moves through soil and rock, but it can still contain minerals, bacteria, or contaminants such as nitrates from fertilizer or industrial chemicals.

Some cities also use **reclaimed water**, desalinated seawater, or imported water. Coastal cities such as San Diego have invested in desalination and water recycling to reduce dependence on imported supplies. Singapore, which has limited freshwater resources, uses a diversified strategy known as the “Four Water Taps”: local catchment water, imported water, reclaimed water, and desalinated water.

Collection infrastructure depends on the source. A reservoir system may include dams, spillways, intake towers, gates, and pipelines. An intake tower has openings at different depths so operators can draw water from the best-quality layer.
