A city water supply is a vast, interconnected system that moves water from a natural source to millions of individual taps while keeping it safe, reliable, and at the right pressure. Although the exact design varies by city, most systems follow the same basic sequence: **source water collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring**. A useful way to understand the system is to follow a single drop of water from a river, lake, aquifer, or reservoir to a kitchen sink.

## 1. Source water collection: where the water comes from

The first step is obtaining raw water. Cities usually rely on one or more of the following sources:

### Surface water
Surface water comes from rivers, lakes, streams, and reservoirs. Many large cities depend on reservoirs because they can store water during wet periods and release it during dry periods. For example, New York City gets much of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds. These watersheds are carefully managed to reduce pollution from farming, development, and industry.

A river-based example is London, which draws a significant portion of its water from the River Thames and its tributaries. Because rivers can carry sediment, bacteria, agricultural runoff, and urban pollutants, river water usually requires more intensive treatment than protected reservoir water.

### Groundwater
Other cities use groundwater from aquifers, which are underground layers of water-bearing rock or sediment. Water is extracted through wells. Groundwater is often naturally filtered as it moves through soil and rock, so it may require less treatment than surface water. However, it can contain dissolved minerals such as iron, manganese, arsenic, or calcium, and it can be vulnerable to contamination from septic systems, landfills, road salt, or industrial sites.

For instance, many cities in the American Midwest rely heavily on groundwater. In places such as Minneapolis or Des Moines, wells tap buried aquifers, and treatment may focus on removing iron, hardness, or naturally occurring contaminants.

### Desalination and alternative sources
In arid coastal cities, seawater desalination may be used. Cities such as Dubai, Jeddah, and parts of California use reverse osmosis to remove salt from seawater. This is energy-intensive but can provide a drought-proof supply. Some cities also use recycled water for non-drinking purposes such as irrigation, toilet flushing, and industrial cooling.
