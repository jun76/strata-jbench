A modern city water supply is a vast, interconnected system that moves water from natural sources to millions of taps while keeping it safe, reliable, and at the right pressure. Although the exact design varies by city, most systems follow the same basic sequence: source selection and collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring. Below is a detailed explanation of each stage, with concrete examples.

## 1. Sources and collection: where city water comes from

City water usually comes from one or more of the following sources:

- **Surface water**: rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams  
- **Groundwater**: aquifers accessed through wells  
- **Imported water**: water transported from another region through aqueducts or pipelines  
- **Recycled or reclaimed water**: treated wastewater used for non-drinking purposes such as irrigation or industrial cooling  
- **Desalinated water**: seawater converted to freshwater, common in arid coastal cities

Many large cities use a combination of sources to reduce risk. For example, New York City gets most of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, with the Croton system as an additional source. Los Angeles relies on imported water from the Colorado River and Northern California, local groundwater, recycled water, and increasingly desalination and stormwater capture. Singapore uses local reservoirs, imported Malaysian water, recycled water known as NEWater, and desalination.

### Surface water collection

Surface water is collected from rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. A reservoir is often created by damming a river to store water during wet periods for use during dry periods. Cities may own or manage large watersheds to protect water quality.

For example, a city might draw water from a reservoir located in a forested watershed upstream. The water flows by gravity through an intake structure into a raw water pipeline. The intake usually has screens to keep out large debris such as leaves, branches, fish, and trash. Larger systems may have multiple intakes at different depths because water quality can vary depending on season, temperature, and algae growth.

A concrete example is the Thames Water system in London. Some of London’s water comes from the River Thames and River Lee. Water is abstracted at intake points and then sent to treatment works. Because rivers can carry pollutants from urban and agricultural areas, treatment must be robust.

### Groundwater collection

Groundwater is stored underground in porous rock or sediment layers called aquifers. Cities collect it through wells.
