A city water supply is a vast, interconnected system that moves water from a natural source to millions of individual taps while keeping it safe, reliable, and at the right pressure. Although the exact design varies by city, most systems follow the same basic sequence: **source water collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring**. A useful way to understand the system is to follow a single drop of water on its journey.

Imagine a city of two million people located beside a large river and a set of hills. The city draws water from the river upstream, treats it at a central plant, stores it in reservoirs, pumps it through trunk mains and neighborhood pipes, and finally delivers it into homes, schools, hospitals, and businesses. At every stage, operators monitor quantity, pressure, and water quality.

## 1. Source water collection: where the water comes from

The first step is obtaining raw water from a source. Cities typically use one or more of the following:

- **Surface water**: rivers, lakes, and reservoirs  
- **Groundwater**: aquifers accessed through wells  
- **Imported water**: water transported from another region through aqueducts or pipelines  
- **Desalinated water**: seawater treated to remove salt, common in arid coastal cities  
- **Recycled water**: treated wastewater used for non-potable purposes such as irrigation or industrial cooling

Many large cities use a combination. For example, New York City gets much of its drinking water from protected reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds. Los Angeles imports water from distant sources such as the Colorado River and Northern California, while also using groundwater and recycled water. Singapore uses local reservoirs, imported Malaysian water, and desalination, reflecting its limited land and high population density.

### Surface water collection

If the city uses a river, engineers install **intake structures** at carefully selected locations. These structures may include screens that block large debris such as branches, plastic, and fish. The intake must be placed where the water is relatively reliable in quantity and quality. Cities often avoid intakes near industrial discharges, heavily urbanized stretches, or areas prone to contamination.

For example, a river intake might consist of a submerged pipe connected to a pumping station. Large bar screens remove coarse debris, and smaller traveling screens remove finer material.
