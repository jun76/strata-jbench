A modern city water supply is a vast, interconnected system that moves water from a natural source to millions of taps while keeping it safe, reliable, and at the right pressure. Although the exact design varies from city to city, most systems follow the same basic sequence: **source water collection**, **treatment**, **storage**, **distribution**, **pressure management**, and **continuous quality monitoring**. Below is a detailed explanation of each stage, with concrete examples.

---

## 1. Water Sources and Collection

The first step is obtaining water from a dependable source. Cities usually rely on one or more of the following:

### Surface water
Surface water comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. Many large cities depend on reservoirs because they can store water during wet seasons and release it during dry periods.

For example, **New York City** gets much of its water from the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, including large reservoirs such as Ashokan and Pepacton Reservoirs. These watersheds are protected to reduce pollution from farms, roads, and development. Water travels by gravity through tunnels and aqueducts toward the city.

Another example is **London**, which draws a significant share of its water from the River Thames and River Lee. Because rivers can contain urban runoff, sewage discharges, and industrial pollutants, river-based systems usually require more intensive treatment.

### Groundwater
Groundwater is stored underground in aquifers and is withdrawn through wells. It is often naturally filtered as it passes through soil and rock, so it may require less treatment than surface water. However, it can contain dissolved minerals, arsenic, nitrates, or contaminants from leaking tanks and septic systems.

Many cities and towns use groundwater extensively. For instance, parts of **Los Angeles County** rely on groundwater basins, while cities in the American Midwest often use aquifers. In India, many urban areas supplement municipal supplies with groundwater, though over-extraction can cause land subsidence and saltwater intrusion in coastal regions.

### Desalination
In dry coastal cities, seawater desalination may be used. Desalination removes salt through reverse osmosis or distillation. It is energy-intensive but can provide a drought-proof supply.

For example, **Singapore** imports water from Malaysia, collects rainwater, recycles wastewater into high-quality reclaimed water known as NEWater, and operates desalination plants. This diversified approach is often called the “Four Water Taps” strategy.
