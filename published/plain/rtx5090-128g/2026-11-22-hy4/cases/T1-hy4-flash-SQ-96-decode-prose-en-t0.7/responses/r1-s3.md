A city water supply is one of the most complex and essential public infrastructure systems. It must collect water from natural or engineered sources, treat it to safe drinking standards, store enough to meet changing demand, distribute it through a vast pipe network, maintain adequate pressure from low-rise homes to high-rise towers, and continuously monitor quality from source to tap. A useful way to understand the system is to follow a single drop of water on its journey.

Imagine a mid-sized city of one million people located beside a river and an underground aquifer. The city draws most of its water from the river upstream of town, with a smaller share from wells during dry periods. Every day, the utility may need to deliver around 150–250 liters per person, depending on climate, household habits, commercial use, and losses in the network. That means the system must reliably move hundreds of millions of liters per day.

## 1. Collection: taking water from the source

The first step is **collection**, also called raw water abstraction. Cities use different sources depending on geography: rivers, lakes, reservoirs, groundwater aquifers, springs, desalination plants, or imported water from distant basins.

### Surface water collection

For a river-based system, the utility usually builds an **intake structure** upstream of the city. This structure must be placed where water quantity and quality are favorable. Engineers avoid areas near industrial discharges, heavily urbanized stretches, or tributaries carrying polluted runoff.

A typical river intake includes:

- **Screens** to block leaves, branches, plastic, fish, and debris.
- **Submersible or vertical turbine pumps** to lift water.
- **Intake pipes or channels** leading to a raw water pumping station.
- **Flow meters and valves** to control how much water is withdrawn.

For example, a city might have two intake points: one upstream main intake and one emergency intake farther upstream. This redundancy matters because if a chemical spill occurs downstream of one intake, operators can reduce or stop withdrawal and switch to another source.

Lakes and reservoirs require different intake designs. Because water quality can vary by depth, utilities often use **selective-depth intakes**. In summer, a lake may stratify: warm water at the surface, colder water below. The bottom layer can become low in oxygen and contain higher concentrations of manganese, iron, or nutrients. A floating or multi-level intake allows operators to draw from the best layer.
