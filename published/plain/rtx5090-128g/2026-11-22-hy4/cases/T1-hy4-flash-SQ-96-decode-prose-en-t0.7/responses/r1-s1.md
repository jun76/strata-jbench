A city water supply is a vast, interconnected system that moves water from a natural source to millions of taps while keeping it safe, reliable, and at the right pressure. Although the exact design varies by city, most systems follow the same basic sequence: **source water collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and continuous quality monitoring**. Below is a detailed explanation of each stage, with concrete examples.

---

## 1. Source Water Collection: Where the Water Comes From

The first step is obtaining water from a dependable source. Cities usually use one or more of the following:

### Surface water
Surface water comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. Many large cities rely on reservoirs because they can store water during wet seasons and release it during dry periods.

**Example:** New York City gets much of its water from reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located outside the city. These reservoirs collect rainfall and snowmelt from large protected watersheds. The water then flows by gravity through tunnels and aqueducts toward the city.

Surface water is generally easier to collect in large volumes, but it is more exposed to pollution from runoff, agriculture, wildlife, and human activity. It usually requires substantial treatment.

### Groundwater
Groundwater is stored underground in aquifers, which are porous layers of rock, sand, or gravel. Cities access it through wells.

**Example:** Many cities in Florida and parts of California rely heavily on groundwater. In Florida, the Floridan Aquifer supplies large quantities of water to homes, farms, and businesses. In California, groundwater basins are especially important during droughts when surface water supplies are reduced.

Groundwater is often naturally filtered as it moves through soil and rock, so it may require less treatment than surface water. However, it can contain dissolved minerals, arsenic, nitrates, or contaminants from septic systems, fertilizers, and industrial activity.

### Desalination and alternative sources
Some cities use desalination, especially where freshwater is scarce. Desalination removes salt from seawater through reverse osmosis or distillation.

**Example:** San Diego has invested in desalination to reduce dependence on imported water. Cities in the Middle East, such as Dubai and Abu Dhabi, rely heavily on desalinated seawater.
