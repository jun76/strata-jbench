A city water supply is a chain of systems that moves water from a natural or engineered source to the tap in a home, school, hospital, or business. The chain usually includes **collection**, **treatment**, **storage**, **distribution**, **pressure management**, and **quality monitoring**. Each stage must work reliably because a break at any point can affect thousands or millions of people.

Below is a detailed explanation of how a typical modern urban water supply works, using concrete examples.

---

## 1. Source and collection: where the water comes from

A city’s water supply begins with a source. The source may be:

- A river or lake  
- A reservoir  
- Groundwater wells  
- Springs  
- Rainwater harvesting in some regions  
- Desalinated seawater in coastal cities  
- Imported water transported from another region  

Many large cities use more than one source to reduce risk. For example, a city might rely on a river during normal years but switch partly to groundwater during drought.

### Surface water collection

If the city uses a river or lake, water is collected through an intake structure. This is often a large pipe or channel built near the riverbank or lake shore. The intake may include screens to keep out fish, leaves, branches, and large debris.

For example, imagine a city of two million people located beside a river. The city’s water utility may operate an intake on the river upstream of the urban area. The intake draws water into a pumping station. Before the water enters the treatment plant, large screens remove debris. The pumps then push the raw water through pipes to the treatment facility.

Reservoirs are also common. A reservoir is a lake created by damming a river or stream. It stores water from rainfall and snowmelt. Cities often prefer reservoirs because they can hold water during dry periods. For instance, New York City gets much of its drinking water from reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located far outside the city. The water travels by aqueducts to the city and is treated before distribution.

### Groundwater collection

Other cities rely on wells. Groundwater is water stored underground in aquifers. Wells pump this water to the surface. For example, many smaller cities and towns use groundwater because it is often naturally filtered through soil and rock. However, groundwater can contain minerals such as iron, manganese, calcium, or arsenic, so it still needs testing and treatment.
