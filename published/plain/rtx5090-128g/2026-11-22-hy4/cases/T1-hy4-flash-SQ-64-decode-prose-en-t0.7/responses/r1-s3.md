A city water supply is a engineered system that moves water from a natural or imported source to millions of taps while keeping it safe, palatable, and available at the right pressure. Although the details vary by city, the basic chain is the same: **source → collection → treatment → storage → distribution → pressure management → quality monitoring → consumer tap**. Below is a detailed walkthrough with concrete examples.

---

## 1. Source: where the water comes from

A city’s water supply usually begins with one or more of the following sources:

### Surface water
Rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams are common sources. For example:
- New York City gets much of its drinking water from reservoirs in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds.
- London relies heavily on the River Thames and River Lee.
- Toronto draws lake water from Lake Ontario.

Surface water is vulnerable to pollution from farms, factories, storm runoff, and human activity, so it almost always requires substantial treatment.

### Groundwater
Many cities use wells tapping aquifers. Examples:
- Phoenix, Arizona, uses groundwater from underground aquifers as part of its supply.
- Bangalore, India, historically depended heavily on borewells and groundwater.
- Many smaller cities and towns rely on groundwater because it can be relatively clean and inexpensive to pump.

Groundwater may contain minerals such as iron, manganese, calcium, fluoride, arsenic, or nitrates, depending on local geology and land use.

### Imported or recycled sources
Some cities import water from distant basins or use reclaimed water for non-potable purposes:
- Los Angeles imports water from the Colorado River and from Northern California via aqueducts.
- Singapore imports water from Malaysia and also uses “NEWater,” highly treated reclaimed water, mostly for industrial and non-potable use but sometimes blended into the supply.
- Israel uses a high proportion of recycled wastewater for agriculture, freeing freshwater for urban use.

A modern city often uses a **mix** of sources to reduce risk. If a drought affects one source, another can compensate.
