A city water supply is a engineered system that moves water from a natural source to every household, business, and public tap. It typically includes several linked stages: **source collection**, **treatment**, **storage**, **distribution**, **pressure management**, and **quality monitoring**. A useful concrete example is a mid-sized city—call it “Riverton,” population 500,000—that draws water from a river and a groundwater aquifer. Riverton’s system shows how each stage works in practice.

## 1. Source collection: where the water comes from

A city first needs a dependable source. Common sources include rivers, lakes, reservoirs, groundwater wells, and sometimes desalination plants. Riverton uses two sources:

- **River water** from the Cedar River, which supplies about 70% of the city’s water.
- **Groundwater** from an aquifer beneath the city, supplying about 30%.

### Surface-water collection

For the Cedar River, Riverton operates an **intake structure** on the riverbank. The intake is a screened opening that draws water into a pipeline leading to the treatment plant. Screens keep out large debris such as branches, leaves, trash, and fish. The intake may also have smaller strainers to remove sand and grit.

For example, in spring, heavy rain can wash sediment into the river. The intake screens prevent logs and plastic bottles from entering the plant, while a grit-removal step later settles out heavier particles. Operators also monitor river flow. If the river is too low, they may draw more from wells; if it is high and muddy, they may rely more on stored water.

### Groundwater collection

Riverton’s groundwater comes from a network of **production wells**. Each well has a pump that lifts water from the aquifer. The wells are spaced around the city to avoid overdrawing one area. Groundwater often has fewer large debris problems than river water, but it can contain dissolved minerals, iron, manganese, arsenic, nitrates, or hardness.

For example, one Riverton well field has water with high iron content. The utility sends that water to a treatment plant where iron is oxidized and filtered out. Another well field has naturally hard water, so the city blends it with softer river water to reduce scale buildup in pipes and household appliances.
