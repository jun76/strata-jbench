A city water supply is one of the most complex yet taken-for-granted public systems in modern life. When someone turns a tap, the water that appears has usually traveled many miles, passed through multiple treatment stages, been stored in tanks or reservoirs, and been pushed through a vast network of pipes by carefully controlled pressure. A reliable water supply requires coordination among engineers, operators, laboratory technicians, emergency planners, and regulators. The system must deliver enough water for drinking, cooking, bathing, firefighting, industry, and sanitation while keeping it safe from contamination. Below is a detailed explanation of how a city water supply works from source to tap, with concrete examples.

## 1. Water sources: where the supply begins

City water systems use two broad categories of sources: surface water and groundwater.

**Surface water** comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and streams. Many large cities rely on reservoirs because they can store large volumes and provide a relatively consistent supply. For example, New York City gets much of its drinking water from protected reservoir systems in the Catskill and Delaware watersheds, located well outside the city. The water travels by gravity through large aqueducts toward the city. London, by contrast, draws heavily from the River Thames and its tributaries, which means the water must undergo more intensive treatment because rivers can contain urban runoff, sewage discharges, agricultural pollutants, and debris.

**Groundwater** comes from underground aquifers, accessed through wells. Cities or towns with porous rock or sand formations may use groundwater because it is often naturally filtered as it moves through soil and rock. For example, many communities in the central United States rely on aquifers, and parts of California use groundwater basins, especially during dry years. Groundwater can be high quality, but it may also contain naturally occurring minerals such as arsenic, fluoride, iron, manganese, or hardness-causing calcium and magnesium. It can also be contaminated by septic systems, fertilizers, industrial chemicals, or road salt.

Some cities use a mix of both. Singapore, for instance, imports water from Malaysia, collects rainwater, treats wastewater, and has developed desalination. This mix is called “water diversification” and is designed to reduce vulnerability to drought, political disputes, or contamination events.

The choice of source affects the entire treatment process. Reservoir water may be relatively clean but can develop algae blooms. River water may carry sediment, bacteria, and pollution. Groundwater may be clear but mineral-heavy. Each source requires a different treatment strategy.
