A city water supply is a chain of systems that moves water from a natural or engineered source to the tap in a home, school, hospital, or business. The chain usually includes **collection**, **treatment**, **storage**, **distribution**, **pressure management**, and **quality monitoring**. Each stage is designed to make water safe, reliable, and available at the right pressure. A useful way to understand the system is to follow one “drop” of water through a typical city.

Imagine a mid-sized city, “Riverton,” with 300,000 residents. Riverton gets much of its water from a river and a reservoir upstream, with groundwater wells as a backup during drought. On a normal day, the drop begins in the reservoir, travels through an intake structure, enters a treatment plant, is filtered and disinfected, moves into a storage tank, flows through miles of pipes, is boosted by pumps, and finally emerges from a kitchen faucet.

## 1. Collection: getting water from the source

The first step is **collection**, also called source water abstraction. Cities collect water from surface water sources such as rivers, lakes, and reservoirs, or from groundwater sources such as aquifers accessed through wells.

### Surface water collection

Riverton’s main source is a reservoir about 25 miles outside the city. The reservoir is fed by rainfall, snowmelt, and a river. At the reservoir, a **intake structure** draws water from a controlled depth. This matters because water quality can vary by depth and season. In summer, warmer surface water may contain more algae, while deeper water may be cooler and clearer. Operators can choose intake depth to reduce problems such as algae, sediment, or taste-and-odor compounds.

A large pipe, sometimes called a **raw water intake line** or **raw water conduit**, carries the water to the treatment plant. In Riverton, this pipe is 36 inches in diameter and gravity-fed because the reservoir is at a higher elevation than the plant. In other cities, raw water may need to be pumped uphill or across long distances. For example, a city drawing water from a river may use large intake pumps at a riverbank, screened to keep out fish, leaves, and debris.

Surface water collection often includes screening at the intake. Screens can be coarse bar screens that remove large debris, or finer screens that prevent smaller organic material from entering the plant. This protects pumps and treatment equipment.
