A city water supply is a chain of systems that moves water from a natural or engineered source to the tap in a home, school, hospital, or business. The chain usually includes **source water collection**, **treatment**, **storage**, **distribution**, **pressure management**, and **quality monitoring**. Each stage is designed to make water safe, reliable, and available at the right pressure. A useful way to understand the system is to follow one drop of water through a typical city.

For example, imagine a city of 500,000 people that draws water from a river and an underground aquifer. The river supplies most of the water during wet months, while groundwater provides backup during droughts. The city’s water utility operates intake structures, a treatment plant, reservoirs, pumping stations, miles of pipes, and a laboratory that tests water quality. The goal is not simply to move water, but to deliver water that meets health standards at every tap.

## 1. Source water collection

The first step is finding and collecting raw water. Cities use several kinds of sources: rivers, lakes, reservoirs, springs, wells, and sometimes recycled or desalinated water.

A river-based system might use an **intake structure** on the riverbank. This structure contains screens that keep out large debris such as branches, leaves, fish, and trash. Water is then pumped or directed into a channel leading to the treatment plant. For instance, a city on a large river may have an intake that can draw 100 million liters per day. Operators monitor river flow, turbidity, and pollution upstream because storms can wash soil, pesticides, and sewage into the river.

A lake or reservoir source often uses a submerged intake pipe. The utility may draw water from a certain depth because deeper water is usually cooler and clearer. For example, a reservoir intake might be located 10 meters below the surface to avoid floating algae and warm surface water. In some reservoirs, operators can choose among several intake depths depending on seasonal conditions.

Groundwater systems collect water through wells. A well field may contain dozens of wells spread across an aquifer. Each well has a pump that lifts water to the surface. For example, a city might operate 30 wells, each producing 1,000 to 3,000 liters per minute. Groundwater often has fewer visible particles than river water, but it can contain dissolved minerals such as iron, manganese, calcium, arsenic, or nitrate. Therefore, treatment needs differ depending on the source.
