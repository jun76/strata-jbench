A city water supply is a connected system that moves water from a natural source—such as a river, lake, reservoir, aquifer, or coastal seawater—through treatment, storage, and distribution networks, and finally to a consumer’s tap. The system must deliver enough water of acceptable quality, at adequate pressure, every hour of every day. To understand it, it is useful to follow one “drop” of water through the major stages: source collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring.

This explanation uses concrete examples from common municipal systems. For instance, a city may draw river water upstream of an urban area, treat it at a conventional surface-water treatment plant, store it in covered reservoirs, pump it into a distribution network, and monitor chlorine residual and bacterial quality throughout the pipes. A coastal city may instead use desalination. A groundwater-dependent city may pump from wells and apply simpler treatment. In all cases, the same basic principles apply.

## 1. Source water collection

The first step is finding and collecting raw water. Cities typically use either surface water, groundwater, or alternative sources such as desalinated seawater or recycled water.

### Surface water: rivers, lakes, and reservoirs

Many large cities rely on rivers, lakes, or reservoirs. A river intake is usually built on the riverbank or in the river itself. It includes screens to keep out large debris such as branches, leaves, fish, and trash. Water is then pumped or directed to a treatment plant.

For example, a city drawing water from a river might have an intake structure with bar screens and traveling screens. The screens are mechanical racks that rotate through the water and remove debris. The raw water then flows through a intake channel to pumping stations. Pumps lift the water to the treatment plant, where it enters the first treatment stages.

Reservoirs are often used as both a source and a storage buffer. A city reservoir may collect water from a watershed—the area of land where rainfall drains into streams and rivers. For example, a reservoir serving a metropolitan region may be fed by several streams flowing from forested hills. The water stored in the reservoir is drawn through an intake tower at a selected depth. Operators can choose water from different depths because temperature, turbidity, and quality can vary by season. In summer, warmer surface water may contain more algae, while deeper water may be cooler and clearer.
