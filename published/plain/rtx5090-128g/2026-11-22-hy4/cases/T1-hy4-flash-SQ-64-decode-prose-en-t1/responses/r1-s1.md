A city water supply is a engineered system that moves water from a natural source to household taps through a sequence of collection, treatment, storage, distribution, pressure management, and quality monitoring steps. A useful way to understand it is to follow a single drop of water through the system.

Imagine a mid-sized city, “Riverton,” with about 300,000 residents. Riverton draws water from three sources: a river, a reservoir, and underground wells. On a normal summer day, the city uses roughly 45 million liters of water. Some of that water goes to homes for drinking, cooking, bathing, and toilet flushing; some goes to businesses, hospitals, schools, restaurants, laundries, and light industry; and some is used for irrigation of parks and street cleaning. The water utility must deliver all of this reliably, safely, and at the right pressure.

## 1. Collection: getting water from the source

The first step is collection. A city may collect water from surface water sources, such as rivers, lakes, and reservoirs, or from groundwater sources, such as wells and aquifers. Many cities use a combination of both, because relying on one source can be risky during droughts, floods, or contamination events.

In Riverton, the river supplies about 50% of the city’s water. The river intake is located upstream of most industrial discharges and urban storm drains. Large screens at the intake prevent fish, leaves, branches, and other debris from entering the treatment plant. The intake pumps then draw water into a raw-water canal.

The reservoir supplies about 35%. It is a large lake filled by rainfall and snowmelt in the surrounding hills. Water is released from the reservoir through a controlled outlet into a pipe leading to the treatment plant. Reservoir water is usually more consistent in quality than river water, but it can develop algae blooms in warm weather, which creates treatment challenges.

The remaining 15% comes from groundwater wells. Wells pump water from underground aquifers. Groundwater is often clearer and cooler than surface water, but it may contain minerals such as iron, manganese, calcium, or arsenic, depending on the local geology. In Riverton, the wells have moderate hardness and some iron, so the treatment plant must remove or manage these substances.
