A city water supply is one of the most complex yet essential public systems. It is designed to collect water from natural or engineered sources, treat it to safe drinking-water standards, store it for reliability, distribute it through a vast network of pipes, maintain adequate pressure, and continuously monitor quality from source to tap. Although the details vary by city, the basic journey is similar: water moves from a source such as a river, lake, reservoir, aquifer, or desalination plant into a treatment facility; it is cleaned and disinfected; it is stored in tanks or reservoirs; it is pushed through transmission mains and local distribution pipes; and finally it enters homes, businesses, schools, and hospitals through service connections.

A useful way to understand the system is to follow a single drop of water. Imagine a city of two million people located beside a large river, with groundwater wells as a backup source. Rainfall in the surrounding watershed feeds the river, which flows into a reservoir upstream of the city. From there, water is withdrawn through intake structures, treated at a plant, stored, pressurized, distributed through neighborhoods, and monitored at many points along the way.

## 1. Water collection and source protection

The first step is **collection**, also called water supply sourcing or abstraction. Cities typically use one or more sources:

- **Surface water**: rivers, lakes, and reservoirs.
- **Groundwater**: aquifers accessed through wells.
- **Imported water**: water brought from another region through canals, aqueducts, or pipelines.
- **Desalinated water**: seawater or brackish water treated to remove salt, common in arid coastal cities.
- **Recycled water**: treated wastewater used for non-potable purposes such as irrigation or industrial cooling, and in some places indirectly or directly reused as drinking water after advanced treatment.

Surface water is often collected through an **intake structure**. This may include screens that block leaves, branches, fish, and debris. For example, a river intake may have coarse bar screens followed by finer traveling screens. The water is then pumped or conveyed by gravity to a treatment plant. If the source is a reservoir, water may be drawn from different depths using a multi-level intake. This matters because water quality can vary by depth: deeper water may be colder and clearer but can contain higher concentrations of certain minerals, while shallow water may be warmer and more affected by algae.
